The Last Tailor of Saint-Germain

Words by Hélène Marceau12 min readPhotographs by Jun Ota

Monsieur Arnaud Delacroix has measured four presidents, two popes, and one very anxious rock star, and he will tell you — without being asked, and at length — that each of them stood wrong. “They lean,” he says, pinning a sleeve with the precision of a bomb-disposal expert. “Power makes men lean. A good jacket must forgive the lean without encouraging it.”

His workshop occupies the second floor of a building so narrow it seems to have been folded into the street. There are three windows, two apprentices, one cat named Gabardine, and roughly eleven thousand metres of cloth, stacked to the ceiling in a colour order that only Delacroix understands.

A suit here takes ninety hours, across three fittings, spread over as many months. Clients are not permitted to be in a hurry. “If you are in a hurry,” he says, “buy a sweater.”

A good jacket must forgive the lean without encouraging it.

The apprentices, both in their twenties, have been sewing buttonholes for eighteen months. They will sew buttonholes for another year before they are allowed to touch a lapel. Neither seems to mind. One of them, Inès, describes the work as “meditation with needles, and occasional bleeding.”

Delacroix is seventy-eight. When asked who will take over the workshop, he looks out of the middle window for a long time, then back at the sleeve in his hands. “The cloth will decide,” he says eventually. It is, one realises, the only answer he has ever given to any question.

We leave as the light goes gold on the Rue de Sèvres. Behind us, through the window, the old man is still working — leaning, very slightly, over the table.
