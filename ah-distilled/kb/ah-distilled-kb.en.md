# $100M Offers — How to make offers so good people feel stupid saying no

**Make an offer so good that people feel stupid saying no, and price stops being the deciding factor.**

### The book in one page {#overview}

Most small businesses sell something the buyer can compare line by line with a competitor. When that happens, the only lever left is price, and a price war has no winner. *$100M Offers* is about escaping that trap by building a **Grand Slam Offer**: a bundle so specific to one market's problems, and so valuable relative to its price, that the buyer can't find anything to compare it with.

The book follows the order in which you should build an offer:

1. **Pick the market first.** A starving crowd with real pain and money to spend makes even an average offer work. A bad market makes a great offer fail.
2. **Charge what it's worth.** Premium prices fund better delivery, attract better clients and create better results, which justifies the price again.
3. **Build value deliberately.** Use the Value Equation, list every problem the buyer will face, turn each into a solution, choose how to deliver it, then trim and stack.
4. **Enhance it.** Scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and a good name raise demand without changing what you deliver.

### How Alex would use this book {#alex}

- When a business struggles, he looks at the offer before the marketing: a weak offer makes every lead more expensive.
- He narrows the target customer until the offer can speak directly to their situation, then raises the price.
- He assumes the buyer's problems are a checklist of things to solve, not objections to argue with.
- He reaches for guarantees and bonuses to remove risk and effort, and only then asks for a higher price.

## Grand Slam Offer

- id: grand-slam-offer
- book: $100M Offers / The commodity trap
- original term: Grand Slam Offer
- key: Commodity → Differentiated → Grand Slam (category of one)
- related: value-equation, starving-crowd, problems-solutions, trim-and-stack, charge-what-its-worth

An offer so valuable for its price, and so specific to one market, that the buyer has nothing to compare it with. It is the way out of competing on price.

#### The gist

A **commodity** is anything the buyer can compare line by line with what someone else sells. Same hours, same features, same deliverables. When the buyer can compare everything, the only thing left to decide on is price, and the cheapest seller wins a race nobody profits from.

A **Grand Slam Offer** is built so that comparison stops working. It solves every problem one specific market has on the way to its dream outcome, it is worth far more than it costs, and it is packaged in a way nobody else packages it. The buyer can't find a second option, so you become a **category of one**. The test Alex uses is how the buyer feels: the offer should be so good that saying no feels foolish.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Commodity** (compared on price) → **Differentiated** (compared on features) → **Grand Slam** (no comparison, priced on value)
</div>

#### How it works

##### The commodity trap

Most businesses describe what they do in the same words as their competitors: "Qlik development at €70 an hour", "personal training sessions", "bookkeeping services". The buyer collects three quotes, lines them up and picks the lowest. Margins shrink, there is no money left to deliver well, results get worse, and the business has to cut price again. The problem isn't the market or the sales team. The problem is that the offer invites comparison.

##### The ladder

| Level | What the buyer sees | How they decide | Your pricing power |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commodity | The same thing as everyone else | Price | None |
| Differentiated | A better version with some extras | Features and price | Small premium |
| Grand Slam | A solution to *their* situation that nobody else offers | Value against price | Price set by value |

Differentiation alone is not enough. A "better" offer can still be put in the same spreadsheet as the others. The jump to Grand Slam happens when the offer is so specific to one market and so complete that the spreadsheet no longer has a matching row.

##### How a Grand Slam Offer is built

The book walks through the build in a fixed order. Each step has its own entry.

1. **Pick the market.** Sell to a [starving crowd](starving-crowd.html) and [commit to a niche](niche.html). An offer is only "unmatched" relative to a specific buyer.
2. **Set the price from value.** Decide early that you will [charge what it's worth](charge-what-its-worth.html), so you have the margin to deliver an outstanding result.
3. **Define the dream outcome and score it with the [Value Equation](value-equation.html).** Raise the outcome and the perceived likelihood, cut the time delay and the effort.
4. **List every problem and turn it into a solution.** Walk the buyer's journey before, during and after the purchase and write down everything that could stop them. Each problem becomes a "how to get X without Y" solution ([Problems into Solutions](problems-solutions.html)).
5. **Choose delivery vehicles.** For each solution, decide how it reaches the buyer: one-on-one or group, done-for-you or do-it-yourself, call, recording, template, software ([Delivery Vehicles](delivery-vehicles.html)).
6. **Trim and stack.** Cut what is costly and low-value, keep what is cheap and highly valuable, and bundle the rest into one package where every piece is named ([Trim & Stack](trim-and-stack.html)).
7. **Enhance.** Add [scarcity](scarcity.html), [urgency](urgency.html), [bonuses](bonuses.html), [guarantees](guarantees.html) and a strong [name](naming.html). These raise demand without changing the core delivery.

##### Why it works

The bundle solves problems the competitors never listed, so they have nothing to match it with. Because each piece removes a reason to hesitate, the buyer's perceived risk drops. And because many of the pieces are cheap to deliver (templates, recordings, checklists, reusable software), the value goes up much faster than your cost. That gap between value and cost is what lets you charge a premium and still deliver more than the buyer expected.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Manufacturing analytics (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Qlik app development at an hourly rate. Estimate: 120 hours." The buyer collects three quotes and picks the lowest rate.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "OEE and scrap under control in 30 days": connection to MES and ERP, master-data clean-up, three ready-made apps for production, a 30-minute weekly review with shift leads, a board-ready checklist and one month of support after launch.
</div>
</div>

Each piece answers an obstacle the buyer has: "our data is messy" (clean-up), "people won't use it" (weekly review), "the board won't trust the numbers" (checklist), "we'll be on our own after handover" (support). The ready-made apps cost little to reuse. With no hourly rate, there is nothing to put next to another quote.

##### Hair salon (local business)

| Obstacle | Solution in the package |
|---|---|
| "I don't know which style suits me" | Trial style six weeks before, with photos |
| "The morning will be stressful" | Home visit and an exact timetable for the bride and bridesmaids |
| "What if it rains?" | Touch-up kit and instructions for the maid of honour |
| "My hair won't last until midnight" | Hair treatment programme in the four weeks before |

Instead of "hairstyle €60", the salon sells "a stress-free wedding morning and hair that lasts all night". The salon down the street doesn't have this package, so comparing on the price of one hairstyle no longer makes sense.

##### AI invoice automation (consulting)

Dream outcome: accounting closes the month two days earlier. Obstacles: fear of AI errors, an auditor who won't trust it, no time for onboarding. Solutions: two weeks of parallel running with a variance report, auditor documentation as a ready template, five 15-minute recordings instead of a workshop, chat support for the first month. Cut: long on-site training, which costs a lot and the client doesn't need.

#### Common mistakes

- **Calling a better commodity a Grand Slam.** Lower price, faster delivery or one extra feature still leaves you in the same comparison. If the buyer can line you up with a competitor, you are not there yet.
- **Building for everyone.** An offer can only be unmatched for a specific buyer. Without a narrow market the problem list stays generic and so does the offer.
- **Stacking filler.** Bonuses that solve no real problem make the package look padded. Every piece has to remove an obstacle or move a lever in the Value Equation.
- **Skipping the trim.** Promising one-on-one attention for everything makes the offer impossible to deliver profitably. Each piece has to survive the cost test.
- **Describing it in commodity words.** If the name and structure still say "hours", "sessions" or "licences", buyers will compare on those units.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He diagnoses commoditisation first.** When a business complains about price pressure, he assumes the offer is comparable and fixes that before touching ads or sales scripts.
- **He builds from the buyer's problems, not from the product.** His starting point is a long list of everything that could go wrong for the buyer, and the offer is the answer to that list.
- **He changes the unit of sale.** He moves away from hours, sessions or seats toward an outcome with a time frame, so the price can no longer be divided and compared.
- **He favours cheap, high-value pieces.** Templates, recordings, checklists and software raise value with little extra delivery cost, so margin grows as the offer improves.
- **He raises the price once the value is clearly higher.** The point of the work is to charge more than competitors and still have the buyer feel they got the better deal.
- **He keeps the core simple and enhances around it.** Scarcity, urgency, bonuses and guarantees come last, on top of a solid core.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who exactly is this for? What could the buyer compare you with today? What are all the things that could stop them getting the result? Which of those does nobody else solve? What would it take for them to feel stupid saying no?

#### Worksheet

Fill this in writing for one offer. Keep the order.

1. **Comparison check:** Write down three offers a buyer could put next to yours. On what units would they compare you (hours, price per user, sessions)?
2. **Ladder:** Is your offer today a commodity, differentiated or a Grand Slam? Give one sentence of evidence.
3. **Problems:** List at least 15 obstacles your buyer faces before, during and after buying.
4. **Unique solutions:** Which of those obstacles does no competitor address? How would you solve each one?
5. **New unit:** Rewrite the offer as an outcome with a time frame, without hours or seats.
6. **Final test:** Who could the buyer still compare you with directly? If anyone, what is missing?

## Starving Crowd

- id: starving-crowd
- book: $100M Offers / Pricing
- original term: Starving Crowd
- key: Market > Offer > Persuasion
- related: niche, grand-slam-offer, charge-what-its-worth, value-equation, core-four

Pick a market that is already hungry for what you sell. The right market makes an average offer work, and the wrong one makes a great offer fail.

#### The gist

Before you improve an offer, check who you are selling it to. If you sell food to a crowd that is starving, you don't need to be a brilliant salesperson or have the best recipe. If you sell to people who aren't hungry or can't pay, no copy and no script will save you.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Market** > **Offer** > **Persuasion**
</div>

This is the order of importance. Most people invest the other way round: they polish sales scripts and ads first, tweak the offer second, and almost never question the market. Alex starts at the top. A good market forgives a mediocre offer and mediocre selling. A bad market punishes even excellent work.

#### How it works

##### The four criteria

A starving crowd meets four conditions. Score candidate markets on each one before you commit.

| Criterion | Question | Weak sign | Strong sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Massive pain | Must they solve this? | "It would be nice to have" | They already pay for bad workarounds |
| Purchasing power | Can they pay, and can the person decide? | Enthusiastic but broke | Budget plus an owner who signs |
| Easy to target | Can you find them by name? | "All SMEs" | A list from an association or registry |
| Growing | Is demand rising? | Shrinking industry | New regulation, technology or habits pushing them |

##### 1. Massive pain

The buyer has to *need* the problem solved, not just like the idea. Pain is a cost they are already paying: lost orders, overtime, fines, embarrassment in front of the board. The best test is whether they already try to solve it themselves, badly and expensively, with spreadsheets, extra staff or contractors. If they do, the pain is real. Pain also drives price: the bigger the problem, the more a solution is worth.

##### 2. Purchasing power

A market can be in great pain and have no money. People who want to start a business but have no income are a classic case: hungry, but unable to pay much. Purchasing power has two parts. They need the money, and the person you talk to must be able to decide. In B2B, a manager who feels the pain but has no budget authority is not yet a buyer.

##### 3. Easy to target

You have to know where they gather and how to reach them: associations, trade fairs, online groups, industry registries, newsletters they read. Easy to target means you can build a list of real names, rather than hoping they find you. "Small businesses" is not targetable. "Plastics manufacturers in Slovenia with 50 or more employees" is.

##### 4. Growing

In a growing market the number of buyers and their willingness to pay rise without any extra skill on your part. In a shrinking one, you fight harder every year for a smaller slice. A growing market is like swimming with the current. Look for forces outside your control that push demand up: new regulation, new technology, demographic change.

##### The three core markets and drilling down

Almost everything people pay for falls under one of three core markets: **health**, **wealth** and **relationships**. These needs never go away, which makes them a safe starting point. The money is not at that level, though. You drill down until you reach a specific group with a specific problem:

1. Wealth
2. B2B services
3. Business analytics
4. Analytics for manufacturers
5. OEE and scrap reporting for mid-sized manufacturers

Each step narrows the market, makes the pain more specific and makes the buyer easier to find. You stop at the level where you can name the buyer, describe their problem in their words and build a list of the first hundred prospects. The commitment to that level is covered in [Commit to a Niche](niche.html).

##### When nothing works

If leads don't convert and the offer seems fine, go back up the hierarchy. Check the market first: are they in pain, can they pay, can you reach them, is demand growing? Fix the offer only after that, and the sales process last.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### AI consulting: comparing three markets (B2B)

Scores from 1 to 5.

| Market | Pain | Money | Reach | Growth | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| "Companies that want AI" | 3 | 3 | 1 | 5 | 12 |
| Micro businesses and sole traders | 3 | 1 | 3 | 4 | 11 |
| Distributors with 30 to 200 staff entering orders by hand | 5 | 4 | 4 | 4 | 17 |

The first market is growing but can't be found by name and its pain is vague. The second is reachable but can't pay. The third is narrower, yet it has sharp pain (order errors and delays), a budget and clear places to find it (chamber of commerce, trade fairs, business registers). A consultant who commits to it can charge more because they speak exactly this buyer's language.

##### Dentist (local business)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A private practice advertises "general dentistry for the whole family" and competes on price with publicly funded clinics.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Implants for people over 50 who hide their smile." Massive pain (shame, trouble chewing), purchasing power (savings), easy to target (local media, retiree associations), and a market that grows as the population ages.
</div>
</div>

The practice still treats everyone, but builds its messaging, proof and offers around one group. Comparison with general clinics falls away, and revenue per patient rises.

##### Reporting SaaS

A generic reporting tool at €30 per user is compared with Power BI. The same platform sold as "monthly reporting for gym franchise owners", with sources pre-connected, industry KPIs and benchmarks across locations, sells at €300 per location. The market is smaller but hungrier, and it has no direct comparison.

#### Common mistakes

- **Fixing persuasion before the market.** Rewriting ads and scripts for a market that isn't hungry wastes time. Check the market first.
- **Confusing enthusiasm with purchasing power.** The warmest feedback often comes from people who can't pay. Interest is not budget.
- **Picking a market you can't find.** If you can't list 100 specific companies or people, the market isn't defined well enough to target.
- **Staying at the top level.** "Health" or "business owners" is too broad to have a specific pain you can speak to.
- **Fighting a shrinking market.** Skill can't outrun a market where buyers are disappearing every year.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He checks the market before the offer.** When someone asks why their sales are slow, his first suspicion is the market, not the copy.
- **He wants all four criteria, and he weighs pain and money most.** Rule of thumb: a market that scores badly on pain or purchasing power is rarely worth saving with a better offer.
- **He drills down from health, wealth or relationships** until he can name the buyer and their exact problem.
- **He prefers buyers already paying for a bad solution.** Existing spending proves both pain and money.
- **He prefers business buyers when he wants high prices.** Companies usually have more money and a clearer return on a solved problem than individuals.
- **He picks one market and commits** rather than spreading the same offer across several.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who exactly is this for? What does the problem cost them today? How are they solving it now? Who signs the cheque? Where can you find the first hundred of them? Why will this market be bigger in three years?

#### Worksheet

Write down two or three candidate markets and score each from 1 to 5 on the four criteria.

1. **Pain:** What does the unsolved problem cost this market today? How are they solving it themselves now?
2. **Money:** Who approves the purchase, and what budget do they have for this kind of problem?
3. **Reach:** Where can you find the first 100 names? Name three concrete sources.
4. **Growth:** Why will this market be bigger, or need your solution more, in three years?
5. **Drill down:** Write the path from health, wealth or relationships down to your market in four or five steps. Where do you stop, and why?

## Commit to a Niche

- id: niche
- book: $100M Offers / Pricing
- original term: Commit to a Niche
- key: Narrow the market, keep the market, iterate the offer.
- related: starving-crowd, grand-slam-offer, charge-what-its-worth, value-equation

Narrow your market until the offer sounds made for one specific buyer, then stay there. A narrower offer is more relevant, more believable and can charge more.

#### The gist

A narrower market looks like fewer customers. In practice it is the opposite of a loss. "Accounting services" is a commodity that competes with every bookkeeper in town. "Accounting for dental practices" speaks to one owner's exact situation, so it sounds more relevant, feels more likely to work and supports a higher price. The product inside can be almost the same. The positioning changes what it is worth.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Narrower market** → more relevant offer → higher perceived likelihood → **higher price**
</div>

The second half of the idea is **commitment**. Picking a niche is easy. Staying with it long enough to learn its language, collect proof and become known is what makes it pay. When results are slow, Alex's default is to keep the market and fix the offer, not to jump to a new market and start from zero.

#### How it works

##### Why narrowing raises value

A specific offer moves several levers of the [Value Equation](value-equation.html) at once:

- **Perceived likelihood rises.** A buyer believes something built for people like them will work for them. Case studies from the same industry are far more convincing than general ones.
- **Effort drops.** You already know their systems, their regulations and their typical problems, so they explain less and you deliver faster.
- **Comparison disappears.** A generalist is compared with every other generalist. A specialist in one niche has few direct competitors, which is the start of a [Grand Slam Offer](grand-slam-offer.html).

##### How to narrow

Start from a broad [starving crowd](starving-crowd.html) and add one qualifier at a time. Typical qualifiers:

| Qualifier | Example |
|---|---|
| Industry | Manufacturers, dental practices, gyms |
| Size | 50 to 200 employees, single location, franchise owners |
| Role | CFO, production manager, practice owner |
| Situation | Moving to a new ERP, opening a second location, preparing for an audit |
| Problem | Month-end close takes too long, scrap is too high |

Each step makes the message sharper and the list of prospects shorter but easier to find. Rule of thumb: you have gone far enough when you can name the buyer, describe their problem in their own words, and list a hundred real prospects.

##### Pick one and commit

Many people treat a niche like an experiment they abandon after a few weeks of weak results. Each switch resets the proof, the reputation and the market knowledge you built. Alex's rule of thumb is to stay with the market and treat poor results as a signal about the offer: change the promise, the price, the guarantee, the bonuses or the delivery before you change who you sell to. A market is worth leaving only if it fails the basic tests of pain, money, reach or growth.

##### What commitment buys you

- **Language.** You learn the words buyers use for their problems, which improves every ad and sales call.
- **Proof.** Every client becomes a case study that is directly relevant to the next one.
- **Reusable assets.** Templates, checklists and ready-made solutions built for one client fit the next client in the same niche, so delivery gets cheaper as value goes up.
- **Reputation.** Niches are small worlds. People talk, and referrals come faster.

##### You can widen later

A niche is a starting point, not a prison. Once you dominate one segment, you can expand to an adjacent one with similar problems, carrying your proof and assets with you. Narrowing first and widening later is far easier than starting broad and trying to stand out.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik consulting (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Qlik development and consulting for all industries." Rate €70 per hour. Every project starts from scratch, and each prospect compares three quotes.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Production analytics for mid-sized plastics and metal manufacturers": a fixed package with ready-made OEE, scrap and delivery apps that connect to the ERP systems common in that industry. Fixed price €18,000.
</div>
</div>

The same skills are now aimed at one buyer. Because most of the apps are reused, a project that used to take 200 hours takes 90. At €70 per hour, 200 hours would have earned €14,000. The niche package earns €18,000 for 90 hours, which is €200 per hour.

##### Bookkeeping for dental practices (local service)

A bookkeeper switches from "accounting for small businesses" to "accounting for dental practices". She learns the practice software, the typical cost structure and the questions owners ask at year-end. She builds a monthly report showing revenue per chair and per dentist. Owners refer her to each other at professional events. Her fee rises from €250 to €400 per month, and she spends less time per client because every month looks familiar.

##### Hopping versus iterating

| Month | Market hopper | Committed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Gyms, no sales | Gyms, no sales |
| 2 | Switches to dentists | Adds a guarantee |
| 3 | Switches to restaurants | Changes the price and the name |
| 4 | Starting over again | First three clients and a case study |

#### Common mistakes

- **Fear of missing out on other customers.** Narrowing your message does not mean refusing other work. It means your marketing speaks to one buyer.
- **Hopping markets after a few weeks.** Each switch resets learning and proof. Change the offer first.
- **A niche defined by you, not by the buyer.** "Companies that value data" is not a group anyone identifies with. Use industry, role or situation.
- **Narrowing into a market with no money.** A niche still has to pass the [starving crowd](starving-crowd.html) tests.
- **Keeping generic delivery.** If the niche is only in the headline and the delivery is unchanged, the buyer notices.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He narrows until the offer feels personal.** The test is whether the buyer reads it and thinks it was made for them.
- **He raises the price after narrowing.** A specific offer is worth more, so he expects to charge more for the same core work.
- **He keeps the market and changes the offer.** Weak results are a reason to rework the promise, price, guarantee or bonuses, not to start over in a new market.
- **He uses the niche to build reusable assets** so that delivery cost falls with every new client.
- **He widens only after winning.** Expansion goes to adjacent markets once the first one is clearly working.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who is the one buyer this is for? What do you know about them that a generalist doesn't? How long have you actually stayed with this market? What have you changed in the offer before blaming the market?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing for your current business.

1. **Current market:** How do you describe your target customer today? Could a competitor use the same sentence?
2. **Narrowing:** Add industry, size, role and situation. Write the narrowest version you could still build a list of 100 prospects for.
3. **Advantage:** What do you already know about this niche (systems, regulations, problems) that a generalist doesn't?
4. **Commitment:** For how long will you stay with this market? Write the date.
5. **Iteration plan:** List three offer changes you will test before you consider changing the market.
6. **Next market:** Which adjacent niche could you widen into later with the same proof and assets?

## Charge What It's Worth

- id: charge-what-its-worth
- book: $100M Offers / Pricing
- original term: Charge What It's Worth (Virtuous Cycle of Price)
- key: Higher price → more margin and more committed buyers → better results → proof that justifies the price
- related: grand-slam-offer, value-equation, starving-crowd, niche, guarantees

Don't compete on price. A premium price funds better delivery, attracts more committed clients and produces better results, which justifies the price again.

#### The gist

Being the cheapest is a weak position. There is always someone willing to go lower, and a low price leaves no money to do the job well. Alex argues the opposite: charge a premium, and use the margin to deliver a result so good that the price feels small next to it.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Higher price** → more margin and more committed buyers → **better results** → proof and referrals → **justifies a higher price**
</div>

This is the **virtuous cycle of price**. The same loop runs the other way at a low price: thin margins, rushed delivery, clients who care less, weak results, no proof and more pressure to cut. The condition is that value stays well above the price. A high price without value is not premium, it is just expensive.

#### How it works

##### The vicious cycle of low price

1. You price low to win the deal.
2. Margin is thin, so you cut delivery: less attention, cheaper people, fewer extras.
3. Clients who paid little invest little attention and effort. Results suffer.
4. Weak results bring complaints, churn and no case studies.
5. Without proof, the only way to win the next deal is another low price.

Low prices also attract the buyers who shop hardest on price. They tend to demand the most and leave first when someone cheaper appears.

##### The virtuous cycle of high price

1. You price well above competitors and deliver value well above the price.
2. The margin funds better delivery: more attention, better hires, better tools, more follow-up.
3. The buyer who paid more is emotionally invested. They show up, do the work and take the advice seriously, so they get better results.
4. Margin also funds more marketing. You can outspend competitors to acquire each client.
5. Better results become proof and referrals, which raise perceived likelihood and let you charge more again.

##### Price is part of the value

Price is a signal. Buyers often read a higher price as higher quality, and they value what cost them something. In the [Value Equation](value-equation.html), a committed buyer is more likely to achieve the result, so a higher price can raise the actual and perceived likelihood of success. Price from the value of the outcome to the buyer, not from your hours or costs.

##### Earning the premium

A premium price needs a reason. The usual sources:

- **A narrow market** where your offer is clearly made for them ([Commit to a Niche](niche.html)).
- **An offer with no direct comparison** ([Grand Slam Offer](grand-slam-offer.html)).
- **Risk reversal** so the buyer feels safe paying more ([Guarantees](guarantees.html)).
- **A market that can pay** ([Starving Crowd](starving-crowd.html)).

##### Test raising prices

You won't know your ceiling until you test it. Raise the price for new prospects, keep everything else the same and watch close rate and profit, not just close rate. Losing some deals at a higher price is normal and often profitable. Rule of thumb: if almost everyone says yes, you are probably priced too low. Some resistance means you are near the edge of value.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### AI automation retainer (B2B consulting)

| | Low price | Premium price |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly fee | €1,000 | €1,500 |
| Delivery cost per client | €600 | €600 |
| Margin per client | €400 | €900 |
| Clients (25% fewer at premium) | 20 | 15 |
| Monthly profit | €8,000 | €13,500 |

At the higher price the consultant has fewer clients and 69% more profit (€5,500 on top of €8,000). Fewer clients also means more time for each one, so results improve. Part of the extra margin could pay for a junior hire to speed up delivery, which strengthens the cycle again.

</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** A 12-week transformation programme for €1,200, paid upfront: three sessions a week, a nutrition plan and weekly check-ins. Clients who paid €1,200 rarely miss a session. The trainer needs far fewer clients and uses the time for coaching between sessions.

Thirty-six sessions at €25 would cost the client €900. The programme costs €300 more, but it includes nutrition and check-ins, and the upfront commitment changes behaviour. Better results bring before-and-after proof that sells the next programme.

##### Qlik dashboard project (B2B analytics)

A consultant moves from €70 an hour to a fixed €9,800 package for one outcome. Prospects who wanted the cheapest hours drop out. Those who stay care about the result, give access quickly and use the dashboards, which produces the case studies that support the price.

#### Common mistakes

- **Pricing low out of fear.** A low price feels safer but attracts the least committed buyers and removes the money for good delivery.
- **Raising price without raising value.** If the offer stays a commodity, a higher price only loses deals.
- **Pricing from cost or hours.** Your costs are irrelevant to the buyer. Price from what the outcome is worth to them.
- **Keeping the margin instead of reinvesting it.** The cycle only works if part of the extra margin goes back into delivery and acquisition.
- **Never testing.** Many businesses have never tried a higher price, so they don't know where their ceiling is.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He refuses to compete on being cheapest.** He sees no lasting advantage in a race to the bottom.
- **He raises value first, then raises the price.** Niche, Grand Slam Offer and guarantees come before the higher number.
- **He reinvests the margin** in better delivery and more marketing spend per client, which a cheaper competitor cannot match.
- **He wants buyers who are invested.** He treats a higher price as a filter that improves client behaviour and results.
- **He tests price increases on new prospects** and judges them by total profit, not by close rate alone.
- **He changes the unit of sale** from hours or sessions to an outcome, so the price is compared with the value, not with other vendors.
- **Questions he would ask:** What is the result worth to the buyer in money? Who is the most expensive competitor, and why are you below them? What is your close rate? If almost everyone says yes, why haven't you raised the price? What would you do for clients with twice the margin?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing with real numbers from your business.

1. **Current position:** Where is your price compared with competitors? Why?
2. **Value in money:** What is the outcome worth to a typical buyer per year? How does your price compare?
3. **Cycle check:** Which signs of the vicious cycle do you see today (thin margin, low-commitment clients, no case studies)?
4. **Reinvestment:** If your price went up 50%, what exactly would you improve in delivery or marketing?
5. **Test:** Choose a higher price for the next ten prospects. Write down the price, the date and the close rate and profit you will compare.

## The Value Equation

- id: value-equation
- book: $100M Offers / Value
- original term: Value Equation
- key: Value = (Dream Outcome × Perceived Likelihood) ÷ (Time Delay × Effort & Sacrifice)
- related: grand-slam-offer, problems-solutions, guarantees, charge-what-its-worth

How a buyer decides, in their head, whether something is worth its price. Push two levers up and two down.

#### The gist

People don't buy a product, they buy a **change**. An offer is worth as much as that change is big and believable, divided by how long it takes and how much pain it costs to get there.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Value** = (**Dream Outcome** × **Perceived Likelihood of Achievement**) ÷ (**Time Delay** × **Effort & Sacrifice**)
</div>

Most sellers only compete on the top line: a bigger promise. The bigger opportunity is usually the bottom line. If the time to a result and the work for the buyer get close to zero, value grows very large, and so does what you can charge.

#### How it works

##### Dream Outcome ↑

The result the buyer actually wants, in their words and emotions, not your features. It is often about status: how others will see them afterwards. "A dashboard" is not a dream outcome. "You answer every board question in ten seconds" is.

##### Perceived Likelihood of Achievement ↑

How strongly the buyer *believes* it will work for them. The key word is perceived: it is not enough that it works, they have to believe it. Proof (client results, numbers), guarantees, specificity ("for manufacturers with 50 to 200 employees") and a clearly described process all raise it.

##### Time Delay ↓

The time from paying to the **first felt result**, not the final one. People pay far more for speed. You often can't speed up the end result, but you can design a quick win in the first days that proves the decision was right.

##### Effort & Sacrifice ↓

Everything the buyer has to do or give up: learning, their time, new habits, and the emotional cost of trying and failing. This is why done-for-you costs more than do-it-yourself even when the knowledge is identical.

##### Why the bottom line matters most

The two top levers have a ceiling: a promise can only grow so big before it stops being believable. The two bottom levers can be pushed toward zero. Removing work and waiting is often cheaper for you to deliver than a bigger result, and buyers value it more.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### BI dashboard implementation (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "We'll build your Qlik dashboards. The project takes three months. We need system access and your people in workshops."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "In ten days you'll have your margin-by-customer report, the one you now build by hand in Excel. Your team spends four hours in total. If it isn't in use within ten days, you don't pay for phase one."
</div>
</div>

The dream outcome is concrete (a report they already know), time delay drops from three months to ten days, effort drops from workshops to four hours, and a conditional guarantee raises likelihood.

##### Personal trainer (local service)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Monthly membership with gym access and a workout plan."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Down 6 kg by summer. Weekly shopping list and recipes, three 30-minute workouts, a weekly weigh-in with your coach. First change on the scale within 14 days."
</div>
</div>

Recipes and the list cut effort, 30 minutes cuts time sacrifice, weigh-ins raise likelihood, and the 14-day marker shortens the perceived delay.

##### AI process automation (consulting)

| Lever | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Outcome | "We implement AI" | "Customer quotes in one hour instead of two days" |
| Likelihood | Generic promises | A demo on their own data in the first meeting |
| Time delay | Six-month programme | First process live in three weeks |
| Effort | Staff training | Works inside the tools they already use |

#### Common mistakes

- **Inflating the promise.** A bigger outcome without proof lowers likelihood. A promise the buyer doesn't believe is worth nothing.
- **Selling the process instead of the result.** Hours, modules and features are costs to the buyer. More steps usually means more effort.
- **Ignoring the denominator.** Everyone competes on outcome. A faster start and less work for the client is often cheaper for you and worth more to them.
- **Confusing actual and perceived likelihood.** Great results nobody knows about don't raise the value of your offer. Proof has to be shown.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He starts from the bottom of the fraction.** His first question is rarely "how do we promise more?" but "how do we make this faster and easier for them?"
- **He looks for the fastest felt win** and builds the first week of delivery around it, because early proof lowers refunds and raises referrals.
- **He sells the outcome in the buyer's words** and moves the mechanics (hours, deliverables, process) to the back.
- **He raises likelihood with proof before he raises the promise:** case studies, specific numbers, a narrower target customer, and a guarantee.
- **He prices from value, not cost.** When value goes up on all four levers, he expects the price to go up too.
- **Questions he would ask:** What does the client really want, and how will others see them when they get it? What would make a sceptic believe it? What can they get in week one? What do they have to do that you could do for them?

#### Worksheet

Take your main offer and answer in writing. Score each lever 1 to 10 as it stands today.

1. **Outcome:** How would the client describe what they want, in their own words? How will others see them afterwards?
2. **Likelihood:** Which three proofs would most help a sceptic believe? Which do you already have?
3. **Delay:** What is the fastest visible win the client can get in week one?
4. **Effort:** List everything the client has to do. Which of it can you take over or remove?
5. **Rewrite:** Write a one-sentence version of the offer that improves the two weakest levers.

## Problems into Solutions

- id: problems-solutions
- book: $100M Offers / Value
- original term: Problems & Solutions
- key: Every problem the buyer has is a solution you can sell: obstacle → how to get X even if / without Y.
- related: value-equation, delivery-vehicles, trim-and-stack, grand-slam-offer, bonuses

List every obstacle the buyer will hit before, during and after buying, then turn each one into a "how to" solution. The longer the list, the more value you can build.

#### The gist

Once you know the dream outcome, the next question is: what stands between the buyer and that result? Every answer is an obstacle, and every obstacle is a piece of value you can add to the offer. Most sellers solve only the core problem (the dashboard, the workout, the filling) and leave the buyer alone with everything around it.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Dream outcome** → list **every obstacle** (before, during, after) → rewrite each as **"how to get X even if / without Y"**
</div>

The list is the raw material for the whole offer. The obstacles you find become solutions, the solutions get delivery vehicles, and the best of those get stacked. Competitors who never wrote the list can't match what you build from it.

#### How it works

##### Step 1: Pin down the dream outcome

Start from where the buyer wants to end up, in their words. "A Qlik app" is your product. "The CFO closes the month in three days and the board stops questioning the numbers" is the outcome. The more concrete it is, the easier it is to see what gets in the way.

##### Step 2: Walk the buyer's journey in time

Go through the buyer's experience step by step and write down everything that could stop them, slow them down or make them give up.

- **Before buying:** Is this for someone like me? Can we afford it? Will my boss approve it? Have I been burned by something similar?
- **During:** Where do I start? What if I get stuck? Do I have the time? Who answers my questions? Will my team cooperate?
- **After:** How do I keep the result? What happens when the consultant leaves? How do I show others that it worked?

Include the small and embarrassing obstacles. Fear of looking stupid in front of colleagues, not knowing which button to press, or forgetting the next step are real reasons people quit.

##### Step 3: Check each obstacle against the value equation

Every obstacle is a hit to one of the four value drivers. Using them as a checklist makes the list longer and more complete:

| Value driver | Typical obstacle in the buyer's head |
|---|---|
| Dream outcome | "It's not worth the money." "It won't change much." |
| Perceived likelihood | "It won't work for us." "Our situation is different." "I can't do this." |
| Time delay | "It will take forever." "We don't have time for this now." |
| Effort & sacrifice | "It's too complicated." "I'd have to give up X." "My team won't put in the work." |

Two questions deserve special attention because they block most purchases: *does this work at all* and *can I personally do it*. A buyer can believe the first and still say no because of the second.

##### Step 4: Rewrite each obstacle as a solution

Flip each obstacle into a "how to" statement. The simplest forms are "how to get X without Y" and "how to get X even if Y".

- "Our ERP data is a mess" → how to get reliable reports even if your ERP data is messy.
- "I don't have time" → how to get this running with less than four hours of your team's time.
- "The board won't trust it" → how to get numbers the board accepts on the first presentation.

These lines do double duty. They are the components of the offer, and they are ready-made copy for your sales page and proposal, because each one answers a doubt the buyer already has.

##### Step 5: Aim for a long list

The length of the list sets the ceiling on value. Ten obstacles give you ten chances to add something a competitor doesn't have. As a rule of thumb, if you found fewer than fifteen or twenty, you haven't walked the full journey yet. You won't solve them all; the trim-and-stack step decides which ones make it into the offer.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Month-end reporting for a manufacturer (B2B analytics)

Dream outcome: the finance team closes the month in three days and the board trusts the numbers.

| When | Obstacle | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Before | "We tried BI before and nobody used it." | How to get dashboards your team opens every day, even if the last tool failed |
| Before | "IT won't give access." | How to connect to the ERP with read-only access IT approves in one meeting |
| Before | "I can't justify the cost to the owner." | How to show the payback in hours saved before signing |
| During | "Our item and customer codes are a mess." | How to get correct reports even if master data is messy |
| During | "My team has no time for workshops." | How to go live with four hours of your team's time |
| During | "What if the numbers don't match Excel?" | How to reconcile every key figure against your current report in week one |
| During | "Who do I call when something breaks?" | How to get answers within one business day without opening a ticket |
| After | "The board won't believe the new numbers." | How to present the first month so the board accepts it |
| After | "When you leave, it will rot." | How to keep the app correct without hiring a BI developer |
| After | "New questions will need a new project." | How to answer new questions yourself in under an hour |

Ten obstacles became ten components. Each one now needs a delivery vehicle and a cost estimate before the offer is final.

##### Adult clear aligners at a dental clinic (local business)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Invisible aligners from €3,500. Book a consultation."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** The clinic listed what adults worry about: looking odd at work, pain, losing aligners, forgetting to wear them, teeth moving back afterwards, and the price. The offer now answers each one: a simulation of the final smile at the first visit, a monthly payment plan, a spare set included, app reminders to wear them, and retainers plus one check-up a year for the first two years.
</div>
</div>

The core treatment is unchanged. What changed is that every doubt the patient had in the waiting room now has an answer written into the offer.

#### Common mistakes

- **Stopping at the core problem.** Solving only the main task leaves the buyer to handle access, adoption, maintenance and their own doubts alone. That is where most of the value sits.
- **Listing from your point of view.** "We need clean requirements" is your obstacle. Write the list as the buyer would say it, about their fears, time and effort.
- **Skipping the before and after phases.** Most sellers only list delivery problems. Doubts before buying and the fear of being alone afterwards decide many purchases.
- **Arguing with obstacles instead of solving them.** Treating "we don't have time" as an objection to overcome in sales misses the point. It is a problem to design into the offer.
- **A short list.** Five obstacles produce a thin offer that looks like everyone else's.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He treats every objection as a missing piece of the offer.** If buyers keep saying the same thing on sales calls, the offer needs a component that solves it.
- **He walks the timeline literally:** what happens the minute after they pay, the first week, the first time they get stuck, the day after the result. He writes it all down.
- **He mines real language** from sales calls, support tickets and reviews instead of guessing what buyers think.
- **He pays special attention to "I can't do it".** Belief in the method is not enough if the buyer doubts themselves, so he looks for ways to make the buyer's part smaller.
- **He wants a long list first and judgement later.** Filtering happens in trim and stack, never while brainstorming.
- **Questions he would ask:** What does the buyer worry about the night before signing? Where do clients usually get stuck or quit? What do they ask you most often? What happens after you leave? Which of these does a competitor already solve?

#### Worksheet

Take your main offer and work on paper. Aim for at least fifteen obstacles.

1. **Dream outcome:** Write the result in one sentence, in the buyer's words.
2. **Before:** List every doubt or barrier before the buyer pays, including approval, budget and past bad experiences.
3. **During:** List everything that could slow them down or make them give up while working with you.
4. **After:** List what could make the result fade or make them feel alone once you're done.
5. **Value check:** Tag each obstacle with the value driver it hurts. Which driver has the most tags?
6. **Rewrite:** Turn every obstacle into a "how to get X even if / without Y" line and mark the five that matter most to the buyer.

## Delivery Vehicles

- id: delivery-vehicles
- book: $100M Offers / Value
- original term: Delivery Vehicles (Solutions → Delivery)
- key: For each solution: who gets attention (1:1, group, 1:many) × who does the work (DIY, DWY, DFY) × medium × speed and access
- related: problems-solutions, trim-and-stack, value-equation, grand-slam-offer, charge-what-its-worth

The same solution can be delivered in many ways, and each way has a different value to the buyer and a different cost to you. Brainstorm many, then pick on value versus cost.

#### The gist

A solution on your list ("how to answer new questions yourself in under an hour") says nothing about how the buyer will get it. It could be a personal session, a recorded video, a template, a support chat or a piece of software. Each of these is a delivery vehicle, and the choice changes both what the buyer feels they got and what it costs you to provide.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Delivery vehicle** = **attention** (1-on-1 · small group · one-to-many) × **who does the work** (do-it-yourself · done-with-you · done-for-you) × **medium** × **speed & access**
</div>

The goal is to generate many options for each solution and only then judge them. The best offers mix a few expensive, high-touch pieces where they matter most with many cheap, scalable pieces that still carry high value.

#### How it works

##### Attention: how many people share you

- **One-on-one:** the most personal and the most expensive. It scales with your hours.
- **Small group:** a handful of clients at once. Less personal, much cheaper per client, and buyers learn from each other.
- **One-to-many:** one session, video or document for everyone. Close to zero marginal cost.

Buyers usually value personal attention more, but often less than you expect when the group format still gets them the result.

##### Who does the work

- **Do-it-yourself (DIY):** you give the method and tools, the buyer does the work. Lowest price and lowest cost.
- **Done-with-you (DWY):** you work alongside, guide and correct. Middle on both.
- **Done-for-you (DFY):** you do it, the buyer receives the result. Highest value because effort and sacrifice drop toward zero, and highest cost to you.

This axis maps directly onto the effort lever of the value equation. Moving one component from DIY to DFY can be the biggest value jump in the whole offer.

##### Medium

How the solution physically reaches the buyer: in person, live online (call, video), recorded (video, audio), written (guides, checklists, email), templates and tools, or software and automation. Recorded and written formats cost once to create and almost nothing to repeat.

##### Speed and access

When and how quickly the buyer can get help: response within an hour, same business day, weekly; 24/7 or office hours; a direct phone line or a ticket queue. Fast access raises perceived likelihood because the buyer knows they won't stay stuck. It can also be very expensive, so it is usually reserved for the moments where speed matters.

##### How to brainstorm

1. Take one solution from your problems-into-solutions list.
2. Combine the four axes to write at least five ways to deliver it.
3. Stretch the range with two questions: how would I deliver this if the buyer paid ten times more? How if they paid ten times less? The extremes reveal options you would not think of otherwise.
4. For each option, estimate value to the buyer (high, medium, low) and cost to you (high, medium, low). Rough estimates are enough; the next step is trim and stack.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Solution: "How to answer new questions yourself in under an hour" (B2B analytics)

| Delivery option | Attention | Who works | Medium / access | Value to buyer | Cost to you |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Weekly 1:1 call with a Qlik developer | 1:1 | DWY | Live online, weekly | High | High |
| Monthly group clinic for all clients' power users | Small group | DWY | Live online, monthly | Medium-high | Low |
| Library of 10-minute recorded how-tos | One-to-many | DIY | Recorded, 24/7 | Medium | Low (once) |
| Template app with prepared master items | One-to-many | DIY | Template | High | Low (reused) |
| AI assistant on the client's data model | One-to-many | DIY | Software, 24/7 | High | Medium |
| On-call developer who builds any new chart in 24 hours | 1:1 | DFY | Chat, 24-hour response | Very high | Very high |

The template app and the group clinic look strongest: high value at low cost. The 24-hour DFY option is too expensive for the base offer but could become a premium tier. The weekly 1:1 call is a candidate to cut.

##### Solution: "How to keep the weight off after the programme" (personal trainer)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** The trainer's only idea was to offer more personal sessions after the 12 weeks, at the full hourly price. Few clients took it.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** The trainer listed options: a printed maintenance plan (DIY, written), a monthly group check-in (small group, in person), a private chat for weigh-in photos with replies within a day (1:1, DWY, chat), and a quarterly 1:1 session. The plan, the group check-in and the chat cost little and solve the real fear, which is sliding back without anyone noticing.
</div>
</div>

The buyer cares about not regaining the weight. Which vehicle delivers that matters much less to them than to the trainer's calendar.

#### Common mistakes

- **Defaulting to your usual format.** Consultants default to 1:1 meetings, coaches to calls. Writing only one option per solution hides cheaper and better ones.
- **Assuming more personal always means more value.** Buyers pay for the result and for certainty. A template that gets them there faster often beats an hour on a call.
- **Promising speed you can't sustain.** 24/7 or one-hour response is valuable only if you can keep it with real staff or software. A broken promise destroys trust.
- **Ignoring your own capacity.** An offer built entirely from 1:1 and DFY pieces caps how many clients you can serve and squeezes margin as you grow.
- **Judging while brainstorming.** Cutting options before you have written them all down leaves the best combinations unseen.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He writes every combination down first** and only then scores value against cost. The scoring belongs to the trim-and-stack step.
- **He looks for things that cost once and deliver many times:** recordings, templates, checklists, software. These are where margin comes from.
- **He spends expensive, personal delivery where it changes the outcome most,** typically at the start (to get a fast win) and at the points where clients usually get stuck.
- **He uses DFY as a lever for price.** Removing effort is one of the strongest value moves, so the done-for-you pieces often anchor the premium version.
- **He matches access to risk:** fast response where a stuck client would quit, slower channels everywhere else.
- **He uses the ten-times-more and ten-times-less questions** to break out of habitual delivery.
- **Questions he would ask:** Where in the journey does the client most need a human? What could be recorded once and reused? What would make them feel supported without costing you hours? If you had to serve ten times more clients next year, which pieces would break?

#### Worksheet

Use your list from Problems into Solutions.

1. **Pick:** Choose the five solutions that matter most to the buyer.
2. **Brainstorm:** For each one, write at least five delivery options by combining attention, who does the work, medium and access.
3. **Stretch:** Add one option for a buyer paying ten times more and one for a buyer paying ten times less.
4. **Score:** Rate each option on value to buyer and cost to you (high, medium, low).
5. **Capacity:** Mark every option that consumes your personal hours. How many clients could you serve if you kept all of them?

## Trim & Stack

- id: trim-and-stack
- book: $100M Offers / Value
- original term: Trim & Stack
- key: Keep low cost / high value, keep the critical high cost / high value, cut high cost / low value, then stack and show the total.
- related: delivery-vehicles, problems-solutions, grand-slam-offer, value-equation, bonuses

Score every solution and delivery option by value to the buyer and cost to you, cut the costly low-value ones, and stack what remains into one offer whose total value clearly exceeds the price.

#### The gist

After listing problems and brainstorming delivery vehicles, you have far more possible components than any offer should contain. Trim and stack is the editing step. You judge every combination of solution and delivery on two things only: how much the buyer values it and how much it costs you to deliver.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Trim:** cut high cost / low value · keep low cost / high value · keep the few critical high cost / high value pieces

**Stack:** bundle what remains, name and value each piece, show the **total value** next to the **price**
</div>

The result is one offer made of many named components. When the buyer sees each piece with its own value, the sum is much larger than the price, and comparing it with a competitor's single line item stops making sense.

#### How it works

##### The 2×2: value versus cost

Place every candidate component in one of four boxes.

| | **Low cost to you** | **High cost to you** |
|---|---|---|
| **High value to buyer** | **Keep, always.** Templates, recordings, checklists, automations. The core of your margin. | **Keep only if critical.** Done-for-you work, 1:1 time, fast access. Use where it changes the outcome; consider a premium tier. |
| **Low value to buyer** | **Optional.** Keep only if it doesn't clutter the offer. Often better left out. | **Cut.** Costs you a lot and the buyer barely notices it. |

"Value" here is value as the buyer perceives it, measured with the value equation: does the piece make the outcome bigger, more likely, faster or easier? A piece that moves none of those is noise.

##### How to trim

1. Put every solution and delivery option from the previous steps in one list.
2. Score each one on value (1 to 5) and cost (1 to 5). Use real delivery cost: hours, people, tools.
3. Remove everything high cost and low value first. This is often what you do today out of habit (long kick-off workshops, weekly status meetings, custom reports nobody reads).
4. Keep all low cost / high value pieces.
5. From the high cost / high value box, keep only what the outcome really depends on. Move the rest to an upgrade.
6. Check for gaps: does every major obstacle from your list still have at least one solution in the offer?

##### How to stack

1. **Group** the kept pieces into a clear sequence, usually following the buyer's timeline (start, delivery, after).
2. **Name** each piece so it describes the result it gives, not the activity ("Board-ready first month" instead of "presentation support").
3. **Assign a value** to each piece. Base it on what the buyer would reasonably pay for it alone, or what solving that problem is worth to them. Keep it defensible: inflated numbers lower trust.
4. **Show the total** next to the price. The gap between total value and price is what makes saying no feel unreasonable.

##### Why stacking works

A single price for a single thing invites comparison: hourly rate against hourly rate. A stack of named solutions has no direct equivalent on the market. The buyer can't find someone else offering the same ten pieces, so they judge the offer on value instead of price.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Month-end reporting offer (B2B analytics)

Trimming the candidate list:

| Component | Value | Cost | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three ready-made finance apps (reused template) | High | Low | Keep |
| Recorded how-to library | Medium-high | Low | Keep |
| Monthly group clinic | Medium-high | Low | Keep |
| ERP connection and data model | High | High | Keep (critical) |
| Master-data clean-up | High | High | Keep (critical) |
| Weekly 1:1 status call | Low | High | Cut |
| Two-day on-site kick-off workshop | Low | High | Cut |
| Any new chart built within 24 hours | Very high | Very high | Premium tier |

The stack shown to the buyer:

| Component | Value |
|---|---|
| ERP connection and finance data model | €6,000 |
| Master-data clean-up | €3,000 |
| Three ready-made apps: margin, cash, month-end | €7,500 |
| Week-one reconciliation against your Excel | €1,500 |
| Six months of monthly group clinics | €1,800 |
| Library of recorded how-tos | €1,200 |
| Board-ready first month presentation template | €1,000 |
| 90 days of support, next business day response | €2,000 |
| **Total value** | **€24,000** |
| **Price** | **€9,800** |

Cutting the status calls and the on-site workshop removed a large share of delivery hours. The kept pieces solve more obstacles than the old offer, and the buyer sees €24,000 of value for €9,800.

<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Wedding cake, three tiers, from €450." Plus a free one-hour tasting session in the shop for every enquiry, which cost the owner a lot of Saturday time and converted poorly.
</div>

- **Keeping things because you always did them.** Habitual pieces such as weekly status meetings often land in the high cost / low value box. Check them first.
- **Stacking filler.** Pieces that solve no real obstacle make the offer look padded and lower trust.
- **Inflated values.** Claiming that a checklist is worth €5,000 makes the buyer doubt every other number. Values should be ones the buyer would accept.
- **Cutting the critical expensive piece.** If the outcome depends on a done-for-you step, removing it to save cost breaks the result and the guarantee.
- **Stacking without showing.** A long list of components with one lump price hides the value. Each piece needs a name and a number.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He cuts before he adds.** His first pass looks for what costs the most and moves the outcome least, because that improves margin and value at the same time.
- **He hunts for low cost / high value pieces** and multiplies them: templates, recordings, scripts, checklists, software.
- **He protects the pieces the outcome depends on,** even when they are expensive, and raises the price to fund them instead of removing them.
- **He moves expensive extras into a higher tier** rather than giving them to everyone.
- **He presents the stack in the buyer's order of concern** and names each piece after the result it delivers.
- **He wants a clear gap between total value and price,** as a rule of thumb large enough that the price feels small next to it, but every number must be believable.
- **Questions he would ask:** What do you deliver today that clients never mention? Which piece costs you the most hours? Which cheap piece do clients thank you for? If you removed one component, which would they miss? Does every major obstacle still have a solution in the stack?

#### Worksheet

Use your delivery options from the previous step.

1. **Score:** Rate every component on value to buyer and cost to you, 1 to 5.
2. **Cut:** List everything high cost / low value. What would you save by removing it?
3. **Protect:** Which high cost / high value pieces does the outcome truly depend on?
4. **Upgrade:** Which expensive pieces could move to a premium tier?
5. **Stack:** Write the final list with a result-based name and a defensible value for each piece. Add up the total.
6. **Compare:** Put total value next to your price. Is the gap large enough that saying no feels unreasonable?

## Scarcity

- id: scarcity
- book: $100M Offers / Enhancing the offer
- original term: Scarcity
- key: Limit the quantity, not the time: total cap, growth cap (N new per period) or cohort cap. It must be real.
- related: urgency, bonuses, charge-what-its-worth, guarantees, grand-slam-offer

Limiting how many people can get the offer makes it more desirable. In B2B your capacity is already limited, so stating it honestly is both true and persuasive.

#### The gist

People want more of what they can't easily get. When an offer is available to anyone at any time, there is no cost to waiting, so buyers wait. When only a few spots exist, saying "maybe later" carries the risk of losing the chance, and the fear of missing out pushes the decision forward.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Scarcity** = a limit on **quantity** (how many can buy)

**Urgency** = a limit on **time** (until when they can buy)
</div>

Scarcity raises demand without changing what you deliver. It only works over time if it is real: if the "last three spots" are still open next month, buyers learn to ignore you. In a service business this is rarely a problem, because your hours, team and attention are genuinely limited.

#### How it works

##### Why it raises demand

Three effects work together. Limited things look more valuable, because others seem to want them too. A limit creates a cost to delaying, which breaks the default of "I'll think about it". And a selective offer signals quality: a provider who can turn clients away looks like one worth hiring. Scarcity often lets you raise the price as well, because demand exceeds supply.

##### Three ways to limit supply

| Type | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Total limit** | Only a fixed number will ever exist or be served at once. | "We work with at most eight manufacturing clients at a time." |
| **Growth limit** | A cap on how many new clients you take per period. | "We onboard two new implementation clients per month." |
| **Cohort limit** | Clients start in groups, each with a fixed number of places. | "The spring cohort has twelve places and starts on 3 March." |

A related form is limiting a part of the offer instead of the whole: only the first five buyers get a bonus, such as a free data audit. The core offer stays open, but the extra is scarce.

##### Picking the right type

- **Total limit** suits high-touch, premium services where quality depends on your personal involvement. It is the strongest signal, and it can raise price significantly.
- **Growth limit** suits most B2B services. It reflects how onboarding actually works: new clients need the most attention in their first weeks, so you can only absorb a few at a time. It also works permanently, since there is always a next month.
- **Cohort limit** suits programmes, training and group delivery. It combines scarcity (places) with urgency (start date).

##### Using it honestly

The limit must be true and you must stick to it. Practical rules:

1. **Calculate the real number.** Start from your delivery capacity: hours per client in onboarding, people available, quality you want to keep. The cap comes from that, not from what sounds impressive.
2. **Explain the reason.** "Each new client takes about forty hours of my time in the first month, so I start two per month" is believable because it's specific.
3. **Show the state honestly.** Tell buyers how many spots are left in the current period, and keep a waiting list when they are gone.
4. **Enforce it.** If you say two per month and sign a third, the limit stops meaning anything. Put the extra buyer on next month's start date.
5. **Never fake it.** Fake countdowns or "only two left" that never change damage trust, and in many markets they also break consumer protection rules.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik implementation partner (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "We can start whenever suits you." Prospects postponed for months, then several signed in the same week, the team was overloaded and two projects started late.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "We start two implementations per month, because the first four weeks need a senior developer almost full time. November is booked; December has one place left. If it fills, the next start is January." The waiting list is now a normal part of the sales process.
</div>
</div>

The growth limit is real (it comes from onboarding effort), it smooths the workload, and it gives prospects a concrete reason to decide now instead of next quarter.

##### Pilates studio (local business)

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Offer | Open drop-in classes, any time | 8-week beginner cohort, max 10 people per group |
| Start | Whenever | Fixed start date each month |
| Reason given | None | Small group so the instructor can correct each person's form |
| Result | Irregular attendance, many no-shows | Groups fill before the start date, members stay together |

The cohort limit is honest because form correction really does need small groups. It also adds a start date, so scarcity and urgency work together.

#### Common mistakes

- **Fake scarcity.** Limits that never run out or reset every week teach buyers that your words mean nothing. One exposed fake costs more than any sale it brought.
- **No reason given.** "Only three spots" sounds like a sales trick. "Only three spots because each needs a dedicated developer" sounds like a fact.
- **A cap that doesn't fit capacity.** A limit far above what you can deliver doesn't create scarcity; a limit far below it leaves money on the table.
- **Breaking your own limit.** Taking one more client "just this once" removes the credibility of the next limit you announce.
- **Confusing scarcity with urgency.** Scarcity limits how many; urgency limits until when. Use both deliberately, not by accident.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He starts from real capacity.** The first question is how many clients the business can serve without results dropping, and the limit follows from that.
- **He prefers the growth limit for services,** because it reflects onboarding reality and can be used every month without wearing out.
- **He states the reason and the remaining count openly,** and treats the waiting list as proof of demand that also justifies raising prices.
- **He uses scarcity as a pricing signal:** when spots consistently sell out, the right move is a higher price, not more spots squeezed in.
- **He combines it with urgency** (cohorts with start dates, bonuses for the first few) to give buyers both a reason to want it and a reason to act now.
- **He refuses fake limits,** because his business depends on long-term reputation and referrals more than on one more sale.
- **Questions he would ask:** How many new clients can you onboard each month without delivery suffering? What happens today when several sign at once? Do you have a waiting list? When did you last turn someone away? Are you full often enough that the price should go up?

#### Worksheet

Work out a limit you can state honestly.

1. **Capacity:** How many hours and which people does a new client need in the first month? How many can you absorb at once?
2. **Type:** Which fits your delivery best: total limit, growth limit or cohort limit?
3. **Number and reason:** Write the limit and the one-sentence reason you will give buyers.
4. **Visibility:** How will buyers see how many spots are left, and what happens when they are gone?
5. **Price signal:** If you fill every spot for three periods in a row, how much will you raise the price?

## Urgency

- id: urgency
- book: $100M Offers / Enhancing the offer
- original term: Urgency
- key: Scarcity limits how many. Urgency limits how long.
- related: scarcity, bonuses, grand-slam-offer, charge-what-its-worth, naming

A real time limit on your offer, price or bonus. It gives buyers a reason to decide now instead of drifting into "maybe later".

#### The gist

Most lost sales end with "not now", and "not now" rarely comes back. Urgency fixes this by putting a clock on the decision: the offer, the price or a bonus is only available until a set moment. When a buyer knows that waiting costs them something, they decide.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Scarcity** = limited **quantity** (seats, units, slots) · **Urgency** = limited **time** (dates, windows, deadlines)
</div>

Urgency only works if it is honest. A deadline you move or a countdown that resets teaches buyers to ignore you. The best urgency already exists: you find the real deadlines that already exist in your business and in the buyer's world, and you make them visible.

#### How it works

##### Urgency vs scarcity

Both raise demand by making an offer harder to get, and they are often used together. The difference is the dimension. Scarcity says "only five spots". Urgency says "only until Friday". Scarcity can sell out early. Urgency ends on schedule whether or not anyone bought. Using both at once (a limited number of spots in a cohort that starts on a fixed date) is stronger than either alone.

##### Rolling cohorts

You start new clients in groups on fixed dates, for example every first Monday of the month. Anyone who misses this start waits for the next one. This creates a natural, recurring deadline without any discount. It also makes delivery easier, because onboarding happens in batches. Tell buyers the honest consequence of waiting: "The next group starts in four weeks."

##### Rolling seasonal urgency

Tie the offer to a season or calendar moment: New Year, summer, back to school, quarter end, the start of the fiscal year. Each season gets its own promotion with its own end date. When one season ends, the next one begins, so there is always a current deadline. The reason has to fit the season, otherwise it looks like a pretext.

##### Pricing or bonus deadlines

The core offer stays available, but something about it changes after a date:

- **Price deadline:** the price goes up on a known date, and you actually raise it.
- **Bonus deadline:** a bonus is only included for those who decide by a date or within the first call.
- **Terms deadline:** a payment plan or a fee waiver ends on a date.

Bonus deadlines are often the safest choice, because the buyer can still buy later. They just get less. This keeps urgency without punishing late buyers too hard.

##### Exploding opportunity

An offer that is valid for a very short time after it is made: the end of the call, 24 hours, or the end of the week. It works when the opportunity really cannot be held open, for example a slot in your calendar that will be offered to the next person on the list. Use it sparingly and only when the reason is true. Exploding offers that are always re-extended destroy trust fast.

##### B2B: use the real windows

In B2B, artificial countdowns usually backfire. The buyer's world already has plenty of real deadlines. Use them:

| Real window | How it creates urgency |
|---|---|
| Budget cycle | Budget not committed by year end is often lost |
| Project start date | Your team can only start on a date if the contract is signed by an earlier date |
| Capacity planning | Your next free implementation slot is in a specific month |
| Compliance or system change | A regulation or software end-of-support date sets a hard limit |
| Seasonality of their business | Changes must be live before their busy season |

Work backwards from their deadline to yours. "If you want this live before the January close, we need to start on 15 November, so we need a decision by 8 November."

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik implementation for a manufacturer (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Let us know when you're ready and we'll schedule the project." The proposal sits in the CFO's inbox for three months.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "We start two implementations per quarter. The next start is 3 February, which puts your margin reports live before the Q1 close. To hold that slot we need a signed order by 20 January. Orders signed this month also include the budget-planning app at no cost."
</div>
</div>

The deadline is tied to their own goal (the Q1 close), to real capacity, and to a bonus. Nothing is fake, so the deadline can be stated plainly and kept.

##### Dental clinic (local business)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Teeth whitening available all year."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Summer smile: book whitening by 31 May and get a free check-up and cleaning. Results ready before wedding and holiday season."
</div>
</div>

Seasonal urgency with a clear reason (summer events) and a bonus deadline. In June the clinic switches to a back-to-school or autumn promotion with its own end date.

##### Online course (rolling cohort)

| Element | Without urgency | With urgency |
|---|---|---|
| Start | Start any time | Cohort starts on the 1st of each month |
| Price | Same forever | Early sign-ups by the 20th pay less |
| Bonus | None | Live onboarding call only for this cohort |

#### Common mistakes

- **Fake deadlines.** Timers that reset, "last chance" emails every week, extensions for everyone. Buyers notice, and from then on no deadline you set is believed.
- **No reason given.** A deadline without a "because" feels like pressure. A deadline with a real reason (start date, season, capacity) feels like information.
- **Confusing urgency with discounting.** Urgency does not require a lower price. A start date or a bonus deadline creates urgency while keeping the price high.
- **Not keeping the deadline.** If you raise the price on the 1st, raise it. Make an exception once and you have trained every future buyer to wait.
- **Ignoring the buyer's own clock.** In B2B, your deadline is weaker than theirs. Their budget, their go-live date and their season matter more than your promotion.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He uses urgency on almost every offer**, because without a reason to act now, most buyers will postpone even when they want it.
- **He prefers rolling structures:** cohorts and seasonal promotions that renew themselves, so there is always a fresh, honest deadline and he never has to invent one.
- **He puts the deadline on the bonus before the price.** The buyer who waits still can buy, they just lose the extra. It drives decisions without making late buyers feel punished.
- **He combines urgency with scarcity** where both are real: limited spots in a group that starts on a date.
- **He refuses fake urgency.** His rule of thumb is that the long-term cost of being caught lying is far bigger than the short-term sales it brings.
- **He states the cost of waiting in plain terms:** what the buyer loses each week they delay, and when the next chance comes.
- **Questions he would ask:** What real deadline already exists here? When does the next group or slot start? What happens to the buyer's problem if they wait three months? Which bonus could expire without hurting the core offer? Have you ever broken a deadline, and do your buyers know it?

#### Worksheet

Take your main offer and write down the honest time limits around it.

1. **Your deadlines:** What real limits exist on your side: start dates, capacity, price changes planned for next year?
2. **Their deadlines:** What dates matter to the buyer: budget year, season, go-live, regulation, a busy period?
3. **Structure:** Could you sell in rolling cohorts or seasonal promotions? Write the next three start or end dates.
4. **Bonus deadline:** Which bonus could be included only for decisions made by a date?
5. **The sentence:** Write one sentence that states the deadline and its real reason, for example "To start on X, we need a decision by Y, because Z."

## Bonuses

- id: bonuses
- book: $100M Offers / Enhancing the offer
- original term: Bonuses
- key: One obstacle, one bonus, one name, one stated value. Total bonus value greater than the core offer.
- related: problems-solutions, value-equation, trim-and-stack, urgency, guarantees

Extras added to the core offer, each built to remove one specific reason the buyer might not buy. Done well, the bonuses alone are worth more than the price.

#### The gist

A bonus is a sales tool with one job. Every hesitation a buyer has ("my team won't use it", "I don't have time", "what if it breaks") is a problem you can solve with a separate, named piece of the offer. When each worry has its own answer, the buyer runs out of reasons to say no.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Obstacle** → **bonus that solves it** → **name** + **stated value** · Sum of bonus values > price of the core offer
</div>

Bonuses also change how the offer is perceived. One offer with ten features is one thing to evaluate. One core offer followed by five bonuses, each with its own value, feels like the buyer gets far more than they pay. The same contents, split and presented this way, are worth more in the buyer's head.

#### How it works

##### Start from the problems list

In the offer-building process, you list every problem and obstacle the buyer faces before, during and after buying. The core offer solves the main problem. The bonuses come from the rest of that list. For each remaining obstacle ask: what small thing could I add that makes this worry go away?

| Obstacle | Bonus type |
|---|---|
| "I won't know how to start" | Quick-start checklist, onboarding call |
| "My team won't adopt it" | Training kit, internal launch email templates |
| "I don't have time" | Done-for-you setup, templates, scripts |
| "What if I get stuck?" | Support channel, office hours, Q&A recordings |
| "I can't convince my boss" | Business-case template, ROI calculator |

##### One bonus per obstacle

Several smaller bonuses usually beat one large bonus. Each one speaks to its own worry, and each one adds its own value to the stack. When you present a bonus, say which obstacle it solves: "Because most teams worry that users won't log in after week two, you also get the adoption kit."

##### Name every bonus

A named bonus feels like a real thing with a real price. "Templates" is vague. "The Board-Ready Report Pack" is a product. Use the same naming logic as for the main offer: say who it is for and what result it gives.

##### State the value of every bonus

Give each bonus a value, and make it believable. Good anchors are what you would charge for it separately, what it cost to produce, or what a client would pay someone else to do it. Values without any basis lower trust. If you can, sell the bonus separately somewhere, so the value is real.

##### The bonuses together should outweigh the core

A rule of thumb from the book: the total value of the bonuses should be greater than the core offer itself. The buyer should feel that the bonuses alone would justify the price. This is what makes an offer feel unfair in the buyer's favour.

##### Present bonuses after the core offer

The order matters. First present the core offer and its price, so the buyer evaluates the main thing. Then reveal the bonuses one at a time, each with its obstacle, name and value. Each addition raises the perceived value while the price stays the same. If you mix bonuses into the core description, the stacking effect is lost.

##### Cheap for you, valuable to them

The best bonuses cost little to deliver again: templates, checklists, recordings, scripts, software you already built. Their value to the buyer comes from saving time and effort, so they can be very high value at almost no marginal cost.

##### Bonuses from partners

You can include other businesses' products or services as bonuses. A partner gives your buyers a free session, a discount or an audit. The partner gets new customers, you get a bonus with a real market value at no cost, and the buyer gets more. Choose partners whose offer solves one of your buyer's obstacles.

##### Bonuses and urgency or scarcity

Bonuses are a natural place for time and quantity limits. "This bonus is included for orders this month" or "the first ten get the setup call" creates a reason to act without changing the price of the core.

#### Examples

| Obstacle | Bonus | Value |
|---|---|---|

| "We still rely on old Excel reports" | Legacy Report Rescue (5 reports rebuilt) | €3,000 |

| "Our data is messy" | Data Quality Check, delivered by a partner firm | €2,500 |
| **Total bonus value** | | **€10,600** |

The bonuses add up to €10,600, which is more than the €9,800 core price. Each answers a real objection CFOs raise. The Data Quality Check comes from a partner and costs Rok nothing to deliver.

##### Gym 12-week programme (local business)

<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "12-week programme, €600. Includes training plan, nutrition advice, app access and a free friend pass."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">

#### Common mistakes

- **Random bonuses.** A bonus that solves no obstacle adds noise. If you cannot finish the sentence "because many worry that…", drop it.
- **Unnamed, unvalued extras.** "Plus some templates" adds almost nothing to perceived value. Name it and price it.
- **Inflated values.** A €5,000 checklist is not believed and lowers trust in the whole offer. Anchor values in something real.
- **Bonuses in the core description.** Mixing them in removes the stacking effect. Price first, bonuses after.
- **Bonuses that add work.** A bonus that requires more effort from the buyer (another course to watch) can lower value. Prefer bonuses that save time.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He builds bonuses from objections.** He collects the reasons people gave for not buying and turns each one into a bonus.
- **He stacks many small bonuses** rather than one big one, and presents them one by one after the price.
- **He makes the bonuses worth more than the core.** His rule of thumb is that the buyer should feel the bonuses alone justify the purchase.
- **He favours bonuses with low delivery cost and high perceived value**, such as templates, scripts, recordings and tools that save the buyer time.
- **He uses partners for bonuses** because it adds real value without cost, and it builds relationships that can send leads back.
- **He attaches urgency or scarcity to bonuses** instead of discounting the core price.
- **Questions he would ask:** Why did the last ten people who didn't buy say no? Which of those reasons does your core offer not address? What do you already have that solves it? What would each bonus cost the buyer to get elsewhere? Which partner already sells something your buyers need?

#### Worksheet

Take your core offer and its price. Answer in writing.

1. **Obstacles:** List every reason a buyer might hesitate before, during and after buying. Aim for at least ten.
2. **Match:** For the top five, write one bonus that removes each obstacle.
3. **Name and value:** Give each bonus a name and a value you could defend. Add them up. Is the total higher than the core price?
4. **Partners:** Which business could provide one bonus in exchange for access to your clients?
5. **Script:** Write how you would present the stack: core and price first, then each bonus with "because…", its name and its value.

## Guarantees

- id: guarantees
- book: $100M Offers / Enhancing the offer
- original term: Guarantees (Risk Reversal)
- key: If you do X and don't get Y, we will do Z. Conditions are the actions that create success.
- related: value-equation, bonuses, grand-slam-offer, charge-what-its-worth, naming

A promise that moves the risk of buying from the buyer to the seller. It raises perceived likelihood of success and usually wins more sales than it costs in refunds.

#### The gist

Every purchase carries risk, and by default the buyer holds all of it. They pay first and hope it works. A guarantee flips that. The seller takes on the risk, so the buyer's biggest fear (paying and getting nothing) is gone. This raises the perceived likelihood of achievement in the value equation, which raises value directly.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Strong guarantee** = If you do **X** (the actions that create success) and do not get **Y** (the specific result) by **when**, we will do **Z** (refund, extra work, payment back).
</div>

Most sellers fear that guarantees will be abused. In practice the extra buyers who say yes usually outnumber those who ask for refunds. And if a guarantee would bankrupt you, that tells you more about your delivery than about the guarantee.

#### How it works

##### Unconditional guarantee

Money back, no questions asked, within a time window. It is the simplest and most persuasive type, because the buyer does not need to prove anything. It fits offers where the buyer's effort is small and the cost of delivery is low, or where your results are so reliable that refunds are rare. A variation is a guarantee that pays back more than the price, which shows extreme confidence.

##### Conditional guarantee

A refund or extra work if the buyer does their part and still does not get the result. The conditions should be the exact actions that produce success: attend the sessions, supply the data, use the system weekly. This does two things. It protects you from buyers who did nothing, and it pushes buyers to do what makes them succeed. A conditional guarantee can promise more than an unconditional one, because it is tied to effort.

Two common forms:

- **Refund conditional:** "Do the steps, and if it doesn't work, you get your money back."
- **Service conditional:** "Do the steps, and if it doesn't work, we keep working with you for free until it does." This often costs you less than a refund and keeps the client.

##### Anti-guarantee

An explicit "all sales are final", with a clear reason. It works when what you deliver cannot be taken back once given: confidential methods, custom work started on day one, access to private material. The reason is what makes it work. Stated well, it signals exclusivity and seriousness: "Because you get our full internal playbooks on day one, we cannot offer refunds." Without a reason, it just sounds like you don't trust your product.

##### Implied guarantee (performance)

You are only paid, or paid more, when the result happens. Forms include pay per result, a share of revenue or savings, or a low base fee plus a performance bonus. The guarantee is implied because the buyer only pays for outcomes. It works when the result is measurable and agreed in advance, and when you have enough control over delivery to make it happen.

##### Choosing a type

| Type | Fits when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Unconditional | Low price, little buyer effort, reliable results | Cost of refunds if delivery is uneven |
| Conditional | Result depends on what the buyer does | Too many conditions read as distrust |
| Anti-guarantee | What you give cannot be returned | Needs a believable reason |
| Implied / performance | Result is measurable, you control delivery | Disputes about how to measure |

##### Stacking guarantees

You can combine guarantees. For example: an unconditional 14-day "love it or leave it" guarantee on the first phase, plus a conditional guarantee on the final result. Or a service guarantee on the timeline plus a performance fee on savings. Each one removes a different fear.

##### Name the guarantee and make it specific

A named guarantee is remembered and repeated: "the Go-Live Guarantee", "the 30-Day Board-Ready Promise". Be precise about what, by when and what happens. Vague guarantees ("satisfaction guaranteed") add little, because nobody knows what they mean.

**Before.** "We will deliver the agreed reports according to the specification." The CFO worries about paying €15,000 for reports nobody uses.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "The Go-Live Guarantee: if you give us database access in week one and your controller joins two one-hour reviews, your margin reports are live in 30 days. </div>
</div>

The measurement method is written into the contract before work starts.

#### Common mistakes

- **No guarantee out of fear.** Assuming everyone will abuse it. Most buyers don't, and the extra sales usually more than cover the refunds.
- **Conditions nobody can meet.** A guarantee buried in fine print reads as distrust and lowers likelihood instead of raising it.
- **Conditions unrelated to success.** Conditions should be the actions that create the result. Random hoops just look defensive.
- **Vague promises.** "Satisfaction guaranteed" does not say what, by when or what happens. Specific guarantees are believed.
- **An anti-guarantee without a reason.** "No refunds" alone sounds like you expect the product to fail.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He wants a guarantee on almost every offer**, and usually the strongest one the business can survive.
- **He ties conditions to the behaviours that create success**, so the guarantee doubles as a way to make clients do the work.
- **He prefers service guarantees** ("we keep working until you get it") when a refund would hurt, because they cost less and keep the client.
- **He stacks guarantees** to remove several fears at once, and gives each one a name.
- **He treats refund rates as a delivery signal.** If a guarantee would be claimed often, he fixes the product before he weakens the guarantee.
- **He uses performance deals** when results are measurable, because they make the decision almost risk-free for the buyer and can pay the seller more.
- **Questions he would ask:** What is the buyer's biggest fear about buying? What must they do to succeed, and could that be your condition? What would happen if 10% asked for their money back? Can you promise more work instead of money? What would you name it so people remember it?

#### Worksheet

Take your main offer and design its guarantee in writing.

1. **Fear:** What is the buyer most afraid of: losing money, wasting time, looking bad to their boss?
2. **Success actions:** List the three to five things a client must do to get the result. These become your conditions.
3. **Type:** Which type fits: unconditional, conditional, anti-guarantee or performance? Why?
4. **Sentence:** Write it as "If you do X and don't get Y by [when], we will Z."
5. **Stack and name:** Add a second guarantee for a different fear. Give both a name.
6. **Stress test:** If 10% of clients claimed it, what would it cost you? What would you fix in delivery so they don't?

## Naming (MAGIC)

- id: naming
- book: $100M Offers / Enhancing the offer
- original term: MAGIC Naming Formula
- key: MAGIC = Magnetic reason why, Announce the avatar, Give them a goal, Indicate a time interval, Complete with a container word
- related: grand-slam-offer, niche, urgency, bonuses, guarantees

A checklist for naming an offer so the name alone tells the right buyer that it is for them and what they will get. A good name attracts better leads and lets you refresh the same offer when ads get tired.

#### The gist

The name is the first part of the offer a buyer sees, often before any price or detail. A generic name ("Consulting Package B", "Monthly Membership") puts you back in the commodity trap, because it looks like everything else. A specific name tells the right person, at a glance, that this was made for them and what it will do.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**M**agnetic reason why · **A**nnounce the avatar · **G**ive them a goal · **I**ndicate a time interval · **C**omplete with a container word
</div>

MAGIC is a checklist. You do not need every element. Most strong names use three to five of the five. Names also wear out. The same core offer can be renamed and repackaged for a new season or a new audience, which keeps ads and outreach feeling fresh without rebuilding the offer.

#### How it works

##### M: Make a magnetic reason why

A reason for the offer to exist right now. It answers the buyer's unspoken question "why is this being offered?". Good reasons: a season ("Spring"), a launch ("Grand Opening"), an anniversary, a holiday, a new year, a change in the market. A reason why also creates a natural deadline, so it pairs well with urgency.

##### A: Announce the avatar

Say who it is for. "For CFOs", "for new mums", "for manufacturers", "for dentists". Naming the avatar makes the right people feel seen and tells the wrong people to move on. That is the point: you want fewer, better-fit leads. The narrower the avatar, the stronger the pull on the people inside it.

##### G: Give them a goal

The dream outcome in a few words. "Clean Reports", "Pain-Free Back", "Month-End in 3 Days". Use the buyer's language and the result they want, not the method you use. Be careful with claims: in regulated areas (health, finance) or on ad platforms, specific promises may not be allowed, so phrase the goal so that it is both appealing and compliant.

##### I: Indicate a time interval

How long it takes. "30-Day", "6-Week", "in 90 Days". A time frame makes the outcome feel concrete and achievable, and it lowers perceived time delay in the value equation. Only state a time frame your delivery can actually meet.

##### C: Complete with a container word

A word that turns a list of things into a product. Examples: programme, system, blueprint, challenge, accelerator, bootcamp, intensive, masterclass, sprint, club. The container word also sets expectations: a "challenge" sounds short and intense, a "club" sounds ongoing, a "blueprint" sounds like a plan you follow.

##### Putting it together

| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Magnetic reason why | Autumn |
| Avatar | for Production Managers |
| Goal | Clean Reports |
| Interval | 30-Day |
| Container | System |
| **Name** | **Autumn 30-Day Clean Reports System for Production Managers** |

Then cut. Long names are fine for a landing page headline. For an ad or an email subject, keep the two or three strongest elements: "30-Day Clean Reports System".

##### Rename to refresh

Ad audiences get tired of seeing the same message. When response drops, a new name and angle often bring it back while the delivery stays exactly the same. Rotate the reason why with the season, try a different avatar for a different segment, or change the container. Track which names bring the best leads, not just the most clicks.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### BI consulting for CFOs (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Qlik Consulting Package, Level 2."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "The Month-End in 3 Days Programme for CFOs." In January the same offer runs as "New Year Fast-Close Sprint for Mid-Size Manufacturers".
</div>
</div>

The first name uses avatar (CFOs), goal and interval together (month-end in three days) and container (programme). The January version adds a reason why (New Year) and targets a narrower avatar, without changing what is delivered.

##### Hair salon (local business)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Hair treatment package."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Summer Frizz-Free 6-Week Programme for Curly Hair."
</div>
</div>

Reason why (summer humidity), goal (frizz-free), interval (6-week), container (programme) and avatar (curly hair). Someone with straight hair skips it, which is fine. Someone with curly hair feels it was made for them.

##### AI automation (refreshing one offer)

| Season or angle | Name |
|---|---|
| Q1 | New Year Quote-in-an-Hour Sprint for Engineering Firms |
| Q2 | 21-Day Inbox Zero Automation Challenge for Sales Teams |
| Q4 | Year-End Admin Cut Blueprint for Distributors |

The core service is the same in each case: automating a manual process with AI. Only the reason, avatar, goal wording and container change.

#### Common mistakes

- **Naming the process.** "Package B", "Premium Tier", "Implementation Service" say nothing about who it is for or what they get.
- **Trying to fit everyone.** Leaving out the avatar to avoid excluding anyone makes the name weaker for everyone.
- **Forcing all five elements.** A name with every element can become unreadable. Keep what is strongest and cut the rest.
- **Promising what you can't deliver.** An interval or a goal in the name is a claim. If "30-Day" isn't true, the name becomes a source of refunds.
- **Never renaming.** Running the same name for years while ad response falls, instead of testing a fresh angle.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He treats the name as part of the offer**, because it decides who stops to look at all.
- **He leads with the avatar and the goal**, since those two elements do most of the filtering and attracting.
- **He uses the reason why to create a natural deadline**, so naming and urgency work together.
- **He drafts many names and picks the one buyers would repeat** to a friend or colleague, not the one that sounds clever.
- **He renames instead of rebuilding.** When ads fatigue, his first move is a new name and angle on the same delivery.
- **He checks platform and legal rules** before putting a specific claim in a name, and adjusts wording to stay compliant.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who exactly is this for? What result do they want, in their words? Why is this being offered now? How long does it take? What would you call it if you had to fit it in a headline? How long has the current name been running?

#### Worksheet

Take your main offer and name it in writing.

1. **Fill in MAGIC:** Write one option for each letter: reason why, avatar, goal, interval, container.
2. **Draft five names:** Combine the elements in different ways. At least one should use only three elements.
3. **Test:** Read each name to someone in your target audience. Which one do they understand fastest and repeat back?
4. **Short version:** Cut your best name to the two or three strongest words for an ad or email subject.
5. **Refresh plan:** Write the names you would use for the next three seasons or segments, keeping the same delivery.

# $100M Leads — How to get strangers to want to buy your stuff

**Get strangers to want to buy your stuff by getting more engaged leads, more cheaply, from channels you control.**

### The book in one page {#overview}

Most small businesses don't have a sales problem or a product problem. They have too few people interested in what they sell. *$100M Leads* is about fixing that. Its central idea is that a business grows by getting more **engaged leads**: people who have shown interest in your offer by replying, booking, downloading or otherwise raising a hand.

Advertising, in this book, simply means making something known. Every message, post and ad counts. You pay for each engaged lead with time or money, so the work is to get more of them, at a cost that is lower than what a customer is worth.

The book follows the order in which you build lead flow:

1. **Understand leads.** Count engaged leads, not contacts. Give a **lead magnet**: a complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals the next problem your paid offer solves.
2. **Pick one of the Core Four.** Warm outreach, free content, cold outreach or paid ads. Choose based on whether you have more time or more money.
3. **Do it at volume.** The Rule of 100 sets a daily minimum of primary actions until the channel is predictable.
4. **Improve it.** Do more of what works, make it better, and only then try something new.
5. **Add lead getters.** Once you know what works, get customers, employees, agencies and affiliates to bring leads too.

### How Alex would use this book {#alex}

- When a business with a good offer isn't growing, he checks lead flow first: how many engaged leads came in last month, and from where.
- He would start a business with no money on warm outreach and a strong lead magnet, before content or ads.
- He insists on one channel at a time, run at volume every day, until it produces leads predictably.
- He measures cost per engaged lead against what a customer is worth, and spends more wherever the first is clearly lower.
- He adds lead getters only after he can teach the process, because others can only repeat what is already working.

## Leads & Engaged Leads

- id: engaged-leads
- book: $100M Leads / Understanding leads
- original term: Engaged Leads
- key: Cost per engaged lead = what you spent (time or money) ÷ number of engaged leads
- related: lead-magnets, core-four, rule-of-100, grand-slam-offer

A lead is someone you can contact. An engaged lead is someone who has shown interest in what you sell, and those are the only leads that turn into customers.

#### The gist

A **lead** is a person you can get in touch with: a name with a phone number, an email, a LinkedIn profile. An **engaged lead** is a lead who has done something that shows interest in your stuff. They replied, booked a call, downloaded a lead magnet, came to an event, or asked a question. The difference is action. A list of 5,000 emails is a list of leads. The 40 people who answered your message are engaged leads.

**Advertising** simply means making something known. A text to an old colleague, a LinkedIn post, a cold email and a paid ad are all advertising. The point of all of it is the same: turn strangers into engaged leads, then turn engaged leads into customers in a conversation.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Strangers** → (advertising) → **Leads** → (they show interest) → **Engaged leads** → (sales) → **Customers**
</div>

Every engaged lead is paid for, either with **time** (writing messages, making content) or with **money** (ads, agencies, list data). So the number that matters is the **cost per engaged lead**, and the business grows when you get more of them, cheaper, from sources you control.

#### How it works

##### Lead vs engaged lead

| | Lead | Engaged lead |
|---|---|---|
| What you have | Contact details | Contact details plus a signal of interest |
| Example | A name on a list of 600 manufacturers | A plant manager who replied "send me the audit" |
| What you do next | Make them aware of you | Start a sales conversation |
| Value to the business | Low until they act | High, they're halfway to buying |

The useful habit is to stop counting leads and start counting engaged leads. A big list feels like progress. The only thing that predicts sales is how many people raised their hand.

##### What counts as "showing interest"

An engaged lead has taken a step toward you. Typical signals, from weaker to stronger:

1. Opened, liked or watched (weak, often not counted).
2. Replied to a message or comment.
3. Downloaded or requested a lead magnet.
4. Filled in a form with real details.
5. Booked a call or showed up to one.

Decide in advance which actions count for your business, write it down, and count the same way every week. Otherwise you can't compare channels or months.

##### Advertising means making known

If nobody knows your offer exists, nobody can buy it. Many service businesses say "we don't advertise, we get work by word of mouth". In Alex's sense, word of mouth is advertising too, just done by someone else and not under your control. Seeing every message, post and ad as advertising helps you treat it as a system you can measure and improve.

##### You pay with time or money

There is no free lead. Warm outreach and free content cost hours. Cold outreach costs hours plus tools and data. Paid ads cost money. Early on most people have more time than money, so they buy leads with time. Later they swap time for money to grow faster. Both are fine. Pretending leads are free means you never measure what they cost.

##### Measure cost per engaged lead

A simple version that works for time and money:

- Track what you spent on a channel in a period (hours × your hourly value, plus money).
- Count engaged leads from that channel in the same period.
- Divide.

Then compare with what an engaged lead is worth: how many of them become customers, and how much profit a customer brings over time. If an engaged lead is worth more than it costs, you buy more of them. That is the whole game.

##### Why this framing comes first

Lead generation is often treated as a creative problem ("we need better content"). Framed as engaged leads, it becomes a numbers problem: how many hands were raised, at what cost, from which source. Numbers can be improved one step at a time.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik consultant counting the wrong thing (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "This month I added 300 new LinkedIn connections and my newsletter grew to 1,200 subscribers." No idea how many people are interested in buying.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** He counts only replies asking for his free reporting audit and booked calls. This month: 14 engaged leads. He spent about 20 hours on outreach, valued at €50/hour, so €1,000 in time. Cost per engaged lead: €1,000 ÷ 14 ≈ €71. Four of the 14 became projects worth €6,000 each.
</div>
</div>

The vanity numbers said nothing about demand. The engaged-lead count shows that one engaged lead is worth about €6,000 × 4 ÷ 14 ≈ €1,714 in revenue against a cost of about €71. The obvious decision is to do much more of the same.

##### Dental practice (local business)

| Source | Spend | Engaged leads (booked check-up) | Cost per engaged lead |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local ad | €600 | 20 | €30 |
| Flyers | €400 | 4 | €100 |
| Texting past patients | 5 hours of staff time (€100) | 25 | €4 |

The practice was about to double the flyer budget because flyers "feel like marketing". Measured as engaged leads, texting past patients is 25 times cheaper per booked check-up than flyers, and the local ad is next best.

#### Common mistakes

- **Counting leads instead of engaged leads.** Followers, connections, list size and impressions are not interest. Count actions that show interest.
- **Thinking leads are free.** Hours count. If you don't price your time, you can't compare a channel that costs time with one that costs money.
- **Changing the definition every month.** If "engaged" means a reply in March and a booked call in April, your numbers lie. Fix the definition.
- **Not knowing what an engaged lead is worth.** Without a close rate and a customer value, you can't tell whether a lead is cheap or expensive.
- **Waiting for word of mouth.** Referrals are welcome, but a business that only waits has no way to get more when it needs them.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He asks for the number of engaged leads first.** Before talking about sales skill or pricing, he wants to know how many people raised their hand last month. Too few is the most common reason a small business isn't growing.
- **He treats lead flow as the default bottleneck** for businesses that sell well once they're in a conversation. More engaged leads is usually the fastest fix.
- **He prices time.** If you spend your evenings on outreach, he counts those hours as a cost and expects you to know what each engaged lead costs you.
- **He compares cost to value.** If an engaged lead is clearly worth more than it costs, his answer is to do more, a lot more, before trying anything new.
- **He wants sources you control.** Word of mouth and luck are fine, but he builds at least one channel you can turn up on demand.
- **Questions he would ask:** How many engaged leads did you get last month, and from where? What counts as engaged for you? What did each one cost in time or money? How many became customers? What is a customer worth to you?

#### Worksheet

Look at the last 30 days and answer with real numbers, not estimates where you can avoid them.

1. **Definition:** Which actions count as "engaged" in your business? Write one clear rule.
2. **Count:** How many engaged leads did you get, and from which sources?
3. **Cost:** How many hours and how much money went into each source? Put a price on your hour.
4. **Cost per engaged lead:** Divide cost by engaged leads for each source. Which is cheapest?
5. **Value:** What share of engaged leads became customers, and what is a customer worth? Is each engaged lead worth more than it costs?
6. **Decision:** Which one source will you do more of next month, and by how much?

## Lead Magnets

- id: lead-magnets
- book: $100M Leads / Understanding leads
- original term: Lead Magnet
- key: Lead magnet = complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals the next problem
- related: engaged-leads, core-four, grand-slam-offer, naming, attraction-offers

A free offer that completely solves one narrow problem and, in doing so, reveals the next problem your paid offer solves. It turns strangers into engaged leads who already trust you.

#### The gist

A **lead magnet** is a free offer that gives a complete solution to a narrow problem. Solving it reveals a **next problem**, and that next problem is the one your core offer solves. It is a small, real win, not a brochure or "an ebook with 50 tips". The person gets value, and you get an engaged lead who already trusts you and knows what they need next.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Narrow problem** solved for free → reveals the **next problem** → solved by your **core offer**
</div>

The lead magnet is how you make every channel work better. A message, post or ad that offers something valuable and free gets far more responses than one that asks for a purchase. The quality of what you give away also sets what people expect from what you sell.

#### How it works

##### Three types of lead magnet

| Type | What it is | Best fit |
|---|---|---|
| **Reveal their problem** | A diagnosis, audit, test, assessment or calculator that shows where the problem is and what it costs | Problems the buyer doesn't see or underestimates, hidden costs |
| **Samples and trials** | A piece of the core offer: a free first session, one module, a limited trial, a tasting | Offers where experience is the best proof |
| **One step of a multi-step process** | You give away one step in full, often the first, of a solution that has several | Processes with clear stages where the rest is work they'd rather hand off |

**Reveal their problem.** Many people don't buy because they don't know they have a problem, or think it's small. A diagnosis turns "we'll see" into "we have to fix this", especially when it puts a number on it.

**Samples and trials.** There is no stronger proof than using the thing. The sample should impress, and stop before it solves everything. A free software trial is a sample.

**One step of a process.** The person makes real progress and at the same moment sees how many steps remain. The natural next question is whether you can do the rest.

##### How to build one: seven steps

1. **Pick the narrow problem and the audience.** One concrete, painful problem your ideal buyer has, linked directly to your paid offer. "Better business" is too broad. "Which of our customers lose us money" is narrow.
2. **Choose the type.** Reveal, sample or one step. Ask which one leads most naturally to the next problem you solve.
3. **Choose the delivery.** Software (a tool, calculator, template that computes), information (a guide, short video, checklist), a service (an audit, a call, a workshop) or physical (a sample, a box). Pick what is easiest for the buyer and affordable for you to give at volume.
4. **Test the name.** The name decides whether people take it. Write several, show them to real prospects or run them in small tests, and keep the one that gets the most responses. A name that states the outcome and the audience usually beats a clever one.
5. **Make it easy to consume.** Few steps, little reading, no waiting. A short video or a filled-in template beats a 40-page PDF nobody opens. If they don't consume it, they never reach the next problem.
6. **Make it really good.** The test is whether people would pay for it. If not, improve it until they would. People judge your paid offer by your free one.
7. **Add a clear call to action.** Tell them exactly what to do next and why now, for example book a call to fix what the audit showed. A great lead magnet with no next step creates grateful people, not customers.

##### Why "complete" and "narrow" go together

If the problem is narrow, you can solve it completely for free without giving away your business. If it is broad, you can only solve it halfway, and a half-solution feels like a teaser. Narrow and complete builds trust. Broad and partial feels like a trick.

##### Give away the secrets, sell the implementation

A common fear is that a great free thing removes the need to buy. In practice, the more people learn, the more they see how much work the rest is, and the more they want someone to do it for them. You give away what to do. You sell doing it, doing it faster, or doing it with less risk.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### ERP data-quality audit (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Download our ebook: 10 business intelligence trends for 2026."
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Send us an export of customers and items from your ERP. Within 48 hours you get a report showing how many duplicates, empty fields and wrong codes you have, and how many invoices are misclassified because of it."
</div>
</div>

Type: reveal the problem, delivered as a service. The ebook solved nothing and gave no reason to hire anyone. The audit fully answers a narrow question ("how bad is our data?") and reveals the next problem: cleaning the data and building reliable reporting, which is the paid offer. Most of the checks are scripted, so each audit is cheap to deliver.

##### Bakery that wants office orders (local business)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A flyer with catering prices left at reception desks of nearby companies.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** On Monday mornings they bring a free tasting box for ten people to a team meeting, with a card: "Order a regular Friday delivery and get the first month at 15% off."
</div>
</div>

Type: sample, physical delivery. The flyer asked people to imagine the product. The box gives the experience and raises the next problem: who sorts out snacks every week? The call to action is concrete and time-limited.

##### Automation process map (AI consulting)

An AI consultancy offers a free 45-minute workshop. With a department head they list the five most common manual tasks and estimate hours per month and how suitable each is for automation. The client leaves with a priority table they can use without the consultant. It is step one of a four-step process (map, prototype, production, maintenance). The close: "We'll get the first process on your list live in three weeks."

#### Common mistakes

- **Too broad a problem.** "Everything about digital transformation" solves nothing. A narrow problem solved completely beats a broad one solved halfway.
- **No link to the paid offer.** If the next problem isn't the one you sell, you collect grateful people, not clients.
- **Low quality because it's free.** An average lead magnet signals an average service.
- **Hard to consume.** Long documents, complex forms and waiting mean most people never get the value, and never reach the next step.
- **No call to action.** The person is impressed and then doesn't know what to do. Always give one next step and make it easy.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He works backwards from the core offer.** First he asks what problem the paid offer solves, then which problem comes right before it. That earlier problem is the lead magnet.
- **He raises the bar on quality.** His rule of thumb: if people wouldn't pay for it, it isn't good enough to give away.
- **He prefers the cheapest delivery that still impresses.** Software and recorded information scale for free; a service converts better but costs time. He picks based on volume and margin.
- **He tests names and hooks before polishing.** The same magnet with a better name can get many more takers, so he tests several and keeps the winner.
- **He puts the lead magnet in every channel.** Warm messages, posts, cold emails and ads all offer the same free thing, so every channel produces engaged leads instead of asking for a sale.
- **He cuts anything that slows consumption:** long intros, extra form fields, downloads that need setup.
- **Questions he would ask:** What problem does your paid offer solve, and what problem comes just before it? Would anyone pay for your free thing? How long does it take to consume? What do they find out at the end that makes them want more? What exactly do you ask them to do next?

#### Worksheet

Design one lead magnet for your main offer. Answer in writing.

1. **Narrow problem:** What one painful problem does your ideal buyer have just before they need your paid offer?
2. **Type:** Is it best solved by revealing the problem, a sample, or one step of your process? Why?
3. **Delivery:** Software, information, service or physical? What is easiest for them and affordable for you at volume?
4. **Name:** Write three names that state the outcome and the audience. Which would you test first?
5. **Quality test:** Would someone pay for it? How much? What would you add so the answer is yes?
6. **Next step:** What will they discover after using it, and what exactly is your call to action?

## The Core Four

- id: core-four
- book: $100M Leads / The Core Four
- original term: Core Four
- key: Known × one-to-one = warm outreach · known × one-to-many = free content · unknown × one-to-one = cold outreach · unknown × one-to-many = paid ads
- related: warm-outreach, free-content, cold-outreach, paid-ads, engaged-leads

There are only four ways to let people know about you, set by two questions. Do they already know you, and are you reaching one person or many? Pick one, master it, then add the next.

#### The gist

Every way of getting engaged leads is a version of four activities. You can only reach people who **know you** or people who **don't**, and you can reach them **one at a time** or **many at once**. Those two axes give four combinations, and Alex calls them the **Core Four**.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
| | **One-to-one** | **One-to-many** |
|---|---|---|
| **Known audience** (warm) | [Warm outreach](warm-outreach.html) | [Posting free content](free-content.html) |
| **Unknown audience** (cold) | [Cold outreach](cold-outreach.html) | [Paid ads](paid-ads.html) |
</div>

Seeing it this way stops the hunt for a secret channel. LinkedIn, email, events, podcasts and billboards are all one of these four. The question becomes which one you do, how much of it, and how well. The rule is simple: **start with one, get good at it, then add the next.**

#### How it works

##### The two axes

**Known vs unknown audience.** People who know you (friends, past clients, followers) trust you more and reply more often, but there are only so many of them. People who don't know you are an almost unlimited pool, but they trust you less and reply less.

**One-to-one vs one-to-many.** Talking to one person is personal and converts well, but your hours cap it. Talking to many at once scales, but each message is less personal and the average response is lower.

| Combination | Strength | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Known, one-to-one | Free, fast, highest reply rate | You run out of people you know |
| Known, one-to-many | One piece reaches many and keeps working | Slow to build, needs an audience |
| Unknown, one-to-one | No limit on who you can reach, precise targeting | Low reply rate, needs volume |
| Unknown, one-to-many | Fastest to scale once it works | Costs money, a bad ad burns budget faster |

##### The four channels in one line each

- **[Warm outreach](warm-outreach.html):** personal messages to people who already know you. The best place to start for almost everyone.
- **[Posting free content](free-content.html):** publishing useful things for an audience that follows you, so warm leads come to you over time.
- **[Cold outreach](cold-outreach.html):** personal messages to people who don't know you, from a list you build. A volume game.
- **[Paid ads](paid-ads.html):** paying a platform to show your message to people who don't know you. The fastest way to scale a message that already works.

##### Start with one, master it, then add

Most people do a little of everything and none of it enough to learn anything. Alex's approach is the opposite:

1. **Pick one channel that fits your resources.** More time than money points to warm outreach, content or cold outreach. More money than time points to paid ads.
2. **Do it at volume, every day.** See [the Rule of 100](rule-of-100.html) for the daily-volume habit.
3. **Get it working predictably.** You know roughly how many engaged leads a given amount of effort produces.
4. **Only then add a second channel.** Larger businesses eventually run all four, but they added them one at a time.

##### A natural order for many small businesses

As a rule of thumb, many start with warm outreach (free and quick), add content (builds trust over time), then cold outreach (removes the limit of who you know), and finally paid ads (once profit pays for them). The right order depends on your time, money and market, so treat this as a default, not a law.

##### What every channel needs

Whichever you choose, the pieces are the same. An offer worth responding to (ideally a [lead magnet](lead-magnets.html)), a message the right people notice, a clear next step, and enough volume to measure [cost per engaged lead](engaged-leads.html).

##### Freelance BI consultant with no new clients (B2B analytics)

<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** An occasional LinkedIn post saying "available for new projects", a website with no traffic, and two months without an enquiry. A bit of everything, nothing at volume.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** He picks warm outreach only. He exports 400 contacts and sends 20 personal messages a day to former colleagues and clients, offering a free reporting review and asking whether they know anyone it would help. In three weeks: 9 conversations, 2 projects.
</div>
</div>

He moved from a passive presence to one active channel he controls. Once it runs, he can add content on the same themes that worked in conversations.

##### Hair salon with empty Tuesdays (local business)

| Channel | What it could look like |
|---|---|
| Warm outreach | A personal text to clients who haven't visited in three months, offering a Tuesday slot |
| Free content | Short before/after videos with an at-home care tip and a booking link |
| Cold outreach | Visiting nearby offices with a staff offer |
| Paid ads | A local ad within 5 km aimed at parents free on Tuesday mornings |

#### Common mistakes

- **All channels at once.** No channel gets enough volume to show whether it works. Pick one and drive it to results.
- **Skipping warm outreach out of embarrassment.** It is often the fastest path to the first clients, and asking for a referral isn't pushy.
- **Selling in the first message.** Offer value first and get an engaged lead. The sale happens in the conversation.
- **Content and ads without a call to action.** Followers and clicks are not engaged leads. Always say what to do next.
- **Quitting too early.** Ten posts or one small campaign is not a test of a channel. Without volume you can't tell whether the channel or the message is the problem.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He maps what you're doing onto the four.** Whatever tactics you list, he sorts them into the grid. Usually it shows you're barely doing any of them.
- **He picks one channel based on resources.** Little money means you buy leads with time: warm outreach, content or cold outreach. Money and no time means ads.
- **He asks about volume before tactics.** If the channel "doesn't work", his first suspicion is that you didn't do enough of it.
- **He adds channels only when the current one is predictable,** and treats each new one as its own skill to learn.
- **He keeps the same lead magnet across channels,** so what wins in one channel can be reused in the next.
- **He cuts shiny-channel hopping.** A new platform is still one of the four. He would rather you get better at the one you have.
- **Questions he would ask:** Which of the four are you doing now, and how much each day? Do you have more time or more money? How many engaged leads did your main channel bring last month? Is that channel predictable yet? What is your offer in every message?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing for your business. The goal is to pick one channel for the next 30 days.

1. **Inventory:** How many people already know you (contacts, followers, past clients)? How many might know someone with your problem?
2. **Resources:** Do you have more time or more money right now? Which channel fits that best?
3. **Choice:** Which one channel will you run for 30 days, and how much volume per day?
4. **Engaged lead:** What exactly must someone do to count as an engaged lead in this channel?
5. **Message:** Write the first message, post or ad. What does it offer for free, and what is the call to action?
6. **Next channel:** What has to be true (leads per week, cost) before you add a second one?

## Warm Outreach

- id: warm-outreach
- book: $100M Leads / The Core Four
- original term: Warm Outreach
- key: ACA = Acknowledge, Compliment, Ask. Reach out to 100 people a day.
- related: core-four, engaged-leads, lead-magnets, rule-of-100, cold-outreach

Personal, one-to-one messages to people who already know you. It's free, works today and is where almost everyone should start getting engaged leads.

#### The gist

**Warm outreach** is reaching out, one person at a time, to people who already know you: friends, former colleagues, past clients, classmates, people in your phone. It's the top-left box of the [Core Four](core-four.html): known audience, one-to-one. It costs no money, the reply rate is the highest of the four channels, and you can start in the next hour.

The method is simple. Go through every contact list you have. Send each person a personal message. Start a real conversation before you mention what you do. Keep it going with **ACA**. Then make an offer, or ask whether they know anyone who could use it. Do this at volume and follow up.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Contact list** → personal message → conversation (**A**cknowledge, **C**ompliment, **A**sk) → offer or referral ask → follow up
</div>

Warm outreach also teaches you to sell. You hear real objections, real words for the problem and real reactions to your offer, from people who are kind enough to answer. That makes every other channel better later.

#### How it works

##### 1. Build the list

Collect everyone you can contact in one place: phone contacts, email, LinkedIn, Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp groups, old client lists, alumni groups. Most people have far more than they think, often several hundred to a few thousand. Don't filter too hard. Someone who would never buy may know someone who would.

##### 2. Personalise the first message

Each message refers to the person, not to you. Mention something you know about them, something you saw them post, or the last time you spoke. A message that could be sent to anyone reads like an ad and gets ignored. Short and specific beats long and polished.

##### 3. Start a conversation before you offer

The first goal is a reply, not a sale. Ask how they are, what they've been up to, how the thing they posted turned out. Only once you're talking do you mention what you're working on.

##### 4. Keep it going with ACA

ACA is a framework commonly associated with *$100M Leads* for keeping a conversation natural:

| Step | What you do | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Acknowledge** | Repeat back or respond to what they said | They feel heard |
| **Compliment** | Say something genuinely positive about them or what they did | It builds goodwill and lowers guard |
| **Ask** | Ask another question that moves the conversation on | It keeps them talking, and steers toward the problem you solve |

You repeat ACA until the conversation naturally reaches their situation, their work, or a problem you can help with.

##### 5. Offer, or ask for a referral

When the moment fits, tell them what you do and offer something valuable, often your [lead magnet](lead-magnets.html), for free. If they don't need it, ask whether they know anyone who does. The referral ask lowers the pressure because you aren't asking them to buy. A common result is that the person says they could use it themselves.

For your very first clients, a free or heavily discounted version in exchange for feedback and a testimonial is a reasonable trade. You are buying proof and practice.

##### 6. Volume

Warm outreach works when you do a lot of it. Alex ties it to the [Rule of 100](rule-of-100.html): 100 primary actions a day, which here means reaching out to 100 people a day. That number is a rule of thumb. The principle is that a handful of messages a week tells you nothing, while hundreds tell you what works.

##### 7. Follow up

Many people don't reply the first time because they are busy, not uninterested. Follow up politely after a few days, and again later. Track who you contacted, when, and where each conversation stands, in a simple spreadsheet or CRM.

##### When warm outreach runs out

Your list is finite. That's fine: warm outreach gets you the first clients, testimonials and a tested offer. Then you add another channel, typically [free content](free-content.html) or [cold outreach](cold-outreach.html), using what you learned.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik consultant starting his own practice (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A LinkedIn post: "I've gone independent, available for Qlik projects, DM me." Twelve likes, no enquiries.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** He exports 600 contacts and messages 100 a day. To a former colleague: "Saw your plant got the new line running, congrats. How's reporting holding up since?" She replies that month-end takes days. He acknowledges it, compliments how her team handled the rollout, and asks what takes longest. Then he offers a free two-hour reporting review, and asks if anyone else in her network has the same problem.
</div>
</div>

The post broadcast an ad. The messages started real conversations about a real problem. Personal, a free offer, a referral ask, at volume: over three weeks that kind of outreach can produce several calls from people who already trust him.

##### Personal trainer filling a new class (local business)

| Step | What she does |
|---|---|
| List | Goes through 800 phone and Instagram contacts |
| Message | "Hey, saw your marathon photos, how did the knee hold up?" |
| ACA | Acknowledges the injury, compliments the finish, asks what they're training for next |
| Offer | Two free strength sessions for runners, ask if a running buddy wants to join |
| Volume | 100 messages a day for two weeks |
| Follow-up | A short reminder after four days to everyone who didn't answer |

If 1,400 messages get a 20% reply rate, that's 280 conversations. Even if only 5% of those conversations take the free sessions, that's 14 people trying the class, and the first ones become her testimonials.

#### Common mistakes

- **Pitching in the first message.** It reads like spam from a friend. Start a conversation first.
- **Copy-paste messages.** Generic messages get generic silence. Mention something about them.
- **Too few messages.** Ten messages and two replies proves nothing. Volume is the method.
- **Skipping people out of embarrassment.** The people who know you are the most likely to help. Asking for a referral is not pushy.
- **No follow-up and no tracking.** Without a list of who's where, warm leads go cold and you message the same people twice.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He makes it the default first channel.** For anyone with no money and no leads, he would start here before content, cold outreach or ads.
- **He sets a daily number and holds to it.** His rule of thumb is 100 reach-outs a day, every day, until it's a habit and the numbers are clear.
- **He leads with value, not a pitch.** He'd offer the lead magnet or the service itself for free to the first people, to get proof and practice.
- **He uses the referral ask** because it removes pressure and opens doors to people you don't know yet.
- **He follows up more than feels comfortable,** because most replies come after the first message.
- **He treats it as sales training.** Every conversation tells you how people describe the problem and what they object to. He'd feed that back into the offer.
- **Questions he would ask:** How many people are on your combined contact lists? How many did you message today? What's your reply rate? What do you offer them for free? Are you asking for referrals? Who did you follow up with this week?

#### Worksheet

Plan your first two weeks of warm outreach. Answer in writing.

1. **List:** Where are all your contacts? Export them into one sheet. How many do you have in total?
2. **Opener:** Write three personal first lines for three different people. What do you know about each of them?
3. **ACA:** For one likely reply, write an Acknowledge, a Compliment and an Ask.
4. **Offer:** What free thing will you offer, and what's your referral question?
5. **Volume:** How many messages a day will you send, and at what time each day?
6. **Tracking:** Where will you log who you contacted, replies and follow-up dates?

## Posting Free Content

- id: free-content
- book: $100M Leads / The Core Four
- original term: Posting Free Content
- key: Every piece = Hook + Retain + Reward. Give far more than you ask.
- related: core-four, lead-magnets, engaged-leads, warm-outreach, more-better-new

Publishing valuable content to people who already follow you, so that trust builds and warm leads come to you. One-to-many to people who know you.

#### The gist

Free content is the Core Four channel where you speak to many people at once who already know you: followers, subscribers, readers. You give value in public, they consume it, and over time some of them raise their hand and ask to buy. It is slow to start and compounds, because each piece keeps working long after you post it.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Content that gets leads** = **Hook** (they start) + **Retain** (they stay) + **Reward** (they got value, so they come back) + **occasional asks** (they know what to do next)
</div>

The core rule is to give far more than you ask. Most pieces should only give. When you do ask, the ask is clear and specific, and because you rarely ask, people listen. The best raw material is your own real work: what you built, fixed, learned or got wrong this week.

#### How it works

##### Hook: get the right person to start

The hook is whatever makes someone stop and pay attention. It has three parts.

- **Topic.** What the piece is about. Good topics come from your work, your clients' problems, and things that already performed well for others in your space. Pick a topic your ideal client already cares about.
- **Headline.** The first line, title or first seconds. Headlines that work tend to be recent, relevant to the reader, specific, a bit unusual, or about a tension or conflict. A concrete number or result helps.
- **Format.** How it is packaged: a short text post, a carousel, a long article, a short video, a long video, a podcast. The same topic can be told in several formats.

##### Retain: keep their attention to the end

Once they start, you have to keep them. Simple structures do most of the work: a numbered list, step-by-step instructions, or a story with a problem, a struggle and a resolution. Open a question early and answer it near the end. Cut anything that does not serve the promise of the hook.

##### Reward: pay off the promise

At the end the reader must feel the time was well spent. They learned something, got a template, saw a result or laughed. Reward is what earns the next click. A great hook with no reward works once and then trains people to ignore you.

##### Give, give, give, ask

Value builds goodwill. Asks spend it. Keep most pieces pure value, then make an occasional ask with a clear call to action: "comment DATA and I'll send the checklist", "book a call via the link", "reply if you want the template". An ask can also live inside a value piece, as a short line at the end. If you never ask, you are building an audience, not leads.

##### Use your real work as the source

You do not need to invent content. Document what you already do: the project, the problem, the fix, the numbers before and after, the mistake that cost a day. This is endless, it is honest, and it doubles as proof that you can do the job.

##### Consistency and volume before polish

Early on, output matters more than perfection. You learn what works by posting a lot and watching what gets attention. Polish comes later, once you know which topics and formats your audience responds to. A rule of thumb: post on a fixed schedule you can keep for months.

##### One platform first

Pick one platform where your ideal clients already spend time and build there until it works. Then repurpose the winning pieces to other platforms. Spreading thin across five channels at the start means none of them gets enough volume to tell you anything.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik consultant on LinkedIn (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** Once a month: "We are a BI partner offering dashboards and analytics. Contact us for a quote." A handful of likes, no conversations.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** Three posts a week, each documenting real work. "A manufacturer's margin report took 40 minutes to load. Here are the three data model changes that got it to 4 seconds." Ends with a short diagram. Once every two weeks: "Comment MODEL and I'll send the checklist I use to review data models."
</div>
</div>

The hook is a concrete before/after number, the list of three changes retains, and the diagram is the reward. The ask is rare and specific, and it hands over a lead magnet, so every comment is an engaged lead he can follow up with.

##### Bakery on Instagram (local business)

| Element | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | "Fresh bread today" | "Why our sourdough takes 36 hours" |
| Retain | One photo | Short video of each step, start to finish |
| Reward | Nothing to take away | One tip for keeping bread fresh at home |
| Ask | Every post says "come visit" | Once a week: "order by Thursday for Saturday pickup" |

The bakery films what it already does every night. The process itself is the content, and the weekly ask gives followers one clear action.

##### AI automation consultant (newsletter)

A consultant sends a weekly email with one automation she built that week: the problem, the tools, the time saved and one screenshot. Every fourth issue ends with an offer of a free process review for three companies. The other three issues only give.

#### Common mistakes

- **Asking in every piece.** If every post sells, people stop reading. Goodwill runs out faster than you build it.
- **Never asking.** Followers are not leads. Without a clear call to action you collect likes, not conversations.
- **Hook without reward.** Clickbait works once. If the piece does not pay off the headline, the next headline gets ignored.
- **Polishing before posting.** Spending a week on one perfect video means you learn nothing. Volume teaches you what works.
- **Starting on every platform at once.** Each platform gets too little output to show results, and you burn out.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He treats content as a long game.** He expects it to compound over months and years, and he does not judge it on a few weeks of results.
- **He leans hard on giving.** He would rather give away his best ideas for free, because the people who act on them become the best buyers.
- **He builds every piece on Hook, Retain, Reward.** If a piece underperforms, he checks which of the three failed instead of blaming the algorithm.
- **He documents instead of inventing.** He turns what he and his companies actually do into content, which keeps it honest and credible.
- **He picks one platform, maxes it out, then goes wide.** He repurposes pieces that already worked rather than starting from scratch elsewhere.
- **He pushes volume first, then improves.** More output, then better output, then new platforms, in that order.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who exactly is this for, and what do they care about right now? What did you do this week that they would want to see? What is the hook, what keeps them, and what do they walk away with? How often do you ask, and what exactly do you ask them to do? How many pieces have you posted on this platform so far?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing for your main platform.

1. **Platform:** Where do your ideal clients already spend time? Commit to that one for the next 90 days.
2. **Topics:** List ten things you did or solved at work in the last month that a client would find useful.
3. **Hook, Retain, Reward:** For your best topic, write the headline, the structure that keeps people reading, and what they take away.
4. **Ask:** What is the one clear action you want readers to take? Which lead magnet will you offer in exchange?
5. **Rhythm:** How many pieces per week can you keep up for months? How often will you include an ask?

## Cold Outreach

- id: cold-outreach
- book: $100M Leads / The Core Four
- original term: Cold Outreach
- key: Targeted list + personal first line + big fast value + many touches, repeated at volume
- related: core-four, warm-outreach, lead-magnets, engaged-leads, rule-of-100

Contacting strangers one by one, by email, DM, phone or text. Response rates are low, so it works through targeted lists, a big free offer, many follow-ups and volume.

#### The gist

Cold outreach is the Core Four channel where you contact people who do not know you, one at a time. Its big advantage is control: you choose exactly who hears from you, and you are not limited by how many people you already know. Its big drawback is that strangers ignore most messages, so it only works with volume, persistence and an offer that makes replying worth it.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Engaged leads from cold outreach** = **Right people** (list) × **Reason to reply** (personalisation + big fast value) × **Touches** (follow-ups) × **Volume**
</div>

Strangers have no reason to trust you. You earn a reply by showing you did your homework and by offering something useful for free, often a lead magnet. You do not sell in the first message. You get an engaged lead first, and the sale happens later in conversation.

#### How it works

##### Build or buy a targeted list

Everything starts with who is on the list. Define your ideal client first: industry, size, role, the problem they have. Then collect contacts that match. You can build a list yourself from public sources, company websites, professional networks and event lists, or buy one from a data provider. Bought lists save time but need checking: remove wrong roles, dead addresses and companies that do not fit. A smaller list of the right people beats a large list of the wrong ones.

##### Personalise at scale

A message that could be sent to anyone gets treated like spam. Add one or two details that show it was written for this person: a recent project, a machine or certificate mentioned on their site, a post they wrote, a hire they made. The trick is to do this at scale. Research a few facts per contact in a spreadsheet, then drop them into a template. A rule of thumb: more personal means better replies but fewer messages per hour, so find the balance that still gives you volume.

##### Lead with big, fast value

The offer in the first message has to be worth a stranger's time. The best cold offers give a real result quickly and for free: a short audit, a calculation of what a problem costs them, a sample on their own data, a useful template. This is where lead magnets do their work. Ask for something small in return, such as a reply or a short call.

##### Many touches and follow-ups

Most replies come after the first message, not from it. People are busy, miss emails and need several reminders. Plan a sequence of follow-ups over days and weeks, each one short and each one adding a new reason to reply. You can also mix channels: an email, then a connection request, then a call.

##### Automate after it works

Do it by hand first. When you know which list, message and offer get replies, automate the repetitive parts: sending, follow-up scheduling, tracking. Automating a message that does not work only spreads the failure faster. Keep the tone personal even when software sends it.

##### Channel options

| Channel | Strength | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Email | Cheap, scalable, easy to automate | Deliverability, spam filters |
| LinkedIn DM | Strong for B2B, profile adds credibility | Platform limits on volume |
| Phone | Fast feedback, real conversation | Time per contact, rejection |
| SMS | High open rates | Strict rules, feels intrusive if cold |

Regulations matter. In the EU, GDPR and national rules on electronic marketing apply, and they are stricter for consumers than for businesses. Know the rules for your country and channel before you send, offer an easy way to opt out, and keep a record of where each contact came from.

##### Deliverability basics for email

Use a separate sending domain so your main domain stays safe. Set up the standard authentication records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC). Warm up new inboxes gradually. Keep volume per inbox moderate, clean your list to avoid bounces, and write plain messages without heavy links or images.

##### Volume and testing

Cold outreach is a numbers game. Track sent, replies, engaged leads and booked calls. Change one thing at a time (list, first line, offer, follow-up) and keep what wins.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### ERP data consultant targeting manufacturers (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** 200 identical emails to a bought list: "We offer BI and ERP reporting services. Can we schedule a meeting?" No follow-ups. Two replies, both "unsubscribe".
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** A cleaned list of production managers at manufacturers with 50 to 300 employees. First line mentions their ERP system or a recent expansion. Offer: "I'll calculate how many hours your planners lose to manual reports, free, in 20 minutes on a call." Four follow-ups over three weeks.
</div>
</div>

With 50 emails per working day for 20 days, that is 1,000 contacts a month. At a 3% reply rate he gets 30 replies, and if a third of those book a call, 10 calls a month. The specific list, the personal first line and a free result make replying worth it, and the follow-ups catch everyone who missed the first message.

##### Plumber reaching property managers (local business)

A plumbing company builds a list of 80 local property managers and building administrators. The owner calls each one, mentions a building they manage, and offers a free inspection of shared pipes in one building. Every manager who says "not now" gets a short email and a call again a month later. Calls are slow, but each signed manager brings recurring work across several buildings.

#### Common mistakes

- **Selling in the first message.** A stranger who gets "buy now" ignores it. Offer value first and earn a conversation.
- **Wrong list.** Perfect copy sent to the wrong people still fails. Most cold outreach problems start with the list.
- **Giving up after one message.** Without follow-ups you leave most of your replies on the table.
- **Automating too early.** Scaling an untested message spreads a bad result and can damage your sending domain.
- **Ignoring rules and deliverability.** Breaking privacy rules or landing in spam can end the channel before it starts.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He fixes the list first.** If replies are low, he checks whether the right people are being contacted before touching the copy.
- **He makes the offer so good that ignoring it feels like a loss.** A big, fast, free result beats a request for a meeting.
- **He expects most results from follow-ups.** He plans several touches before the first message goes out.
- **He does it by hand, then automates, then hires.** He proves the message works himself, then uses software and people to scale it.
- **He treats it as volume plus testing.** He sets a daily number of contacts, sticks to it, and improves one variable at a time.
- **He uses cold outreach to buy speed when warm contacts run out,** and he keeps the personal tone even at high volume.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who exactly is on the list, and how did you choose them? What do you offer in the first message that is worth a reply? How many touches does each person get? How many messages do you send per day, and what is the reply rate? What did you change last week, and what happened?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing before you send anything.

1. **List:** Describe your ideal contact in one sentence. Where will you find or buy 500 of them?
2. **Personalisation:** Which one or two facts can you find for each contact in under two minutes?
3. **Offer:** What big, fast, free result can you give in the first message?
4. **Sequence:** Write the first message and three follow-ups. What new reason to reply does each one add?
5. **Rules:** Which privacy and marketing rules apply to your country and channel, and how will you handle opt-outs?
6. **Numbers:** How many contacts per day will you commit to for the next 30 days?

## Paid Ads

- id: paid-ads
- book: $100M Leads / The Core Four
- original term: Paid Ads
- key: Ad = Callout + Value + Call to Action. Start small, find a winner, scale.
- related: core-four, lead-magnets, client-financed-acquisition, engaged-leads, money-models

Paying a platform to show your message to many strangers at once. The fastest way to scale leads, but only once your offer works and your money model can pay back the cost of each customer.

#### The gist

Paid ads are the Core Four channel where you reach many people who do not know you, by paying a platform for their attention. Of the four channels it scales the fastest: once you find an ad that works, more money buys more leads. It is also the channel where mistakes cost the most, because a bad ad simply burns budget faster.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Ad** = **Callout** (who it is for) + **Value** (what they get) + **Call to Action** (what to do now)
</div>

An ad is a hook attached to an offer. The hook gets the right stranger to stop. The offer, often a lead magnet, gives them a reason to act. Success is measured in money: what one engaged lead costs, what one customer costs, and whether your money model earns that back fast enough to keep buying more.

#### How it works

##### Callout: tell the right people it is for them

The callout makes the right person feel addressed. It can name a group ("production managers in metalworking"), a situation ("still building month-end reports in Excel?"), a place ("dog owners in Ljubljana") or a desire. Strangers scroll fast, so the callout has to work in the first line or the first seconds. A clear callout also filters out the wrong people, which keeps your cost per engaged lead honest.

##### Value: what they get

Say what they get if they respond, in the result they want. This is usually a lead magnet or an attraction offer: a free audit, a guide, a trial, a calculator, a low-priced first step. The stronger the value relative to what you ask, the cheaper your leads.

##### Call to Action: what to do now

Tell them exactly what to do next: book a call, download the guide, claim the offer, send a message. One action per ad. Reasons to act now, such as limited spots or a deadline, help when they are real.

##### Ads are hooks plus offers

The same Hook, Retain, Reward thinking as in free content applies. Most of the work is testing hooks: headlines, images, the first seconds of video. A strong hook on a weak offer gets clicks and no leads. A strong offer with a weak hook never gets seen. Test both.

##### Start small, find a winner, scale

1. Start with a small budget you can afford to lose while learning.
2. Run several variants of hook and creative against the same offer.
3. Track cost per engaged lead, then cost per customer.
4. Turn off losers, keep winners, and make new variants of the winners.
5. Raise spend on what works step by step, and watch whether cost per customer holds.

Expect most ads to lose. You are looking for the few that win.

##### Measure what matters

Track the full chain: spend, engaged leads, booked calls, customers, and the gross profit each customer brings. Cost per click is a means; cost to acquire a customer (CAC) is what counts. Platform costs vary by market, audience and season, so measure your own numbers instead of relying on general benchmarks.

##### Can your money model pay for it?

Ads work long term only if each customer earns back what it cost to get them. The best case is client-financed acquisition: the customer's early purchases pay for the ad spend that found them, so you can keep spending without running out of cash. Alex's rule of thumb in Money Models is that profit in the first 30 days should be at least twice the cost to acquire the customer. If your money model does not recover CAC quickly, fix the offer and the upsells before you raise the budget.

##### Speed to lead

Strangers from ads are cold and distracted. Contact them fast after they respond, and have a follow-up sequence ready. Slow follow-up wastes leads you already paid for.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### SaaS for production planning (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A LinkedIn ad with the company logo: "Smart planning software for modern manufacturing. Learn more." One version, a large monthly budget from day one. Plenty of clicks, almost no demos.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** Five variants on a small budget, each with a different callout: "Planners in metalworking: how many hours did rescheduled orders cost you last month?" Value: a free calculator that shows lost hours using their own order numbers. Call to action: "Get your number in 5 minutes." After two weeks the best variant gets its budget raised, the rest are turned off.
</div>
</div>

Suppose the winning variant brings an engaged lead for 40 €, one in ten leads becomes a customer, so CAC is 400 €. If a customer pays 300 € setup plus 200 € per month and delivery costs are close to zero, the first 30 days bring about 500 € of gross profit. That covers CAC once, not twice, so before scaling the team adds a paid onboarding package to push 30-day gross profit past 800 €.

| Part | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Callout | "Dental clinic Smile" | "Parents in Kranj with kids aged 6 to 12" |

#### Common mistakes

- **Scaling before you have a winner.** A big budget on an untested ad buys expensive lessons. Find the winner small first.
- **Running ads before the offer works.** If you cannot convert warm leads, cold paid leads will convert worse.
- **Measuring clicks instead of customers.** Cheap clicks that never buy are expensive. Track cost per engaged lead and per customer.
- **No callout.** An ad for everyone is an ad for no one. The right people do not stop.
- **Ignoring payback.** If customers take a year to repay CAC, ads drain cash. Fix the money model before adding spend.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He wants a proven offer before paid traffic.** He gets results with warm outreach or content first, then pours money on what already converts.
- **He tests many hooks against one offer.** He expects most creatives to fail and treats finding the winners as the job.
- **He judges ads on customer economics.** His question is whether the customer pays back the ad cost fast, ideally inside the first 30 days.
- **He fixes the money model to afford more ads.** Upsells and a stronger front-end offer let him outspend competitors on acquisition.
- **He scales winners step by step,** watching cost per customer, and cuts anything that does not pay back.
- **He insists on fast follow-up,** because a paid lead that waits goes cold.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who is the ad for, and does the first line say so? What do they get for responding? What does one engaged lead cost, and what does one customer cost? How much gross profit does a customer bring in the first 30 days? How many variants have you tested, and which one won?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing before you spend the first euro.

1. **Callout:** Who exactly is this ad for? Write three different first lines that would make them stop.
2. **Value:** What lead magnet or attraction offer will they get for responding?
3. **Action:** What is the single next step, and how fast can you follow up after it?
4. **Budget:** What small test budget can you afford to lose while you find a winner?
5. **Economics:** What is the most you can pay for one customer, given their 30-day gross profit?
6. **Proof:** Has this offer already converted with warm leads? If not, test it there first.

## Lead Getters

- id: lead-getters
- book: $100M Leads / Lead getters
- original term: Lead Getters
- key: Customers refer, employees run the Core Four, agencies teach, affiliates lend their audience
- related: core-four, warm-outreach, lead-magnets, rule-of-100, constraint

Other people who bring you leads: customers, employees, agencies, and affiliates and partners. They turn the Core Four from something you do into something that runs without your time per lead.

#### The gist

You can run the Core Four yourself: warm outreach, free content, cold outreach and paid ads. But your time is limited, and at some point you are the bottleneck. Lead getters are the people who do the advertising for you or send you their audience. They are how a business keeps getting leads when the founder stops doing it all.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Lead getters** = **Customers** (referrals) + **Employees** (run the Core Four) + **Agencies** (rent skills) + **Affiliates & partners** (other people's audiences)
</div>

Each type needs a different incentive and a different setup. Customers refer when they got great results and it is easy to do. Employees need a process they can follow. Agencies are best used to learn a skill. Affiliates and partners need something that helps their audience and a fair reward for sending it to you.

#### How it works

##### Customers: referrals

Referred leads arrive with trust borrowed from someone they know, so they tend to buy faster and stay longer. The foundation is results: customers talk about what impressed them. A referral programme cannot save an average service. Once delivery is strong, use these levers:

- **Ask.** Most happy customers never refer because nobody asked. Ask at the moment they feel the win, such as right after a first result or a good review.
- **Make it easy.** Give them a ready message, a link, or a voucher they can forward in one step.
- **Reward.** Offer something to the referrer, the new customer, or both. It can be money, a discount, a bonus, or public recognition.
- **Deliver great results.** This keeps the loop going. Each new customer who gets results becomes a source of the next referral.

##### Employees

Employees can run the same Core Four activities you do: send outreach, make content, run ads. The condition is that you have a process. The usual path is to do the work yourself first and write it down, then show them, then watch them do it, and finally let them run it while you track the numbers. Measure activity and results (contacts made, engaged leads, calls booked), not effort. If you have never done the activity yourself, you cannot train or judge the person doing it.

##### Agencies

An agency is a way to rent skills you do not have yet, such as running paid ads or high-volume cold email. Alex's view is that the best use of an agency is to learn from it. Ask them to explain what they do and why. Keep the ad accounts, data and assets in your own name. Over time, bring the skill in-house with an employee who learned from the agency. An agency that refuses to show you what it does keeps you dependent.

##### Affiliates and partners

These are people or businesses who already have your ideal customers but do not sell what you sell. Examples: an accountant for small firms, an ERP implementer, a software vendor, an industry association, an influencer in your niche. They put their reputation on the line when they recommend you, so give them:

- something that helps their audience, such as your lead magnet, a free workshop or a joint webinar
- a clear reward, usually a commission per lead or per sale
- ready-made materials: copy, links, a short description of who is a good fit
- clean tracking, so everyone knows which lead came from whom

Start with a few partners who fit well and make them successful before adding more.

##### When to add lead getters

Lead getters multiply what already works. Get leads yourself first, so you know which message and offer convert. Then hand that proven system to others. Adding lead getters to a broken offer just spreads the problem.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### BI consultant who can no longer prospect alone (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** Every new client comes from his own calls and the odd LinkedIn post. When projects pile up, prospecting stops. When they end, the pipeline is empty.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** After each successful dashboard go-live he asks the client for one intro to a peer and hands over a ready email. Two ERP implementers offer their clients his free data review and get a commission on every signed project. A part-time assistant runs his cold outreach from a documented script.
</div>
</div>

Leads now come from three sources that do not need his time per contact. Referrals use the moment of satisfaction, partners lend their trust, and the assistant runs a process he already proved himself.

##### Physiotherapy clinic (local business)

| Lead getter | How they activate it |
|---|---|
| Customers | After the last session the patient gets two "first assessment for a friend" vouchers. When one is used, the patient gets a free check-up. |
| Employees | The receptionist calls past patients with a short "how is the knee?" script and tracks calls and bookings. |
| Partners | Two local gyms and a running club get a free injury-prevention talk for their members. |
| Agency | A local agency runs the clinic's ads for six months and trains the receptionist on the setup. |

None of this needs a big budget. Each step is ready to use: voucher, script, talk. People refer when it is easy and when it helps someone they care about.

#### Common mistakes

- **Reward before results.** A referral bonus does not fix average service. If customers are not impressed, no discount will make them risk their reputation.
- **Delegating what you never did.** If you do not know what good outreach looks like, you cannot teach it or check it. Do it first, then hand it over.
- **The agency as a black box.** Without access to data and explanations you stay dependent, and when the agency leaves, the learning leaves with it.
- **Giving partners only a commission.** Partners protect their relationships. They need something their audience values and tools that make sending leads easy.
- **Never asking.** Most customers would refer if asked at the right moment. Not asking leaves the cheapest leads unused.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He makes delivery referral-worthy first.** He sees referrals as a result of great outcomes, and fixes the product before building a referral programme.
- **He builds the ask into the process.** Asking for referrals happens at a fixed point, right after the customer feels the win, every time.
- **He does it, documents it, then delegates it.** No activity goes to an employee before he has proven it works and written down how.
- **He hires agencies to learn, then brings skills in-house.** He keeps accounts and data in his own name and treats the agency fee as tuition.
- **He goes where the audience already is.** Partners and affiliates with his ideal customers are the fastest way to reach many people who will trust him.
- **He judges every lead getter on numbers.** Engaged leads, customers and cost per customer by source, so he knows which to grow.
- **Questions he would ask:** Do your customers get results good enough to talk about? When do you ask for referrals, and what do you hand them? Which lead-getting activity have you proven and documented well enough to hand over? What are you learning from your agency? Who already has your customers, and what would help their audience?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing and note one step per source for this week.

1. **Customers:** At what moment is your client happiest? What exactly will you hand them then to make referring easy?
2. **Employees:** Which lead-getting activity do you do yourself that you could document and hand over in the next 30 days?
3. **Agencies:** Which skill are you missing, and what do you want to learn from an agency, not just buy?
4. **Partners:** List three businesses that already serve your ideal clients. What could you offer their clients for free?
5. **Reward:** What will each type of lead getter get for sending you a lead, and how will you track it?

## The Rule of 100

- id: rule-of-100
- book: $100M Leads / Getting started
- original term: Rule of 100
- key: 100 primary actions a day × 100 days, done first, tracked daily
- related: core-four, more-better-new, warm-outreach, cold-outreach, constraint

Do 100 primary lead-getting actions every day for 100 days. Early on, volume teaches you more than talent or planning ever will.

#### The gist

Most new businesses don't fail because they picked the wrong channel. They fail because they never did enough of anything to find out. The Rule of 100 fixes that with a simple commitment: every day, for 100 days, do 100 of your primary lead-getting actions.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**100 primary actions a day** × **100 days**, done **first**, tracked **daily**
</div>

What counts as "100" depends on the channel. In outreach it is 100 reach-outs. In content it is 100 minutes spent making and posting. In paid ads it is commonly described as $100 a day. The number is a rule of thumb. It is big enough to produce real data and concrete enough that you can't negotiate your way out of it.

#### How it works

##### Pick one channel from the Core Four

The Core Four are the ways to let people know about your stuff: warm outreach, posting free content, cold outreach and paid ads. Choose one to start. Warm outreach is usually the easiest first step because the people already know you. Cold outreach and ads cost more effort or money but scale further. Pick the one you can do today with the time and money you have.

##### Translate "100" into a countable action

The action has to be something you can count at the end of the day without arguing with yourself.

| Channel | Your daily 100 |
|---|---|
| Warm outreach | 100 personal reach-outs to people you know |
| Cold outreach | 100 personalised reach-outs to strangers on a list |
| Free content | 100 minutes making and posting content |
| Paid ads | About $100 (or €100) a day in ad spend |

Count actions you control, such as messages sent or minutes worked. Don't count replies or sales. Those are results, and early on they are too small to steer by.

##### Do it first, every day, for 100 days

The 100 actions happen before email, delivery, admin and everything else. If lead-getting gets the leftover time, it doesn't happen. One hundred days matters because results in any channel lag. The first weeks are mostly noise and learning. Quitting after two bad weeks means you pay the full cost of learning and never collect the benefit.

##### Volume beats talent, early on

At the start you don't know which message, headline, offer or audience works, and you can't work it out from theory. Volume gives you two things at once. It gives you enough attempts to see patterns, and enough repetitions to get better at the skill itself. Someone who sends a thousand average messages learns more than someone who polished ten perfect ones. Skill comes out of volume.

##### Track every day

The real purpose of the 100 days is data. Without numbers you can't tell whether your problem is too little activity or poor conversion. Keep a simple sheet with at least:

1. **Actions** done (messages, minutes, money spent).
2. **Responses** (replies, comments, clicks).
3. **Engaged leads** (people who show interest in your offer).
4. **Calls or meetings** booked, and **sales**, reviewed weekly.

After 100 days you know your numbers for that channel. That knowledge is what lets you grow it on purpose, using More, Better, New.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik analytics consultant (B2B, cold outreach)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A LinkedIn post when there's time, a newsletter once a month, a few cold emails on quiet days and a small Google Ads test. A bit of everything, nothing measured. After three months nobody can say what works.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** Cold outreach only. Every morning before 10:00, 100 personalised messages to finance and operations managers at manufacturers, offering a free review of one manual Excel report. Every number logged in one sheet.
</div>
</div>

| Week | Messages | Replies | Calls | Sales |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 500 | 15 (3%) | 3 | 0 |
| 2 | 500 | 20 (4%) | 5 | 1 |
| 3 | 500 | 35 (7%) | 8 | 2 |

Volume stays fixed at 100 a day, five days a week, so any change in replies comes from the messages themselves. The reply rate more than doubles in three weeks because the consultant had enough attempts to see which first lines worked. At ten messages a week, the same 1,500 data points would have taken almost three years.

##### Dental practice (local business, paid ads)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** The practice runs a whitening ad for a week, sees an expensive cost per booking and switches it off. Next month it tries a different platform for a week. Same result.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** €100 a day for 100 days on one platform, five ad variations, weekly review of cost per lead and show-up rate. Week one is expensive, and they keep going.
</div>
</div>

Over 100 days they spend €10,000. In week one cost per lead is €50 (€700 for 14 leads). By week six one patient-video ad brings leads at €25, half the starting cost. They only found it because they kept running long enough for the winner to show up.

#### Common mistakes

- **Quitting after two weeks.** Switching channels at the first bad result keeps you a beginner everywhere. Every switch restarts the learning curve.
- **Perfecting instead of doing.** Hours spent polishing one message or video feel productive but produce one data point. Early on, a hundred sent beats one perfect.
- **Doing it with leftover time.** If the 100 happens after everything else, it stops happening by week two. Lead-getting goes first.
- **Counting results instead of actions.** "I'll stop when I get five calls" gives you nothing to control. Count what you did.
- **Not tracking.** Without numbers you can't tell a volume problem from a conversion problem, so you won't know what to fix next.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He diagnoses volume before anything else.** When someone says a channel doesn't work, his first move is to ask how much they actually did. Usually the answer is far too little to judge.
- **He insists on one channel at a time.** Spreading effort across four channels at low volume means none of them gets enough attempts to work.
- **He makes it non-negotiable and first.** The lead-getting block goes at the start of the day and is protected like a client meeting.
- **He treats the first 100 days as paid learning.** Poor early results are expected. The goal of this phase is enough data to see what works.
- **He would rather see bad work at high volume than good work at low volume.** Volume can be improved later. Low volume gives you nothing to improve.
- **He wants the numbers written down daily,** because the next step (More, Better or New) depends entirely on them.
- **Questions he would ask:** How many reach-outs did you do yesterday, exactly? What's your daily 100, and when in the day does it happen? Which one channel are you committing to? What do your numbers show after the first two weeks? What would you drop to make room for this?

#### Worksheet

Write your 100-day plan and set up the tracking sheet before you start.

1. **Channel:** Which one Core Four channel will you commit to for the next 100 days, and why that one?
2. **Your 100:** What exactly is the daily action, and how do you count it (messages, minutes, euros)?
3. **Schedule:** At what time does it happen every day, before anything else? What will you drop to make that time?
4. **Tracking:** Which 3 to 5 numbers will you log daily and weekly, and where?
5. **Review:** On which dates will you review the numbers (for example days 14, 30, 60 and 100), and what will you look for each time?

## More, Better, New

- id: more-better-new
- book: $100M Leads / Getting started
- original term: More, Better, New
- key: More → Better → New, in that order
- related: rule-of-100, core-four, constraint, lead-getters, paid-ads

The order in which to grow lead flow. Do more of what works, then make it work better, and only then add something new.

#### The gist

Once a channel starts producing leads, there are three ways to get more out of it. You can do **more** of it, do it **better**, or try something **new**. The order matters. Each step is more expensive and less certain than the one before, so you only move on when the previous one has stopped paying.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**More** (raise volume) → **Better** (raise conversion) → **New** (add channels, platforms, offers)
</div>

Most founders get the order backwards. They get bored with what works and jump to a new platform, a new funnel or a new offer, which puts them back at zero. Shiny new things feel like progress. Usually they are a way to avoid the repetitive work that is actually producing results.

#### How it works

##### More: do more of what already works

**What.** Raise the volume of the activity that is producing leads. From 100 reach-outs a day to 200. From one video a week to three. A higher ad budget on the ad that already converts.

**Why first.** It is the cheapest and most predictable growth. You are repeating something already proven, so there is little new to learn and little risk.

**How.** Increase in steps and watch whether results per unit hold, such as replies per 100 messages or cost per lead. Keep going until one of these happens:

1. Results per unit drop noticeably (you are running out of good prospects, or the audience is saturated).
2. You run out of your own time, and the next step is hiring or delegating to keep volume growing.
3. You hit a real limit of the platform or the market.

Getting help to keep doing more (an assistant who builds lists, a second person sending messages, an editor for your content) still counts as More.

##### Better: improve conversion at each step

**What.** Get more out of the same volume by improving each step of the path from first contact to sale.

**Why second.** Better is harder than More. It takes testing and patience, and many tests fail. But once volume is at its limit, conversion is where the growth is.

**How.** Map the steps and their rates, then improve the weakest one.

| Step | Typical metric |
|---|---|
| Contact → response | Reply rate, click rate, hook retention |
| Response → engaged lead | Share who show interest in the offer |
| Engaged lead → booked call | Booking rate |
| Booked call → showed up | Show rate |
| Call → sale | Close rate |

Change one thing at a time: the first line, the offer, the hook, the audience, the reminder sequence. Compare it with the previous version on a large enough sample. Keep the winner and test the next change. Because the steps multiply, a modest gain at two or three steps compounds into a large gain at the end.

##### New: only then add something new

**What.** A new channel from the Core Four, a new platform within the same channel, a new audience or a new lead magnet or offer.

**Why last.** New means starting over. You face a new learning curve, a new period of weak results and a new set of things to track. It also splits your attention from the channel that already pays.

**How.** Treat it as a fresh start. Apply the Rule of 100 to the new channel, then run More, Better, New on it as well. Keep the existing channel running while you do. It is what funds the experiment.

##### Why the order protects you

Each step produces the information and cash the next step needs. More gives you volume and data. Better uses that data to raise conversion. The cash and skill from both pay for New. Skip a step and you are guessing with less data and less money.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### AI automation consultant for manufacturers (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** Cold outreach to production managers works: 100 messages a day bring about 5 calls a week. After a month he gets restless and starts planning a podcast, a webinar series and a LinkedIn ad campaign at once.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** More: an assistant builds lists and volume goes to 200 a day, bringing about 10 calls a week. Better: he tests first lines and finds that naming the prospect's ERP system doubles replies. New: after four months, with client stories in hand, he starts posting case studies on LinkedIn.
</div>
</div>

More doubled calls with no new skills. Better doubled replies on top of that. By the time he added content, he had stories worth posting and a channel paying for the time. Starting the podcast in month two would have put all three at risk.

##### Dental practice (local business)

| Phase | What they do | Result |
|---|---|---|
| More | The whitening ad that works goes from €100 to €200 a day | Leads double at a similar cost per lead |
| Better | A text reminder the day before each appointment | Show-ups rise from 60% to 80% |
| New | Only now they test ads for dental implants | A second offer, built on what they learned |

The Better step had nothing to do with the ad. Lifting show-ups from 60% to 80% means a third more patients from the same leads (80 ÷ 60 ≈ 1.33). That is just as much a conversion gain as a better headline.

#### Common mistakes

- **Jumping to New because you're bored.** Repeating what works feels dull. That dullness is usually a sign it's working, not a reason to stop.
- **Optimising before you have volume.** Testing headlines on 30 messages is noise. Better only works once More has given you enough data.
- **Stopping More too early.** Most people quit raising volume long before results per unit actually drop. Check the numbers before deciding you've hit the ceiling.
- **Changing many things at once.** If you change offer, copy and audience together, you can't tell what helped, and you can't repeat it.
- **Dropping the old channel for the new one.** The working channel funds the experiment. Turn it off and the new channel has to pay for itself from day one.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **His default answer to "what should I do next?" is more.** He first asks whether the current activity can simply be doubled, and usually it can.
- **He sees boredom as the enemy of growth.** Many owners abandon what works because it's repetitive. He keeps the boring thing going and often scales it further.
- **He moves to Better only when volume stalls,** and then works on the weakest step in the funnel, one change at a time.
- **He is sceptical of new platforms, funnels and offers** while an existing channel still has room to grow. New is a last resort, not a first idea.
- **He buys volume with people before he buys novelty.** Hiring someone to do more of the proven work comes before adding a channel.
- **When he does go New, he restarts the Rule of 100** on it and keeps the old channel running.
- **Questions he would ask:** What's working right now? Can you do twice as much of it next month? What happens to results per unit as you scale? Which step in your funnel converts worst? What's the one change you'll test there first? Why do you want to add something new, and what will it cost the channel that works?

#### Worksheet

Use your last 30 days of numbers. Where you have none, start with the Rule of 100 first.

1. **What works:** Which one activity brings most of your leads today, and what are its numbers per 100 actions?
2. **More:** What would it take to double that volume next month (time, money, an assistant)? What would make you stop?
3. **Funnel:** Write down each step from contact to sale with its conversion rate. Which is the weakest?
4. **Better:** What single change will you test on the weakest step, and how large a sample do you need before you decide?
5. **New:** What new thing are you tempted by? Write down what must be true about More and Better before you're allowed to start it.

# $100M Money Models — How to make money

**Make more money from each customer, faster, so the customer pays for your growth.**

### The book in one page {#overview}

Most businesses sell one thing at one price and wait months for a new customer to pay back what it cost to acquire them. Growth is then limited by cash in the bank: you can only buy as many customers as you can afford to wait for. *$100M Money Models* is about removing that limit. The book focuses on how you sell to a customer once you have them, and leaves lead generation to *$100M Leads*.

A **money model** is a deliberate sequence of offers around a few core products. When it works, the gross profit an average customer generates in the first 30 days exceeds their acquisition cost. That is **client-financed acquisition**: each customer pays for the next one, so you can spend more on marketing without new capital. The goal is to make more money from each customer, and to make it sooner.

The book follows the order in which a customer meets the offers:

1. **Attraction offers** bring the right customer in at a low or even negative net cost.
2. **Upsell offers** come right after the yes, solving the next problem the first purchase reveals.
3. **Downsell offers** catch the no with a smaller, cheaper or differently structured step.
4. **Continuity offers** bill again and again, smoothing cash flow and raising lifetime value.
5. **Assemble and test** them one at a time around your core offer, measured by 30-day gross profit against CAC.

### How Alex would use this book {#alex}

- He reaches for it when a business has a decent offer and leads but can't afford to scale ads, because payback is too slow.
- He measures CAC and 30-day gross profit per customer first, and judges every change by whether it moves that ratio.
- He keeps the product list short and creates new offers by changing price, terms, bundling and timing.
- He adds one offer at a time, starting with whichever piece closes the biggest gap in the first 30 days.
- He treats covering CAC within 30 days as the threshold, and roughly twice CAC as the point where growth stops being capped by cash (a rule of thumb).

## Money Models

- id: money-models
- book: $100M Money Models / Foundations
- original term: Money Model
- key: Attraction → Upsell → Downsell → Continuity
- related: client-financed-acquisition, attraction-offers, upsell-offers, downsell-offers, continuity-offers

A deliberate sequence of offers designed to make more money from each customer, faster. The business that can afford to spend the most to acquire a customer wins.

#### The gist

Most businesses have one offer and hope the customer comes back. A **money model** is a planned sequence of offers: what you sell first, what you offer right after a yes, what you offer after a no, and what keeps paying every month. Its job is to make more money from each customer, and to make it sooner.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Attraction** (get them in) → **Upsell** (more, right after yes) → **Downsell** (save the sale after no) → **Continuity** (recurring)
</div>

This matters because acquisition is where businesses compete. If each customer is worth more to you in the first weeks, you can spend more to get them than your competitors can. A small, simple business with a strong money model can outspend a much bigger one on ads and sales, and still grow on its own cash.

#### How it works

##### Attraction offers: get the right customers in

**What.** The first thing a new customer buys. Its job is to turn strangers into customers at a low, zero or even negative net cost. Common forms include a free or cheap entry service, a "buy one, get one" offer, a cheaper option placed next to a premium one, or a challenge where the customer earns money back for hitting a result.

**Why.** It lowers the barrier to the first yes. Once someone has bought, the next purchase is far easier.

**How.** Tie it closely to your core product, so continuing feels like the natural next step. An entry offer that attracts bargain hunters fills the funnel with people who never buy anything else.

##### Upsell offers: sell more right after a yes

**What.** An offer made immediately after the customer buys. It solves the next problem the first purchase reveals, or gives them more, better or faster.

**Why.** Right after a purchase, trust is highest and the decision to spend has already been made. A week later the energy is gone.

**How.** Use the logic of "you can't have X without Y." You bought the analysis, now you need it implemented. You bought the training plan, now you need the nutrition plan. Offer one clear next step, not a menu of ten.

##### Downsell offers: save the sale after a no

**What.** When a customer says no, offer a smaller, cheaper or differently paid version.

**Why.** A no is often a no to the price, the scope or the risk, not to the outcome. Without a downsell you lose everyone whose problem was the terms.

**How.** Change something real: less scope, fewer features, a shorter trial, a payment plan. Avoid simply cutting the price of the same thing, because that teaches buyers to say no and punishes those who paid in full.

##### Continuity offers: make it recur

**What.** Anything the customer pays for repeatedly: membership, subscription, maintenance, retainer, ongoing support.

**Why.** It turns one sale into a stream of gross profit and makes revenue predictable. It also gives you a reason to keep delivering value.

**How.** Build it around value that genuinely recurs: new data each month, regular sessions, monthly reviews, upkeep. A bonus for signing up, a discount for committing longer, or a waived setup fee helps people join.

##### Simple scales

A money model is many offers around the **same core product**, not many products. The gym sells the same training as a challenge, a membership and a coaching add-on. The consultant sells the same expertise as a workshop, a project and a retainer. Each new product adds delivery complexity. Each new offer around the core adds money with little extra work. Build the model in order: get the attraction offer working first, then add one piece at a time.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Gym (local business)

Before, the gym sold only a €99 monthly membership with €70 gross profit. Ads cost €150 per new member, so a new member returned about half their acquisition cost in the first month (70 ÷ 150 ≈ 0.47).

After, a sequence of offers within the first 30 days. Expected gross profit = gross profit per buyer × share of customers who take it.

| Offer | Gross profit per buyer | Take rate | Expected per customer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attraction: 4-week challenge, €300 | €240 | 100% | €240 |
| Upsell: nutrition coaching | €150 | 30% | €45 |
| Downsell: meal plan guide (half of the 70% who decline) | €40 | 35% | €14 |
| Continuity: membership, first month | €70 | 50% | €35 |
| **30-day gross profit per customer** | | | **€334** |
| **Acquisition cost (CAC)** | | | **€150** |

Each new customer now brings in about 2.2 times what it cost to acquire them (334 ÷ 150 ≈ 2.2) within a month. The gym can afford to pay more for ads than the gym down the street and still have cash left over.

##### Qlik analytics consultancy (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** One offer: a three-month dashboard project. Sales calls end in yes or no. Those who say no are gone. Those who say yes leave when the project ends.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** Attraction: a paid two-day data review, credited against the project if they sign within 30 days. Upsell: the first dashboard built, offered at the end of the review while the findings are on the screen. Downsell: a single-department pilot or payment in three parts. Continuity: a monthly retainer for data model upkeep, new reports and support.
</div>
</div>

The review pays for much of the sales effort, so a new client no longer depends on one big decision. A no to the project turns into a pilot instead of a lost lead. The retainer turns a one-off project into income that continues after delivery.

#### Common mistakes

- **One offer and hope.** Selling a single thing and waiting for repeat business leaves most of each customer's value on the table.
- **Attraction offers that attract the wrong people.** If the entry offer isn't connected to the core product, you get freebie seekers who never buy again.
- **Upselling at the wrong time.** An upsell sent a week later competes with everything else in their inbox. Make it while the yes is fresh.
- **Treating no as the end.** Without a downsell, every objection about price, scope or risk becomes a lost customer.
- **More products instead of more offers.** Adding new products to increase revenue multiplies delivery work. Package the core product in new ways first.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He looks at the money model before the marketing.** When growth stalls because ads seem too expensive, he first asks how much each customer is worth in the first 30 days. Often the fix is the sequence of offers.
- **He builds it one offer at a time,** starting with the attraction offer. Once that reliably brings in the right customers, he adds the upsell, then the downsell, then continuity.
- **He wants every customer to hear the next offer.** An upsell after every yes and a downsell after every no is a process, not a matter of mood.
- **He keeps it simple.** Many offers around one core product beat a catalogue. He would cut products before he would cut offers.
- **He measures it with 30-day gross profit against CAC,** and aims for the first month to pay for acquiring that customer and ideally two more.
- **He prefers models that pay up front.** Setup fees, prepayments and first-month upsells bring cash in when the business needs it to acquire the next customer.
- **Questions he would ask:** What's your entry offer, and does it lead naturally to your core product? What do you offer right after someone says yes? What happens to the people who say no? What do customers pay for every month? How much gross profit does a new customer bring in the first 30 days, compared with what they cost you?

#### Worksheet

Draw your current sequence of offers, then improve it. For each offer, estimate gross profit per buyer and the share of customers who take it.

1. **Core:** What is your one core product or service that all offers should be built around?
2. **Attraction:** What could you offer that attracts the right customers and is a natural first step to the core?
3. **Upsell and downsell:** What next problem does the first purchase reveal? Why do people most often say no, and what could you change in scope or terms to clear that obstacle?
4. **Continuity:** What value recurs naturally every month in your business, and how could customers pay for it?
5. **Numbers:** Add up the expected 30-day gross profit per new customer and divide by CAC. How far are you from 2×?

## Attraction Offers

- id: attraction-offers
- book: $100M Money Models / The four offer types
- original term: Attraction Offer
- key: A good attraction offer pulls in the right buyer and pays back part or all of its acquisition cost.
- related: money-models, client-financed-acquisition, upsell-offers, lead-magnets, guarantees

The first offer a new customer sees. Its job is to bring the right people in and, ideally, cover what it cost to get them.

#### The gist

An attraction offer is the front door of your money model. It is the thing you advertise, the thing a stranger buys first. Its main job is to turn interest into a first transaction with the right kind of customer, at a cost you can afford.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Attraction offer** = easy first **yes** + filters for the **right buyer** + **recovers acquisition cost** (fully or partly)
</div>

Most businesses advertise their core product at full price and hope. Alex's view is that the first offer should be designed for getting customers, and the money is made by what follows it. A strong attraction offer is either cheap or free to the buyer in a way that still brings cash in, or it is structured so the buyer pays now and earns value back later.

#### How it works

##### What makes a good attraction offer

- **Low friction.** The buyer can say yes quickly, without a big decision.
- **Relevant.** It solves a real piece of the same problem your core offer solves, so the next step feels natural.
- **Self-funding where possible.** It brings in enough gross profit to cover a good part of the cost of acquiring the customer.
- **Filters.** It attracts people who will buy more, and puts off freebie hunters.

##### Win Your Money Back

The customer pays up front and gets their money back, or a credit toward the next purchase, if they hit a defined goal. Example goals: attend every session, complete every task, reach a target number. The buyer feels the offer is risk-free if they do the work. You collect cash now, the people who follow through get results and become your best customers, and crediting the money toward the next purchase turns the refund into a sale.

How to set it up:
1. Pick a goal that tracks the actions that produce results (attendance, homework), not just the outcome.
2. Decide refund or credit. Credit toward the next offer is usually stronger for you.
3. Make the conditions clear and written.

##### Giveaways

You offer a prize worth having, and people enter by giving their contact details. One person wins. Everyone else receives a follow-up offer, often a discount on the same or a similar thing. The prize gathers a large list of interested people, and the consolation offer turns many of them into buyers.

##### Decoy offer

You present two options side by side: a basic, cheap option and a much better premium option. The cheap one exists to make an attractive headline price and to make the premium one look like the obvious choice. Many buyers who came for the cheap price end up choosing the better version once they see the difference.

##### Buy X Get Y Free

The buyer pays for one thing and gets something extra included. The extra is often cheap for you to deliver but feels valuable to the buyer. "Free" is a strong word in advertising, and attaching it to something real makes the main purchase easier.

##### Pay Less Now or Pay More Later

The buyer can start cheaply or for free now, and either pays more later or pays a fee if certain conditions are not met. A typical shape is a free or low-cost trial where the buyer agrees to a fee if they miss the conditions, or a low starting price that rises after an introductory period. This lowers the barrier to starting while protecting you from people who never intended to engage.

##### Choosing between them

| Offer | Best when | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Win Your Money Back | Results depend on the buyer showing up | Vague conditions cause disputes |
| Giveaway | You need a big list fast | Leads are cold until the follow-up offer |
| Decoy | Price shoppers dominate your market | A decoy so bad it looks dishonest |
| Buy X Get Y Free | You have cheap-to-deliver extras | Giving away something that devalues the core |
| Pay Less Now, More Later | Starting is the main barrier | Unclear terms that feel like a trap |

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik reporting audit (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "We offer Qlik development at €90 per hour. Contact us for a quote." Leads trickle in, and each one needs a long sales process.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Reporting audit for €1,500. In two weeks you get a list of your five slowest reports and a plan to fix them. If you start the implementation within 30 days, the full €1,500 is credited toward it."
</div>
</div>

The audit is cheap enough to approve without a board decision, relevant to the core project, and brings in cash that covers most of the sales effort. The credit is a Win Your Money Back variant that pulls buyers into the implementation.

##### Dental clinic (local business)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** "Whitening, €350." The ad competes on price with every clinic in town.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Check-up and cleaning for €49 (decoy, shown first), or our Bright Smile package: check-up, cleaning and whitening for €290, with a second cleaning in six months included free."
</div>
</div>

The €49 option creates a low headline price and brings in people who were not ready to spend more. Next to it, the package looks like better value, and the free second cleaning (Buy X Get Y Free) books the patient's next visit.

##### Bakery (local business)

A bakery runs a giveaway: a year of free bread for one winner, entered by leaving an email. 800 people enter. One wins. The other 799 get an offer for a ten-loaf card at €25 instead of €35. If 10% buy, that is about 80 cards and €2,000 in revenue from one campaign, plus a list of people who already showed interest.

#### Common mistakes

- **Advertising the core offer cold.** A full-price, high-commitment offer is a hard first step for a stranger. Lead with something easier.
- **Attracting the wrong people.** A free thing unrelated to your core offer fills your list with people who will never buy again.
- **Treating the attraction offer as the whole model.** Its job is to start the relationship. Without an upsell, downsell or continuity offer after it, the cheap front end loses money.
- **Unclear conditions.** Money-back goals, trial fees and price rises must be written down and explained up front, or they feel like tricks.
- **A dishonest decoy.** The cheap option must be a real product you will deliver happily.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He designs the first offer to get customers, and makes money on the back end.** He would accept thin profit at the front if what follows covers the acquisition cost quickly.
- **He wants the front end to pay for itself where possible.** His rule of thumb is that the attraction offer plus what follows in the first 30 days should cover the cost of acquiring the customer.
- **He prefers cash now with value back later.** Win Your Money Back and credits toward the next purchase are favourite moves, because they collect money and still feel risk-free.
- **He ties the conditions to the behaviours that create results.** That way the offer filters out uncommitted buyers and produces success stories.
- **He tests one attraction offer at a time** and keeps the one that brings in the best buyers at the lowest net cost, not the one with the most sign-ups.
- **Questions he would ask:** What does it cost you to get one customer today? What is the smallest real result you can sell first? Who do you want to attract, and who do you want to repel? What do buyers purchase next, and how quickly?

#### Worksheet

Answer in writing for your main product or service.

1. **Cost:** What does it cost you today to acquire one new customer?
2. **First step:** What is a small, real result a stranger could buy from you quickly?
3. **Type:** Which of the five attraction offers fits your market best, and why?
4. **Filter:** What condition or price would put off people who will never buy more?
5. **Next step:** What do you offer immediately after the attraction offer, and how much of the acquisition cost does it recover?

## Upsell Offers

- id: upsell-offers
- book: $100M Money Models / The four offer types
- original term: Upsell Offer
- key: Every solution creates a new problem: sell the next solution right after the yes.
- related: money-models, attraction-offers, downsell-offers, problems-solutions, client-financed-acquisition

The offer you make right after someone buys. It solves the next problem the first purchase creates, when trust and buying intent are highest.

#### The gist

An upsell is a second offer made immediately after the first yes. The moment someone buys is the moment they trust you most and are most ready to act. Their wallet is out and their decision is fresh. Waiting a week throws that away.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Upsell** = the **next problem** the first purchase reveals + offered **right after the yes** + framed as what makes the first purchase work
</div>

Good upsells are not random extras. Every solution creates a new problem: you bought a training plan, now you need to eat right; you bought a data warehouse, now you need reports on it. The upsell sells the answer to that next problem. Alex describes four ways to do it: the classic, the menu, the anchor and the rollover upsell.

#### How it works

##### Classic upsell

The logic is "you can't have X without Y". You offer the natural complement to what they just bought, the thing that makes it work or makes it last. The buyer already accepted the problem and the solution, so the complement is an easy next step.

How to do it:
1. List what customers struggle with right after buying your core offer.
2. Package the solution to the most common one.
3. Offer it at the point of sale, framed as completing the first purchase.

##### Menu upsell

You walk the buyer through a menu of add-ons and act like a guide, not a salesperson.
- **Unsell first.** Tell them what they do not need. This builds trust and makes your recommendations credible.
- **Prescribe.** Tell them what you recommend for their situation and why.
- **Offer A or B.** Ask which of two options they prefer, rather than whether they want anything at all.
- **Use the card on file.** If they already paid, ask if you should add it to the same card. This removes a second payment decision.

##### Anchor upsell

You show a premium option first, priced well above the core offer (Alex describes something in the range of five to ten times the base price). The buyer reacts to the high price. After that reaction, the core offer looks reasonable by comparison. Some buyers take the premium option, which makes the anchor profitable on its own. The rest buy the core offer with less price resistance.

The premium option must be real and worth its price. It is a genuine offer for the buyers who want the most.

##### Rollover upsell

You credit what the buyer already spent toward a bigger purchase. "You paid €500 for the starter. Upgrade to the full programme within 14 days and the €500 counts toward it." The buyer does not feel they lose the first payment, so moving up is easier. This also works for customers who bought a while ago and are ready for the next level.

##### When to use which

| Upsell | Best when |
|---|---|
| Classic | There is one obvious next problem |
| Menu | There are several add-ons and buyers vary |
| Anchor | Price resistance is the main objection |
| Rollover | Buyers start small and many could go bigger |

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Qlik Cloud migration (B2B analytics)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** The client signs a fixed-price migration to Qlik Cloud. After go-live the consultant leaves. Three months later the client's reports break after a source change, and they hire someone else.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** At signing: "Once you are in the cloud, someone has to watch the reloads and fix them when sources change. Most clients add our monitoring and support package. Should I add it to the same invoice, quarterly or yearly?"
</div>
</div>

This is a classic upsell (you can't run cloud reports without keeping reloads healthy), offered as a choice between two options with the existing invoice. It solves a problem the migration itself creates.

##### Hair salon (local business)

A client books a cut and colour for €120. At the chair, the stylist says: "You don't need the deep-conditioning treatment, your hair is in good shape. To keep this colour from fading, I'd take the colour-safe shampoo and conditioner. Do you want the set for €38, or just the shampoo for €22? I can add it to your card."

The stylist unsells one item, prescribes another, offers A or B and uses the payment already on file. That is a menu upsell. If 40% of clients take the €38 set, the average ticket rises by about €15 (0.4 × 38 = 15.20).

##### Business coaching (anchor)

The coach first presents a one-year private programme at €30,000. Then: "Most people start with the six-month group programme at €4,000." The group programme now looks modest. Six months later, a rollover offer credits part of the €4,000 toward the private programme.

#### Common mistakes

- **Waiting too long.** An upsell offered weeks later competes with a cooled decision. Offer it at the point of sale or right after.
- **Random add-ons.** An extra that does not solve the next problem feels like a cash grab and lowers trust.
- **Asking open questions.** "Anything else?" invites "no". Offer a specific recommendation or two concrete options.
- **A fake anchor.** If the premium option is not real or not worth its price, buyers sense the trick.
- **Never unselling.** Recommending everything makes every recommendation worthless.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He maps the next problem first.** Before designing an upsell, he asks what goes wrong for the customer right after they buy, and sells the fix to that.
- **He upsells at the moment of the yes.** His default is to make the next offer immediately, while trust and intent are at their peak.
- **He frames it as completing the purchase.** The upsell is positioned as what makes the first purchase succeed, not as a second sale.
- **He removes the second payment decision.** Using the card on file or the same invoice is one of his standard moves.
- **He judges upsells by contribution to 30-day gross profit.** As a rule of thumb, an upsell that many buyers take is often what turns a break-even front end into one that funds growth.
- **Questions he would ask:** What problem shows up right after they buy? What do your best customers buy next, and when? What do you tell them they don't need? What is your most expensive option, and do you show it first?

#### Worksheet

Use your core offer and a list of your last ten customers.

1. **Next problem:** What problem does a customer face in the first weeks after buying from you?
2. **Classic:** What is the "you can't have X without Y" product or service you could offer?
3. **Menu:** Which add-ons would you unsell, and which would you prescribe?
4. **Anchor:** What would a premium option at five to ten times your base price include?
5. **Rollover:** How could past purchases count toward a bigger next step?
6. **Timing:** At which exact moment in your sales process will you present the upsell?

## Downsell Offers

- id: downsell-offers
- book: $100M Money Models / The four offer types
- original term: Downsell Offer
- key: After a no, change the terms, the size or the risk. Do not just cut the price.
- related: money-models, upsell-offers, attraction-offers, continuity-offers, guarantees

What you offer after someone says no. A smaller, cheaper or easier version that removes the real obstacle, so you keep the customer instead of losing them.

#### The gist

A no is rarely a no to the outcome. More often it is a no to the price, the payment timing, the size of the commitment or the risk. A downsell is a second offer, made right after the no, that removes that specific obstacle.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Downsell** = find the **real objection** + offer a version that removes it (**payment terms**, **trial**, or **fewer features**) + stop at the **first yes**
</div>

Without a downsell, everyone who couldn't or wouldn't take the main offer leaves with nothing, and you lose all the money you spent getting them to the table. With one, many of them become customers, and some of them later move up to the full offer.

#### How it works

##### Find the real objection

Ask why, and listen. The answers usually fall into a few groups:
- **Money now.** They want it but can't pay the full amount today.
- **Risk.** They are not sure it will work for them.
- **Size.** It is more than they need right now.

Each needs a different downsell. Lowering the price of the same thing answers none of them well and teaches buyers to wait for discounts.

##### Payment plans

When the problem is cash flow, change when and how they pay, not what they get. Alex describes stepping down through options and stopping as soon as the buyer says yes. A typical sequence:

1. **Pay in full** with a discount or a bonus for paying up front.
2. **Financing or card**, so a third party or their credit card carries the cost.
3. **Half now, half later**, for example half today and half in 30 days.
4. **Check priority**, then **three payments.** Ask how important solving the problem is to them on a scale of 1 to 10. A high score (summaries mention 8 or more) means the problem is real and it is worth offering to split the amount in three.
5. **Spread monthly**, the smallest payment per month over a longer term.

Present one step at a time. Do not show the full list, because people will jump straight to the easiest option.

##### Trials with a penalty

When the problem is risk, let them try before they fully commit. A free or cheap trial comes with conditions, such as attending, completing tasks or using the product, and a fee if they do not meet them or do not cancel in time. The buyer gets low risk, and you get people who actually engage.

##### Feature downsells

When the problem is size, take parts out and lower the price accordingly. Fewer sessions, a group instead of one-to-one, do-it-yourself instead of done-for-you, one department instead of the whole company. The buyer gets a real, smaller product. The full offer stays at its price and stays more valuable.

##### Be transparent about fees

If paying by card or in instalments costs you a fee, say so. Offer the choice: pay the card fee, or pay by bank transfer without it. Clear terms protect trust, and many buyers will pick the cheaper option for you.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### ERP-integration project (B2B manufacturing)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A consultant quotes €24,000 to connect the ERP to production data and build reports. The plant manager says the budget for this year is gone. The deal dies.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** The consultant asks what blocks it. Answer: budget this year. Offer 1: half now, half in the new budget year. Still no. Offer 2: a pilot on one production line for €6,000, with the fee credited if they roll out to the whole plant.
</div>
</div>

The first downsell changed timing. The second removed scope and also lowered risk. The plant gets proof on one line, and the consultant keeps the relationship and a clear path to the full project.

##### Gym (local business)

A gym sells a 12-week programme for €600. For those who say no:

| Step | Offer | Price per payment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Pay in full, nutrition guide included | €600 once |
| 2 | Half now, half in 30 days | €300 × 2 |
| 3 | Three monthly payments | €200 × 3 |
| 4 | 12 weekly payments | €50 × 12 |

All steps total €600, and the gym stops at the first yes. A buyer who refuses every plan is offered a two-week trial for €49, with the €49 credited if they join the full programme.

##### Plumber (local business)

A customer balks at a €3,200 bathroom renovation. The plumber offers the critical part only: replace the leaking pipes and the shower valve for €900 now, and keep the rest of the quote valid for six months. The customer agrees, and the plumber is first in line for the rest.

#### Common mistakes

- **Discounting the same thing.** It punishes those who paid in full and trains buyers to say no first.
- **Showing every option at once.** People jump to the cheapest. Step down one level at a time.
- **Not asking why.** A payment plan does not solve a risk objection, and a trial does not solve a cash objection.
- **Hidden fees and conditions.** Trial penalties and card fees must be stated up front and in writing.
- **Downsells that cost you more to deliver.** A smaller version must have lower delivery cost, or you lose margin on every no.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He never lets a no end the conversation.** His default is to have a downsell ready for every offer in the sequence.
- **He diagnoses before he downsells.** He wants to know whether the objection is money, risk or size, and picks the downsell to match.
- **He changes terms before he changes the product.** Payment plans come first, because the buyer still gets the full offer and you keep full price.
- **He steps down one rung at a time** and stops at the first yes.
- **He removes features rather than cutting price.** As a rule of thumb, a lower price should always come with less delivered.
- **He keeps a path back up.** Credits and pilots give downsold customers a reason to buy the full offer later.
- **Questions he would ask:** What are the top three reasons people say no? What happens to them today after they say no? What is your payment plan sequence? What could you remove and still deliver a real result?

#### Worksheet

Take your main offer and the last ten people who said no.

1. **Reasons:** Sort their objections into money, risk and size. Which is most common?
2. **Payment ladder:** Write your step-down sequence of payment options, from pay in full to monthly.
3. **Trial:** What trial could you offer, and what conditions and fee would make people engage?
4. **Smaller version:** What could you remove to make a real, cheaper product with lower delivery cost?
5. **Way back up:** How would a downsold customer move up to the full offer later?

## Continuity Offers

- id: continuity-offers
- book: $100M Money Models / The four offer types
- original term: Continuity Offer
- key: Get them to join, then get them to stay. Reward commitment, and longer commitments usually churn less.
- related: money-models, client-financed-acquisition, upsell-offers, downsell-offers, bonuses

Offers that bill the customer again and again for ongoing value. They turn one sale into recurring revenue, and the work is as much about keeping customers as getting them to join.

#### The gist

A continuity offer is anything the customer pays for on a recurring basis: a membership, a subscription, a retainer, maintenance, ongoing support. It is the last piece of the money model, and often the one that makes the business stable. Each month that a customer stays adds gross profit without another acquisition cost.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Continuity** = **recurring value** + a reason to **join now** (bonus, discount or waived fee) + a reason to **stay** (commitment and ongoing results)
</div>

Continuity has two problems to solve. First, getting people to sign up for something that never ends feels like a big commitment. Second, people cancel. Alex's three continuity offers address both: they reward the buyer for joining, and they tie the reward to staying.

#### How it works

##### What makes continuity work

- **The value recurs.** Something new or useful happens every billing period: new data, a session, a review, an improvement.
- **The customer feels the loss of leaving.** They would lose access, progress or a benefit.
- **The commitment is rewarded.** Longer commitments get something extra, and short ones are still possible.

##### Continuity bonus

You give a bonus to people who join the recurring offer. The bonus should be valuable, related to the outcome, and ideally something the buyer receives right away. It gives a concrete reason to join today, and a reason to stay if the bonus is unlocked over time or kept only by members.

How to set it up:
1. Pick a bonus that helps them get the result faster.
2. Make it exclusive to members.
3. Give it on joining, or release it in parts to reward staying.

##### Continuity discount

You give a discount for committing longer, often expressed as free months. For example, pay for a year and get some months free. The buyer saves money, and you get cash up front and a customer who has committed for a longer period.

You can also offer the discount only to people who prepay, or apply it as a credit at the end of the term.

##### Waived fee

You charge a setup or joining fee, and waive it for buyers who commit to a minimum term. If they cancel before the term ends, the fee becomes due. The fee is often real: onboarding, setup or equipment costs you money. Waiving it makes joining easy, and the fee on early cancellation discourages people from leaving early.

##### Longer billing reduces churn

As a general pattern, customers on longer billing intervals tend to cancel less than those billed monthly. Every billing event is a moment when the customer reconsiders. Fewer billing events mean fewer moments to quit, and a bigger upfront commitment usually comes with stronger intent. This is why the continuity discount and the waived fee both push toward longer terms.

| Offer | Main effect | Typical use |
|---|---|---|
| Continuity bonus | Reason to join now | Memberships, subscriptions |
| Continuity discount | Longer commitment, cash up front | Annual plans |
| Waived fee | Low barrier with a cost to leaving early | Services with setup cost |

##### Managed analytics service (B2B SaaS/BI)

</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** "Managed analytics at €800 per month. Setup (€2,400) is waived with a 12-month commitment, and due if you cancel earlier. Pay annually and get two months free: €8,000 instead of €9,600. Members also get a quarterly board-ready KPI review."

The waived fee removes the joining barrier, the annual option gives cash up front (12 × €800 = €9,600, minus two months = €8,000), and the quarterly review is a continuity bonus that recurs.

A gym charges €60 per month. New members who sign up today get a free body-composition scan and a nutrition plan (continuity bonus). #### Common mistakes

- **No recurring value.** Charging every month for something delivered once leads to cancellations. The value has to repeat.
- **Discounting without commitment.** A cheaper monthly price with no longer term gives away margin and keeps churn the same.
- **Hiding the terms.** Commitment periods and early-cancellation fees must be clear at signup, or they damage trust and invite disputes.
- **Ignoring retention.** Most of the value of continuity comes from how long people stay. Track churn as closely as signups.
- **Bonuses unrelated to the outcome.** A bonus should help them succeed with the core offer, which also helps them stay.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He looks for continuity in every business.** His default question is what the customer could keep receiving, because recurring revenue makes acquisition cheaper over time.
- **He rewards joining and staying.** He stacks a bonus for joining with a discount or waived fee tied to a commitment.
- **He pushes toward longer terms.** As a rule of thumb, he prefers annual or longer commitments where the market allows, because they bring cash up front and tend to churn less.
- **He uses upfront cash to fund acquisition.** Prepaid continuity is one of the ways a business can pay for new customers with customers' money.
- **He keeps the terms clean and visible.** Fees and commitments are explained up front so nobody feels trapped.
- **Questions he would ask:** What value can you deliver every month? Why do people cancel, and when? What does it cost you to onboard a customer? What would make a customer commit for a year?

#### Worksheet

Answer for your business, even if you sell one-off projects today.

1. **Recurring value:** What could a customer receive from you every month or quarter?
2. **Bonus:** What member-only bonus would help them get results faster?
3. **Discount:** What would you give for a 12-month commitment or prepayment?
4. **Fee:** What setup cost could you waive in exchange for a minimum term?
5. **Churn:** When and why do customers leave today, and what would make them stay?

## Building Your Money Model

- id: build-your-money-model
- book: $100M Money Models / Putting it together
- original term: Money Model
- key: Core offer first, then attraction, upsell, downsell, continuity. Add one at a time until 30-day gross profit ≥ 2 × CAC.
- related: money-models, client-financed-acquisition, attraction-offers, upsell-offers, continuity-offers

How to assemble a money model piece by piece, starting from your core offer, so that each new customer pays for their own acquisition within 30 days.

#### The gist

A money model is the sequence of offers a customer meets from first contact onwards. You don't design it in one sitting. You start with the thing you already sell well, wrap one offer around it, prove it works, and only then add the next.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Core offer** → **Attraction** (gets them in, covers CAC) → **Upsell** (at the yes) → **Downsell** (at the no) → **Continuity** (keeps them)

**Target:** 30-day gross profit per customer ≥ **2 × CAC**
</div>

The yardstick is cash, measured early. What matters is how much gross profit the average new customer produces in the first 30 days compared with what it cost to get them. When that number beats CAC, growth funds itself. When it beats CAC twice, each customer pays for the next two. The aim is a few products sold through many offers. You change how you package and present the products and leave the products themselves mostly alone.

#### How it works

##### Step 1. Start from the core offer

Write down what you deliver best and what it costs you to deliver. Every other offer in the model borrows its credibility from this one. If the core offer is weak, fix it first (see *$100M Offers*). Then measure three numbers you will need for every later decision:

- **CAC:** all marketing and sales cost in a period ÷ new customers in that period. Include your own selling time.
- **Gross profit per sale:** price minus direct delivery cost.
- **30-day gross profit today:** what an average new customer brings in the first month with your current setup.

##### Step 2. Add one attraction offer

The attraction offer exists to bring the right customer in at the lowest net cost. Its first job is to cover as much of CAC as possible on day one. It must lead naturally into the core offer: a paid diagnostic before an implementation, a short challenge before a membership. Pick one form and stop there. Common forms include a low-priced entry product, "buy X get Y", a decoy next to a premium option, or a challenge where people earn their money back by hitting a target.

##### Step 3. Add one upsell at the moment of the yes

The best moment to offer more is right after someone has said yes, because the decision to trust you is already made. A good upsell solves the next problem the first purchase reveals. The audit shows what to fix, so the upsell is fixing it. Make it a natural next step in the same conversation or the same checkout.

##### Step 4. Add a downsell for the no

Many buyers who say no to the upsell are saying no to the price, size or risk. Give them a smaller step: less scope, a payment plan, or a trial. Take something away or change the terms. Cutting the price of the identical thing teaches people to wait for discounts and punishes those who paid in full.

##### Step 5. Add continuity for retention

Continuity is anything that bills again: a retainer, a membership, a subscription. It smooths cash flow and raises lifetime value. Offer it when the customer has just felt a result, typically near the end of the first engagement. A bonus for joining, or a waived setup fee, helps people decide.

##### Step 6. Sequence and timing

Order matters as much as content. Put the offers that collect cash early inside the first 30 days, because that window decides whether you can afford to acquire the next customer. A useful layout:

| When | Offer | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | Attraction | Get in, cover most of CAC |
| Day 0 to 14 | Upsell, then downsell for the no | Push 30-day gross profit past CAC |
| Day 21 to 30 | Continuity | Start recurring revenue before the first result fades |

##### Step 7. Test one piece at a time

Change one offer, run it long enough to get a stable take rate, then decide. If you change two things at once you can't tell which one moved the number. Rule of thumb: get the attraction offer working before adding an upsell, and keep adding pieces only while each one raises 30-day gross profit.

##### Step 8. Keep it simple

More products means more delivery, more training and more ways to fail. Most businesses need only a handful of products. You create new offers by changing price, terms, bundling and timing around the same products.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

#### Common mistakes

- **Building everything at once.** Four new offers launched together give you no idea which one works. Add and test them one by one.
- **Measuring revenue instead of gross profit.** Only gross profit can pay for the next customer. A high-revenue, low-margin offer can look great and still drain cash.
- **Missing the 30-day window.** An upsell sold in month four helps lifetime value but does nothing for funding acquisition today.
- **A downsell that is just a discount.** It trains buyers to say no first. Remove scope or change terms instead.
- **Too many products.** Every new product adds delivery complexity. New offers around existing products are cheaper to test and easier to deliver.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He starts with the 30-day number.** Before discussing any offer, he wants CAC and 30-day gross profit per customer. Everything else is judged by whether it moves that ratio.
- **He fixes the biggest gap first.** If the first sale covers little of CAC, he works on the attraction offer. If the first sale is fine but nothing follows, he adds an upsell. He builds in that order and adds one piece at a time.
- **He puts the upsell where the yes is.** He would move the next offer into the same call, the same checkout or the same readout meeting where the customer just agreed.
- **He would rather collect cash upfront.** Upfront payments, setup fees and first months bundled into the entry offer pull gross profit into the first 30 days.
- **He cuts products and adds offers.** When a business has many products and weak economics, he would trim the product list and package the best few in different ways.
- **He treats the 2× CAC target as the point where growth stops being capped by cash** (a rule of thumb). Below 1×, he would hold back on scaling ads until the model is fixed.
- **Questions he would ask:** What does a customer cost you, all in? How much gross profit do they bring in the first 30 days? What do people buy right after saying yes? What do the ones who say no actually object to? What keeps a customer paying after the first result? Which one offer would you test next?

2. **Attraction:** Which easy first purchase leads naturally into your core offer, and how much of CAC could it cover?

4. **Downsell:** When people say no, is it price, scope or risk? What smaller step removes that objection?

6. **Table:** Build the 30-day table with honest take rates. Which single offer, added next, would move the ratio most?

### This collection in one page {#overview}

This is not a book. Alex has not published a book on these topics, so this collection gathers ideas he teaches repeatedly in his talks, trainings and podcast episodes. They fill the gap the three books leave: what happens once a good offer and a steady flow of leads are in place.

1. **Make the offer and get the leads** (the three books).
2. **Close the conversations** with CLOSER, so leads become clients.
3. **Find the constraint** with the doubling test and your funnel numbers.
4. **Fix only that,** saying no to everything else for now.
5. **Repeat** when the constraint moves.

- He checks the constraint before any other advice, because the right next step depends on where growth is stuck.
- When sales is the constraint, he reviews recorded calls against CLOSER and trains the weakest step.

## The CLOSER Framework

- id: closer
- book: Selling & Scaling / Sales
- original term: CLOSER Framework
- key: Clarify, Label, Overview, Sell the vacation, Explain away concerns, Reinforce
- related: value-equation, grand-slam-offer, guarantees, constraint, charge-what-its-worth

Six steps that turn a sales call into a diagnosis the buyer walks through themselves. Understand first, show the destination second, clear doubts last.

#### The gist

A good sales call is a **diagnosis**. The buyer arrives with a problem, a reason it matters now, and a history of failed attempts to fix it. Your job is to make all three visible to them, then show them where they could be, then help them deal with what still holds them back.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**C**larify why they're here · **L**abel them with a problem · **O**verview past pain · **S**ell the vacation · **E**xplain away concerns · **R**einforce the decision
</div>

The order matters more than any single line. The first three letters are entirely about the buyer. Only once they have confirmed the problem in their own words and admitted that past attempts didn't work does it make sense to talk about your offer. A seller who jumps straight to S is pitching to someone who hasn't yet bought the problem.

#### How it works

##### C: Clarify why they're here

People don't book a call for no reason. Something happened: a lost customer, a new demand from the board, another missed deadline. That trigger drives the whole conversation. Open with a question about it ("What made you reach out now?") and keep asking until you hear the real reason, which is rarely the first polite answer. If the buyer doesn't know why they came, they won't know why they should buy.

##### L: Label them with a problem they confirm

When you have heard enough, sum the problem up in one sentence and end with a question: "So the issue is that you have the data but can't trust it in time for board meetings. Is that right?" The buyer must confirm it. Once they say "yes, exactly", the problem belongs to them. If they correct you, that is useful too: you now have a sharper picture. A label is a mirror you hold up. It is never a diagnosis you push on them.

##### O: Overview past pain

Ask what they have already tried, how long it took, what it cost and why it didn't work. This does three things:

1. The buyer admits, to themselves, that their current approaches won't solve it.
2. You learn what not to offer, because it has already failed.
3. You can separate the problem from blame: the past solution failed because it was the wrong fit, which keeps the buyer's pride intact.

##### S: Sell the vacation, not the plane flight

Nobody books a holiday for the flight. They want the beach. Describe what the buyer's week or month looks like once the problem is solved, in their own words from C and L. Mention the process only as far as it makes the promise believable. Every extra detail about the plane (methodology, meetings, architecture) is another thing that can feel tiring or risky, which raises effort and lowers perceived likelihood in the Value Equation.

##### E: Explain away concerns

Concerns are normal and usually mean "I'm not sure yet". In Alex's teaching they commonly come down to a few groups:

| Group | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| Circumstances | "I don't have time right now", "It's too expensive" |
| Other people | "I need to ask my partner / the director" |
| Self-doubt | "It won't work for us", "I'm not sure I can do it" |

A practical sequence: repeat the concern back, ask whether it is the only thing holding them back, then return to what they told you in C, L and O. Their reason, their problem and their failed attempts are your best arguments, because they are theirs. Don't argue. Arguing makes people defend their position.

##### R: Reinforce the decision

Right after buying, many people feel buyer's remorse. The sale doesn't end at payment. Congratulate them, tell them exactly what happens next and when, and get them to a first contact or a first small win as fast as possible. Reinforcement cuts cancellations and refunds and starts the experience that later produces referrals.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### Call with a manufacturer's CEO (B2B analytics)

<div class="dialogue" markdown="1">
**Seller (Clarify):** What made you get in touch this month in particular?

**Client:** At the last board meeting I couldn't say which customers actually make us money. It was embarrassing.

**Seller (Label):** So the issue isn't a lack of data. You don't get it in time, in a form you trust. Is that right?

**Client:** Exactly. We have numbers, but every department has its own.

**Seller (Overview):** What have you tried so far?

**Client:** Last year we bought a reporting tool. We pay for licences, one person used it, and she's now on maternity leave.

**Seller (Sell):** Picture the next board meeting. You open one page, see margin by customer for last month, and answer every question in seconds without calling accounting.

**Client (concern):** Sounds good, but we'll pay again for something nobody uses.

**Seller (Explain):** That's exactly what happened last year, so it's a fair worry. Is it the only thing holding you back? In the first 30 days we build the report and review it with you twice before the meeting. Nobody on your side has to learn the tool.

**Seller (Reinforce, after signing):** Good decision. Our analyst calls you tomorrow at nine, and you'll have the first version of the report by Friday.
</div>

The concern was self-doubt ("it won't work for us"). The seller didn't argue. He used the client's own story from the Overview step and tied it to a specific part of the offer that removes the effort that caused the last failure.

##### Adult braces consultation (dental clinic)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** Reception explains bracket types, materials, treatment length in months and the price. The patient says she'll think about it and never comes back.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** First the question why now: her sister's wedding in a year. Label: "You don't like smiling in photos, is that right?" Then what she has tried. Then the destination: smiling in the wedding photos. The money concern is handled with instalments, and the clinic calls that same week to confirm the first appointment.
</div>

##### Concerns in quote automation (AI consulting)

| Group | Client says | Possible response |
|---|---|---|
| Circumstances (time) | "We're in the middle of the season." | "The season is when quotes cost you the most hours. How many will you write this spring?" |
| Circumstances (money) | "The budget is spent." | Compare the fee with the monthly cost of manual work they named in Overview. |
| Other people | "I need to ask the director." | "What will the director ask? Let's prepare the answers, or invite them to a short call." |
| Self-doubt | "Our processes are too specific." | "Step one is a working prototype on three of your real enquiries." |

#### Common mistakes

- **Jumping straight to the pitch.** Without C, L and O there is nothing to build on. The buyer hears a solution to a problem they haven't voiced, and everything sounds like cost.
- **Stating the problem yourself.** If the buyer doesn't confirm it in their own words, they don't own it. Every label ends with a question.
- **Selling the plane.** Technical sellers love explaining architecture and methodology. Each detail is a new place for a concern to appear.
- **Arguing with the concern.** It hardens the buyer's position. Isolate it and go back to their own words.
- **Going silent after the signature.** A few days without contact is enough for doubt to grow. Plan the first 48 hours before the call.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He treats the call as the buyer's conversation.** The seller asks most of the questions and the buyer does most of the talking, especially in the first half.
- **He makes the buyer say the problem.** A problem the seller states is an opinion. A problem the buyer confirms is a reason to buy.
- **He uses past pain as leverage.** What they tried and why it failed becomes the case for doing something different now.
- **He keeps the pitch short and outcome-first.** If the offer needs a long explanation, he fixes the offer before he fixes the pitch.
- **He sees most concerns as avoidance of a decision,** and helps the buyer make one, yes or no, instead of leaving it open. As a rule of thumb, a clear no is better than a vague maybe.
- **He reviews recorded calls against the six steps** to find where deals are lost, then trains that step.
- **Questions he would ask:** Why did they reach out now? Did they confirm the problem in their own words? What have they already tried, and why did it fail? Did you describe the destination or the process? Which concern did they raise, and did you isolate it? What happened in the first 48 hours after they paid?

#### Worksheet

Take your last two sales conversations, or one coming up, and answer in writing.

1. **Clarify:** What is your opening question that reveals why the buyer got in touch right now?
2. **Label:** Write the last buyer's problem as one sentence that ends with "is that right?".
3. **Overview:** Which three solutions do your buyers usually try before you, and why don't they work?
4. **Sell:** Describe the vacation of your offer in two sentences without a single technical word.
5. **Explain:** List the concerns you hear most. Which group does each belong to, and what is your response?
6. **Reinforce:** What happens in the first 48 hours after purchase, and what should the buyer feel by the end of them?

## The Constraint

- id: constraint
- book: Selling & Scaling / Scaling
- original term: Constraint (bottleneck)
- key: Find the one constraint, fix it, find the next one. Test: if leads doubled tomorrow, what breaks first?
- related: core-four, closer, money-models, continuity-offers, rule-of-100

At any moment a business has one main constraint that limits its growth. Work on anything else has little effect until that one is fixed, and then the constraint moves.

#### The gist

A business works like a pipe with several sections: leads come in, some become sales, sales become delivered work, and delivered clients stay or leave. Only as much flows through the whole pipe as the **narrowest section** lets through. Widening any other section changes nothing, because the flow still waits at the same point.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**One constraint at a time** → fix it → the constraint **moves** → find the next one
</div>

The idea comes from Eliyahu Goldratt's **Theory of Constraints**, set out in his book *The Goal*. Alex uses it as a simple operating rule for founders: find the one thing that limits growth right now, put your effort there, and say no to good ideas elsewhere until the constraint has moved.

#### How it works

##### There is always one main constraint

A business always has many problems, but usually one of them matters most: the one whose improvement would raise the result of the whole business the most. The common places are:

- **Leads.** Too few of the right people hear about you.
- **Sales conversion.** People come, but they don't buy.
- **Delivery or capacity.** You sell more than you can fulfil well.
- **Retention.** Clients leave too soon (churn).
- **Sometimes cash or people.** Growth stalls on cash flow, or on one key person who is missing or overloaded.

##### Work elsewhere is low leverage

If delivery is the constraint, another ad campaign brings more disappointed clients and longer queues. If retention is the constraint, a new website fills a leaking bucket. This work looks productive but doesn't move the result, and it can make it worse. Hence the rule: say no to anything that isn't the constraint, good ideas included.

##### Find it with a thought experiment

The quickest test is to ask: **"If my leads doubled tomorrow, what would break first?"**

- "Nothing, we'd handle them happily" means leads are the constraint.
- "We wouldn't have time for all the calls" or "most wouldn't buy" means sales.
- "We couldn't deliver on time" means delivery or capacity.
- "We'd sign them, but they'd be gone in a few months" means retention.
- "We couldn't fund the extra work" or "it all goes through me" means cash or people.

##### Confirm it with funnel numbers

Write down the numbers for each step of the last quarter: leads, calls held, new clients, onboarded clients, clients still active after a set period. Calculate the conversion at each step and compare it with a reasonable expectation for your market. The step that falls furthest short, or where work and people pile up in a queue, is the candidate. The thought experiment gives you a hypothesis. The numbers confirm or reject it.

##### Fix it, then it moves

When you remove a constraint, it doesn't disappear. It moves. More leads make sales the new bottleneck. Better sales overload delivery. Better delivery reveals that clients don't stay. That is progress, not failure. Finding the constraint is a standing habit, for example a monthly review, rather than a one-off project.

##### Goldratt's five focusing steps, simplified

1. **Identify** the constraint.
2. **Exploit** it: get the most out of it with what you already have, so it never sits idle.
3. **Subordinate** everything else to it.
4. **Elevate** it with new people, systems or money.
5. **Repeat** once it has moved.

#### Examples

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

##### One quarter's funnel (B2B analytics SaaS)

A small company sells an analytics platform to manufacturers. The founder wants to spend more on ads, but first looks at the numbers.

| Step | Count | Conversion | Expected |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leads | 240 | | |
| Calls held | 150 | 63 % | 60 % |
| New clients | 30 | 20 % | 20 % |
| Onboarded within 30 days | 27 | 90 % | 85 % |
| Active after 6 months | 12 | 44 % | 80 % |

Every step meets expectations except retention. More ads would bring more clients, and the company would lose more than half of them within six months. The constraint is retention. The first move is a monthly usage review with each client and an alert when someone hasn't opened the platform for two weeks. Only once retention rises does it pay to scale ads.

##### Diagnostic table (AI consultancy)

A general aid for locating the constraint. Compare the symptoms with your business.

| Constraint | Symptoms | Number to watch | First move |
|---|---|---|---|
| Leads | Empty calendar, sellers waiting | New qualified leads per week | More volume on one channel |
| Sales | Many calls, few signatures | Close rate | Call reviews, a script, a stronger offer |
| Delivery | Delays, queues, overloaded team | Time to first result | Standardise, automate, hire |
| Retention | Clients leave after a few months | Monthly churn | Fast first win, regular check-ins |
| Cash or people | Growth stalls on cash or one person | Months of runway, owner hours in delivery | Upfront payments, delegation |

An AI consultancy has enough leads and a good close rate, but the founder runs every project personally. The symptoms point to delivery and people. Instead of new campaigns, the founder writes a standard process for the first project and brings in a project lead.

#### Common mistakes

- **Working on everything at once.** Ten small improvements move little, because the constraint gets only a tenth of the attention.
- **Defaulting to "we need more clients".** Leads are the most common answer and often the wrong one. More clients into leaky retention or overloaded delivery does harm.
- **Choosing by gut instead of numbers.** Founders like working on what they enjoy. Without funnel numbers, picking the constraint often means picking a hobby.
- **Forgetting that it moves.** After one constraint is solved, some keep doing the same work. Re-run the test after every improvement.

#### How Alex would apply it

- **He asks what the constraint is before giving any advice.** Most business questions have a different right answer depending on where the bottleneck sits.
- **He runs the doubling test first,** then checks it against the funnel numbers.
- **He puts the owner's time on the constraint** and cuts or pauses projects that don't touch it, even promising ones.
- **He is wary of adding leads to a broken funnel.** Fixing conversion, delivery or retention first makes every future lead worth more.
- **He exploits before he elevates:** squeeze more out of the current team, process and offer before hiring or spending.
- **He expects the constraint to move** and reviews it regularly, treating a new bottleneck as a sign of progress.
- **Questions he would ask:** If leads doubled tomorrow, what breaks first? What are your numbers at each step? Where do people or work queue up? How much of delivery still depends on you? What would you stop doing this month if you took the answer seriously?

#### Worksheet

Take the last three months of your business and answer in writing.

1. **Test:** If your leads doubled tomorrow, what would break first?
2. **Funnel:** Write down the numbers for each step (leads, calls, deals, onboarding, retention). Where is the biggest gap against what you'd expect?
3. **Queues:** Where do work or clients pile up and wait?
4. **Focus:** Which three projects that don't touch the constraint will you pause this month?
5. **Action:** What one action widens the constraint within 30 days, and how will you measure it?
6. **Next:** Where is the constraint likely to move once you fix it?
