## The gist {#gist}

Cold outreach is the Core Four channel where you contact people who do not know you, one at a time. Its big advantage is control: you choose exactly who hears from you, and you are not limited by how many people you already know. Its big drawback is that strangers ignore most messages, so it only works with volume, persistence and an offer that makes replying worth it.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Engaged leads from cold outreach** = **Right people** (list) × **Reason to reply** (personalisation + big fast value) × **Touches** (follow-ups) × **Volume**
</div>

Strangers have no reason to trust you. You earn a reply by showing you did your homework and by offering something useful for free, often a lead magnet. You do not sell in the first message. You get an engaged lead first, and the sale happens later in conversation.

## How it works {#how}

### Build or buy a targeted list

Everything starts with who is on the list. Define your ideal client first: industry, size, role, the problem they have. Then collect contacts that match. You can build a list yourself from public sources, company websites, professional networks and event lists, or buy one from a data provider. Bought lists save time but need checking: remove wrong roles, dead addresses and companies that do not fit. A smaller list of the right people beats a large list of the wrong ones.

### Personalise at scale

A message that could be sent to anyone gets treated like spam. Add one or two details that show it was written for this person: a recent project, a machine or certificate mentioned on their site, a post they wrote, a hire they made. The trick is to do this at scale. Research a few facts per contact in a spreadsheet, then drop them into a template. A rule of thumb: more personal means better replies but fewer messages per hour, so find the balance that still gives you volume.

### Lead with big, fast value

The offer in the first message has to be worth a stranger's time. The best cold offers give a real result quickly and for free: a short audit, a calculation of what a problem costs them, a sample on their own data, a useful template. This is where lead magnets do their work. Ask for something small in return, such as a reply or a short call.

### Many touches and follow-ups

Most replies come after the first message, not from it. People are busy, miss emails and need several reminders. Plan a sequence of follow-ups over days and weeks, each one short and each one adding a new reason to reply. You can also mix channels: an email, then a connection request, then a call.

### Automate after it works

Do it by hand first. When you know which list, message and offer get replies, automate the repetitive parts: sending, follow-up scheduling, tracking. Automating a message that does not work only spreads the failure faster. Keep the tone personal even when software sends it.

### Channel options

| Channel | Strength | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Email | Cheap, scalable, easy to automate | Deliverability, spam filters |
| LinkedIn DM | Strong for B2B, profile adds credibility | Platform limits on volume |
| Phone | Fast feedback, real conversation | Time per contact, rejection |
| SMS | High open rates | Strict rules, feels intrusive if cold |

Regulations matter. In the EU, GDPR and national rules on electronic marketing apply, and they are stricter for consumers than for businesses. Know the rules for your country and channel before you send, offer an easy way to opt out, and keep a record of where each contact came from.

### Deliverability basics for email

Use a separate sending domain so your main domain stays safe. Set up the standard authentication records (SPF, DKIM, DMARC). Warm up new inboxes gradually. Keep volume per inbox moderate, clean your list to avoid bounces, and write plain messages without heavy links or images.

### Volume and testing

Cold outreach is a numbers game. Track sent, replies, engaged leads and booked calls. Change one thing at a time (list, first line, offer, follow-up) and keep what wins.

## Examples {#examples}

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

### ERP data consultant targeting manufacturers (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** 200 identical emails to a bought list: "We offer BI and ERP reporting services. Can we schedule a meeting?" No follow-ups. Two replies, both "unsubscribe".
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** A cleaned list of production managers at manufacturers with 50 to 300 employees. First line mentions their ERP system or a recent expansion. Offer: "I'll calculate how many hours your planners lose to manual reports, free, in 20 minutes on a call." Four follow-ups over three weeks.
</div>
</div>

With 50 emails per working day for 20 days, that is 1,000 contacts a month. At a 3% reply rate he gets 30 replies, and if a third of those book a call, 10 calls a month. The specific list, the personal first line and a free result make replying worth it, and the follow-ups catch everyone who missed the first message.

### Plumber reaching property managers (local business)

A plumbing company builds a list of 80 local property managers and building administrators. The owner calls each one, mentions a building they manage, and offers a free inspection of shared pipes in one building. Every manager who says "not now" gets a short email and a call again a month later. Calls are slow, but each signed manager brings recurring work across several buildings.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Selling in the first message.** A stranger who gets "buy now" ignores it. Offer value first and earn a conversation.
- **Wrong list.** Perfect copy sent to the wrong people still fails. Most cold outreach problems start with the list.
- **Giving up after one message.** Without follow-ups you leave most of your replies on the table.
- **Automating too early.** Scaling an untested message spreads a bad result and can damage your sending domain.
- **Ignoring rules and deliverability.** Breaking privacy rules or landing in spam can end the channel before it starts.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He fixes the list first.** If replies are low, he checks whether the right people are being contacted before touching the copy.
- **He makes the offer so good that ignoring it feels like a loss.** A big, fast, free result beats a request for a meeting.
- **He expects most results from follow-ups.** He plans several touches before the first message goes out.
- **He does it by hand, then automates, then hires.** He proves the message works himself, then uses software and people to scale it.
- **He treats it as volume plus testing.** He sets a daily number of contacts, sticks to it, and improves one variable at a time.
- **He uses cold outreach to buy speed when warm contacts run out,** and he keeps the personal tone even at high volume.
- **Questions he would ask:** Who exactly is on the list, and how did you choose them? What do you offer in the first message that is worth a reply? How many touches does each person get? How many messages do you send per day, and what is the reply rate? What did you change last week, and what happened?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Answer in writing before you send anything.

1. **List:** Describe your ideal contact in one sentence. Where will you find or buy 500 of them?
2. **Personalisation:** Which one or two facts can you find for each contact in under two minutes?
3. **Offer:** What big, fast, free result can you give in the first message?
4. **Sequence:** Write the first message and three follow-ups. What new reason to reply does each one add?
5. **Rules:** Which privacy and marketing rules apply to your country and channel, and how will you handle opt-outs?
6. **Numbers:** How many contacts per day will you commit to for the next 30 days?
