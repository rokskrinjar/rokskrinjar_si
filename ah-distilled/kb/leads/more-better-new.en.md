## The gist {#gist}

Once a channel starts producing leads, there are three ways to get more out of it. You can do **more** of it, do it **better**, or try something **new**. The order matters. Each step is more expensive and less certain than the one before, so you only move on when the previous one has stopped paying.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**More** (raise volume) → **Better** (raise conversion) → **New** (add channels, platforms, offers)
</div>

Most founders get the order backwards. They get bored with what works and jump to a new platform, a new funnel or a new offer, which puts them back at zero. Shiny new things feel like progress. Usually they are a way to avoid the repetitive work that is actually producing results.

## How it works {#how}

### More: do more of what already works

**What.** Raise the volume of the activity that is producing leads. From 100 reach-outs a day to 200. From one video a week to three. A higher ad budget on the ad that already converts.

**Why first.** It is the cheapest and most predictable growth. You are repeating something already proven, so there is little new to learn and little risk.

**How.** Increase in steps and watch whether results per unit hold, such as replies per 100 messages or cost per lead. Keep going until one of these happens:

1. Results per unit drop noticeably (you are running out of good prospects, or the audience is saturated).
2. You run out of your own time, and the next step is hiring or delegating to keep volume growing.
3. You hit a real limit of the platform or the market.

Getting help to keep doing more (an assistant who builds lists, a second person sending messages, an editor for your content) still counts as More.

### Better: improve conversion at each step

**What.** Get more out of the same volume by improving each step of the path from first contact to sale.

**Why second.** Better is harder than More. It takes testing and patience, and many tests fail. But once volume is at its limit, conversion is where the growth is.

**How.** Map the steps and their rates, then improve the weakest one.

| Step | Typical metric |
|---|---|
| Contact → response | Reply rate, click rate, hook retention |
| Response → engaged lead | Share who show interest in the offer |
| Engaged lead → booked call | Booking rate |
| Booked call → showed up | Show rate |
| Call → sale | Close rate |

Change one thing at a time: the first line, the offer, the hook, the audience, the reminder sequence. Compare it with the previous version on a large enough sample. Keep the winner and test the next change. Because the steps multiply, a modest gain at two or three steps compounds into a large gain at the end.

### New: only then add something new

**What.** A new channel from the Core Four, a new platform within the same channel, a new audience or a new lead magnet or offer.

**Why last.** New means starting over. You face a new learning curve, a new period of weak results and a new set of things to track. It also splits your attention from the channel that already pays.

**How.** Treat it as a fresh start. Apply the Rule of 100 to the new channel, then run More, Better, New on it as well. Keep the existing channel running while you do. It is what funds the experiment.

### Why the order protects you

Each step produces the information and cash the next step needs. More gives you volume and data. Better uses that data to raise conversion. The cash and skill from both pay for New. Skip a step and you are guessing with less data and less money.

## Examples {#examples}

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

### AI automation consultant for manufacturers (B2B)

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** Cold outreach to production managers works: 100 messages a day bring about 5 calls a week. After a month he gets restless and starts planning a podcast, a webinar series and a LinkedIn ad campaign at once.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** More: an assistant builds lists and volume goes to 200 a day, bringing about 10 calls a week. Better: he tests first lines and finds that naming the prospect's ERP system doubles replies. New: after four months, with client stories in hand, he starts posting case studies on LinkedIn.
</div>
</div>

More doubled calls with no new skills. Better doubled replies on top of that. By the time he added content, he had stories worth posting and a channel paying for the time. Starting the podcast in month two would have put all three at risk.

### Dental practice (local business)

| Phase | What they do | Result |
|---|---|---|
| More | The whitening ad that works goes from €100 to €200 a day | Leads double at a similar cost per lead |
| Better | A text reminder the day before each appointment | Show-ups rise from 60% to 80% |
| New | Only now they test ads for dental implants | A second offer, built on what they learned |

The Better step had nothing to do with the ad. Lifting show-ups from 60% to 80% means a third more patients from the same leads (80 ÷ 60 ≈ 1.33). That is just as much a conversion gain as a better headline.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Jumping to New because you're bored.** Repeating what works feels dull. That dullness is usually a sign it's working, not a reason to stop.
- **Optimising before you have volume.** Testing headlines on 30 messages is noise. Better only works once More has given you enough data.
- **Stopping More too early.** Most people quit raising volume long before results per unit actually drop. Check the numbers before deciding you've hit the ceiling.
- **Changing many things at once.** If you change offer, copy and audience together, you can't tell what helped, and you can't repeat it.
- **Dropping the old channel for the new one.** The working channel funds the experiment. Turn it off and the new channel has to pay for itself from day one.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **His default answer to "what should I do next?" is more.** He first asks whether the current activity can simply be doubled, and usually it can.
- **He sees boredom as the enemy of growth.** Many owners abandon what works because it's repetitive. He keeps the boring thing going and often scales it further.
- **He moves to Better only when volume stalls,** and then works on the weakest step in the funnel, one change at a time.
- **He is sceptical of new platforms, funnels and offers** while an existing channel still has room to grow. New is a last resort, not a first idea.
- **He buys volume with people before he buys novelty.** Hiring someone to do more of the proven work comes before adding a channel.
- **When he does go New, he restarts the Rule of 100** on it and keeps the old channel running.
- **Questions he would ask:** What's working right now? Can you do twice as much of it next month? What happens to results per unit as you scale? Which step in your funnel converts worst? What's the one change you'll test there first? Why do you want to add something new, and what will it cost the channel that works?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Use your last 30 days of numbers. Where you have none, start with the Rule of 100 first.

1. **What works:** Which one activity brings most of your leads today, and what are its numbers per 100 actions?
2. **More:** What would it take to double that volume next month (time, money, an assistant)? What would make you stop?
3. **Funnel:** Write down each step from contact to sale with its conversion rate. Which is the weakest?
4. **Better:** What single change will you test on the weakest step, and how large a sample do you need before you decide?
5. **New:** What new thing are you tempted by? Write down what must be true about More and Better before you're allowed to start it.
