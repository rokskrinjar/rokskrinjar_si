## The gist {#gist}

A money model is the sequence of offers a customer meets from first contact onwards. You don't design it in one sitting. You start with the thing you already sell well, wrap one offer around it, prove it works, and only then add the next.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Core offer** → **Attraction** (gets them in, covers CAC) → **Upsell** (at the yes) → **Downsell** (at the no) → **Continuity** (keeps them)

**Target:** 30-day gross profit per customer ≥ **2 × CAC**
</div>

The yardstick is cash, measured early. What matters is how much gross profit the average new customer produces in the first 30 days compared with what it cost to get them. When that number beats CAC, growth funds itself. When it beats CAC twice, each customer pays for the next two. The aim is a few products sold through many offers. You change how you package and present the products and leave the products themselves mostly alone.

## How it works {#how}

### Step 1. Start from the core offer

Write down what you deliver best and what it costs you to deliver. Every other offer in the model borrows its credibility from this one. If the core offer is weak, fix it first (see *$100M Offers*). Then measure three numbers you will need for every later decision:

- **CAC:** all marketing and sales cost in a period ÷ new customers in that period. Include your own selling time.
- **Gross profit per sale:** price minus direct delivery cost.
- **30-day gross profit today:** what an average new customer brings in the first month with your current setup.

### Step 2. Add one attraction offer

The attraction offer exists to bring the right customer in at the lowest net cost. Its first job is to cover as much of CAC as possible on day one. It must lead naturally into the core offer: a paid diagnostic before an implementation, a short challenge before a membership. Pick one form and stop there. Common forms include a low-priced entry product, "buy X get Y", a decoy next to a premium option, or a challenge where people earn their money back by hitting a target.

### Step 3. Add one upsell at the moment of the yes

The best moment to offer more is right after someone has said yes, because the decision to trust you is already made. A good upsell solves the next problem the first purchase reveals. The audit shows what to fix, so the upsell is fixing it. Make it a natural next step in the same conversation or the same checkout.

### Step 4. Add a downsell for the no

Many buyers who say no to the upsell are saying no to the price, size or risk. Give them a smaller step: less scope, a payment plan, or a trial. Take something away or change the terms. Cutting the price of the identical thing teaches people to wait for discounts and punishes those who paid in full.

### Step 5. Add continuity for retention

Continuity is anything that bills again: a retainer, a membership, a subscription. It smooths cash flow and raises lifetime value. Offer it when the customer has just felt a result, typically near the end of the first engagement. A bonus for joining, or a waived setup fee, helps people decide.

### Step 6. Sequence and timing

Order matters as much as content. Put the offers that collect cash early inside the first 30 days, because that window decides whether you can afford to acquire the next customer. A useful layout:

| When | Offer | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Day 0 | Attraction | Get in, cover most of CAC |
| Day 0 to 14 | Upsell, then downsell for the no | Push 30-day gross profit past CAC |
| Day 21 to 30 | Continuity | Start recurring revenue before the first result fades |

### Step 7. Test one piece at a time

Change one offer, run it long enough to get a stable take rate, then decide. If you change two things at once you can't tell which one moved the number. Rule of thumb: get the attraction offer working before adding an upsell, and keep adding pieces only while each one raises 30-day gross profit.

### Step 8. Keep it simple

More products means more delivery, more training and more ways to fail. Most businesses need only a handful of products. You create new offers by changing price, terms, bundling and timing around the same products.

## Examples {#examples}

*Examples are invented for illustration.*

Expected gross profit = gross profit per sale × share of new customers who take it. Take rates below are shares of all new customers.

Both models work for the same reason: the first purchase is easy to say yes to, the next offer arrives when trust is highest, and the recurring revenue starts before the first result fades.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Building everything at once.** Four new offers launched together give you no idea which one works. Add and test them one by one.
- **Measuring revenue instead of gross profit.** Only gross profit can pay for the next customer. A high-revenue, low-margin offer can look great and still drain cash.
- **Missing the 30-day window.** An upsell sold in month four helps lifetime value but does nothing for funding acquisition today.
- **A downsell that is just a discount.** It trains buyers to say no first. Remove scope or change terms instead.
- **Too many products.** Every new product adds delivery complexity. New offers around existing products are cheaper to test and easier to deliver.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He starts with the 30-day number.** Before discussing any offer, he wants CAC and 30-day gross profit per customer. Everything else is judged by whether it moves that ratio.
- **He fixes the biggest gap first.** If the first sale covers little of CAC, he works on the attraction offer. If the first sale is fine but nothing follows, he adds an upsell. He builds in that order and adds one piece at a time.
- **He puts the upsell where the yes is.** He would move the next offer into the same call, the same checkout or the same readout meeting where the customer just agreed.
- **He would rather collect cash upfront.** Upfront payments, setup fees and first months bundled into the entry offer pull gross profit into the first 30 days.
- **He cuts products and adds offers.** When a business has many products and weak economics, he would trim the product list and package the best few in different ways.
- **He treats the 2× CAC target as the point where growth stops being capped by cash** (a rule of thumb). Below 1×, he would hold back on scaling ads until the model is fixed.
- **Questions he would ask:** What does a customer cost you, all in? How much gross profit do they bring in the first 30 days? What do people buy right after saying yes? What do the ones who say no actually object to? What keeps a customer paying after the first result? Which one offer would you test next?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Fill this in for your own business, with numbers.

1. **Baseline:** What are your CAC and 30-day gross profit per customer today? What is the ratio?
2. **Attraction:** Which easy first purchase leads naturally into your core offer, and how much of CAC could it cover?
3. **Upsell:** What is the next problem your customer faces right after buying? What would you offer at that moment, and at what price?
4. **Downsell:** When people say no, is it price, scope or risk? What smaller step removes that objection?
5. **Continuity:** What value could recur every month? When would you offer it, and with what bonus?
6. **Table:** Build the 30-day table with honest take rates. Which single offer, added next, would move the ratio most?
