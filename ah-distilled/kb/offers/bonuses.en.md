## The gist {#gist}

A bonus is a sales tool with one job. Every hesitation a buyer has ("my team won't use it", "I don't have time", "what if it breaks") is a problem you can solve with a separate, named piece of the offer. When each worry has its own answer, the buyer runs out of reasons to say no.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Obstacle** → **bonus that solves it** → **name** + **stated value** · Sum of bonus values > price of the core offer
</div>

Bonuses also change how the offer is perceived. One offer with ten features is one thing to evaluate. One core offer followed by five bonuses, each with its own value, feels like the buyer gets far more than they pay. The same contents, split and presented this way, are worth more in the buyer's head.

## How it works {#how}

### Start from the problems list

In the offer-building process, you list every problem and obstacle the buyer faces before, during and after buying. The core offer solves the main problem. The bonuses come from the rest of that list. For each remaining obstacle ask: what small thing could I add that makes this worry go away?

| Obstacle | Bonus type |
|---|---|
| "I won't know how to start" | Quick-start checklist, onboarding call |
| "My team won't adopt it" | Training kit, internal launch email templates |
| "I don't have time" | Done-for-you setup, templates, scripts |
| "What if I get stuck?" | Support channel, office hours, Q&A recordings |
| "I can't convince my boss" | Business-case template, ROI calculator |

### One bonus per obstacle

Several smaller bonuses usually beat one large bonus. Each one speaks to its own worry, and each one adds its own value to the stack. When you present a bonus, say which obstacle it solves: "Because most teams worry that users won't log in after week two, you also get the adoption kit."

### Name every bonus

A named bonus feels like a real thing with a real price. "Templates" is vague. "The Board-Ready Report Pack" is a product. Use the same naming logic as for the main offer: say who it is for and what result it gives.

### State the value of every bonus

Give each bonus a value, and make it believable. Good anchors are what you would charge for it separately, what it cost to produce, or what a client would pay someone else to do it. Values without any basis lower trust. If you can, sell the bonus separately somewhere, so the value is real.

### The bonuses together should outweigh the core

A rule of thumb from the book: the total value of the bonuses should be greater than the core offer itself. The buyer should feel that the bonuses alone would justify the price. This is what makes an offer feel unfair in the buyer's favour.

### Present bonuses after the core offer

The order matters. First present the core offer and its price, so the buyer evaluates the main thing. Then reveal the bonuses one at a time, each with its obstacle, name and value. Each addition raises the perceived value while the price stays the same. If you mix bonuses into the core description, the stacking effect is lost.

### Cheap for you, valuable to them

The best bonuses cost little to deliver again: templates, checklists, recordings, scripts, software you already built. Their value to the buyer comes from saving time and effort, so they can be very high value at almost no marginal cost.

### Bonuses from partners

You can include other businesses' products or services as bonuses. A partner gives your buyers a free session, a discount or an audit. The partner gets new customers, you get a bonus with a real market value at no cost, and the buyer gets more. Choose partners whose offer solves one of your buyer's obstacles.

### Bonuses and urgency or scarcity

Bonuses are a natural place for time and quantity limits. "This bonus is included for orders this month" or "the first ten get the setup call" creates a reason to act without changing the price of the core.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Random bonuses.** A bonus that solves no obstacle adds noise. If you cannot finish the sentence "because many worry that…", drop it.
- **Unnamed, unvalued extras.** "Plus some templates" adds almost nothing to perceived value. Name it and price it.
- **Inflated values.** A €5,000 checklist is not believed and lowers trust in the whole offer. Anchor values in something real.
- **Bonuses in the core description.** Mixing them in removes the stacking effect. Price first, bonuses after.
- **Bonuses that add work.** A bonus that requires more effort from the buyer (another course to watch) can lower value. Prefer bonuses that save time.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He builds bonuses from objections.** He collects the reasons people gave for not buying and turns each one into a bonus.
- **He stacks many small bonuses** rather than one big one, and presents them one by one after the price.
- **He makes the bonuses worth more than the core.** His rule of thumb is that the buyer should feel the bonuses alone justify the purchase.
- **He favours bonuses with low delivery cost and high perceived value**, such as templates, scripts, recordings and tools that save the buyer time.
- **He uses partners for bonuses** because it adds real value without cost, and it builds relationships that can send leads back.
- **He attaches urgency or scarcity to bonuses** instead of discounting the core price.
- **Questions he would ask:** Why did the last ten people who didn't buy say no? Which of those reasons does your core offer not address? What do you already have that solves it? What would each bonus cost the buyer to get elsewhere? Which partner already sells something your buyers need?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Take your core offer and its price. Answer in writing.

1. **Obstacles:** List every reason a buyer might hesitate before, during and after buying. Aim for at least ten.
2. **Match:** For the top five, write one bonus that removes each obstacle.
3. **Name and value:** Give each bonus a name and a value you could defend. Add them up. Is the total higher than the core price?
4. **Partners:** Which business could provide one bonus in exchange for access to your clients?
5. **Script:** Write how you would present the stack: core and price first, then each bonus with "because…", its name and its value.
