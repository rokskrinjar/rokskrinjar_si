## The gist {#gist}

In the notes, cash flow problems are almost never solved with a loan or a funding round. Alex solves them by changing how and when the customer pays: prepay discounts, setup fees, quarterly or annual billing, layaway, card on file, a planned ladder of payment options. The pattern recurs across hotseats, lectures and sales trainings, and up-front cash works as the master variable behind much of his advice.

The reasoning is simple. If a new customer returns more cash in the first month than they cost to acquire and serve, every customer funds the next one. He ties the 30-day window to the interest-free period on a credit card: it is about as long as a small business can carry the cost of acquisition without its own money.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**30-day rule:** cash collected in the first 30 days > fully loaded delivery cost + CAC.
**Stronger version (one keynote):** 30-day gross profit from one customer > cost to acquire and serve two customers.
</div>

## How it works {#how}

### The 30-day rules

- **Target CAC payback within 30 days.** With that in place he says there is no fixed marketing budget: you spend as long as it stays profitable. A fixed budget, in his framing, means you lose money up front.
- **Know your payback curve.** In one talk he asks for cash returned per dollar of ad spend at day 15, 30 and 60. Without it, marketing decisions are guesswork.
- **Testing budget.** Let a new campaign spend up to about twice the allowable CAC (based on 30-day cash) before switching it off.
- **When payback is too long,** he looks at setup fees, onboarding fees, bundled physical products, longer prepaid durations, buy-one-get-two offers, or last month paid up front.

### Prepay and pay-in-full discounts

He wants at least a 10% discount for paying in full, and calls 10–20% worth it because a small business earns more on its capital than the markets do. A ladder he uses in one sales training: about 20% off for full prepay, about 10% for half down, list price on the payment plan. Two framing rules:

- **Show the highest price first** (the payment plan), then offer discounts for paying sooner. You become the one giving a benefit, not the one adding interest.
- **Say "prepayment discount",** not "paid in full". The second is seller language with nothing in it for the buyer.

He often prefers perks to discounts: faster delivery (skip the line), less risk (a longer guarantee), more ease (concierge). He quotes rough uptake in one lecture: buy-10-get-2 gets 15–20% to prepay, a moderate discount plus perks 30–40%.

### Up-front fees

Use something up front as the vehicle for cash: a setup fee, onboarding fee or equipment. Build the first payment from hard costs up (delivery, hardware, commission) so it covers what you have to pay before the customer has been with you long. A waived setup fee works as an A/B close: waive it for an annual commitment, charge it for quarter-to-quarter flexibility.

### Quarterly and annual prepay

Ask **new** customers, not existing ones, to prepay a quarter to offset CAC; changing billing for newcomers doesn't push current clients away. Annual can be the default or even the only option, with a status perk or a physical bonus attached only to annual. A typical annual discount is about 16% (pay ten months, get twelve). In one lecture he argues that even at half the conversion, annual prepay can bring in several times more cash up front, which funds aggressive acquisition. Quarterly is the middle ground when annual is too much for the buyer.

### Layaway and payment ladders

Layaway means the buyer pays first and delivery starts when they are paid up. Buyers then speed up their own payments, collection is easier, and you carry no delivery risk. His standard step-down order on a sales call:

1. Full prepay with a discount
2. Third-party financing
3. Partial prepay with a smaller discount
4. Payment plan (with automatic renewal at the end, which he finds stickier than month-to-month)
5. A smaller commitment, or just booking the next visit

Financing can lift sales (he cites about 35% in one lecture) but won't save a bad product.

### Card on file

A heavily discounted assessment or a small paid ticket (around $99 in one hotseat) mainly serves to get a card on file. He says a small up-front fee typically gives 70–80% show rates and makes the later sale and upsell smoother: the next purchase is charged to the card already stored.

### When cash is short right now

His order for cash in 48 hours, from one live Q&A: current customers first (prepay, preferably for extra service rather than a discount), then past customers (an "I owe you" credit toward a bigger package), then the lead list (an attraction offer). In a cash crisis he picks one bet with the biggest cash potential in the window and sells the strongest prepaid offer to the existing list, then recycles the cash into ads. Short-term cash plays are acceptable if they fund fixing the real constraint.

Cash at signup covers the cost of each customer, so commission-only door-to-door reps can scale without debt. He notes that even if only 20% take the anchor, average cash per sale goes up.

### Indoor family park, weeks from running out of cash

Revenue around $4.8M, unprofitable, heavily indebted. Alex chose one bet only: a prepaid summer camp offer sold to the existing list through a giveaway with a claim deadline and phone follow-up, with the cash recycled into ads.

### Couples coaching, slow payback

Two 12-month offers and ad-driven challenges with slow payback. He asked for about $9K (the first quarter) up front, offered layaway as the downsell, and tied fast-action bonuses to the up-front payment. His expectation was that the first payment, more than the total price, drives conversion.

### Invented: analytics retainer for a manufacturer

| | Monthly billing | Setup fee + quarter up front |
|---|---|---|
| Cash in first 30 days | €3,000 | €4,500 + €9,000 = €13,500 |
| Delivery cost in first 30 days | €2,400 | €2,400 |
| CAC | €6,000 | €6,000 |
| 30-day position | −€5,400 | +€5,100 |

With monthly billing the agency waits almost four months to recover the cost of each client. With an onboarding fee and the first quarter prepaid, the client funds the next acquisition in month one.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Reaching for debt or investors first.** He treats a cash gap as an offer-design problem. If a business with strong returns can't raise money, he blames the deal terms, not the pitch.
- **Discounting without asking for cash.** A discount that isn't tied to prepayment or commitment gives away margin and gets nothing back.
- **Calling it "paid in full".** Seller language. Frame it as a prepayment discount the buyer receives.
- **Changing billing for existing clients.** Apply new payment terms to new customers.
- **Paying sales on revenue booked.** He says pay rarely changes rep behaviour, with one exception: paying on cash collected raises cash collected.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He asks for the 30-day number first.** What is a customer worth in gross profit within 30 days, and what does it cost to acquire and serve them?
- **He redesigns the offer before the financing.** Setup fees, prepay, annual default, layaway. Debt and capital come after the offer cannot be improved.
- **He anchors high and steps down.** Payment-plan price first, prepay discount second, then financing, partial prepay, plan, smaller commitment. Everyone buys something.
- **He uses perks before percentages.** Faster, safer, easier, or a bonus worth more to the buyer than it costs you.
- **He goes to existing customers when cash is urgent.** Prepay for extra service, credits toward bigger packages, one strong offer to the list.
- **He removes the money ceiling, then spends.** Once acquisition funds itself, the limit should be operational, not financial.
- **Questions he would ask:** How much cash do you collect in the first 30 days? What is your CAC, and how long until it's paid back? Do you offer prepay or financing, and what share takes it? Do you have a card on file? How much cash do you have, and can you deliver what you've already sold with it?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Fill in with your last ten new clients.

1. **30-day cash:** What did each client pay in their first 30 days, on average?
2. **30-day cost:** What did delivery cost in those 30 days, and what was CAC per client?
3. **Gap:** Is cash collected above or below cost? By how much?
4. **Vehicles:** Which up-front item fits your offer: a setup fee, onboarding, first quarter prepaid, annual default?
5. **Ladder:** Write your step-down sequence from full prepay to the smallest commitment, with exact prices.
6. **Urgent cash:** If you needed cash in 48 hours, what would you offer current and past clients?
