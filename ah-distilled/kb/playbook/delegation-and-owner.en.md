## The gist {#gist}

Early on, a business is a suit of armour that multiplies the owner but cannot move without them. Alex's goal is a business that creates value without you: only then is it worth something to someone else, and only then can you step away. Two companies with the same profit are worth very different amounts if one needs its owner eighty hours a week.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Delegation** = time study → task list → **process** → decision rules with limits → **KPIs** → graduation test (about 80% as good) → shadow, supervise, support
</div>

He treats this as a sequence of giving up control. At each stage, the thing you hold on to is the next thing you must hand off, and it always feels uncomfortable. Handing over control never hands over accountability: you stay responsible for the result and close enough to know where the problems are.

## How it works {#how}

### The buyback order

Across several lectures the order is consistent:

1. Low-skill support and delivery tasks.
2. Admin: billing, collections, contracts. Then money tracking, once someone tracks it better than you (you still look at it).
3. Selling. He puts the ceiling for founder-led selling around $1M a year.
4. Running the sales team. He places this roughly between $3M and $10M.
5. Leading leaders. From about $10M to $30M you lead a small group, and culture travels through them.

The rule for each task: if the market will do it for a fraction of what your hour is worth, hand it off, even if you believe nobody does it as well. Freed time must go into higher-value work. Founders who free time and then fill it with nothing see margins fall.

### The delegation system

1. **Time study.** Log what you do every 15 minutes for a week. List every task as granularly as possible. He uses the same study on any key employee the business depends on too much.
2. **Person, process or project.** Next to each task, write whether it needs a person, a process or a one-off project. Rate it green (can hand off now), yellow (you need to build a process first) or red (you lack the skill or the person). Clear greens first, then yellows, then reds.
3. **Decision rules.** Document decisions as well as tasks: if this happens, do that. The more repetitive the role, the more standard the rules. Give each person spending authority without approval; his examples include under $500 per decision with a $5,000 monthly cap, or an operator who can spend up to 1,000 without calling him. A monthly financial review catches waste.
4. **KPIs.** Ask each person how their role makes the company money. If they cannot answer, help them, for example by reversing the costs of doing the job badly into KPIs.
5. **Graduation test.** Can they get 100% of the result about 80% as well as you? At handoff he accepts 60 to 80% if there is a clear path to improve, and judges people by how fast they get better.
6. **Handoff.** They shadow you, then you supervise them, then you are available but not involved.

### Document, demonstrate, duplicate

His training method recurs in hotseats and sales talks: write the checklist, do the task in front of them using only the checklist, then have them do it in front of you while you correct immediately. Keep the steps simple enough for someone with no experience. If you have no written process, record yourself doing the work: the structure is already there.

### Know where the bodies are buried

Delegation succeeds only when the function's results hold or improve after the handoff. If they drop, you abdicated. He wants owners able to name each department's current problems. If a department only brings good news, you are too far away. The fix is more visibility, never taking control back.

### Owner-independence tests

- You are off the company chat, one person can reach you, you meet that person once a week, and the company keeps growing.
- Basic pass: a month away and nothing burns down. Real pass: three months away (or six months in another video) and the business grows. He recommends actually taking the time off, fixing what breaks and repeating.
- A new operator should leave the owner with more time. If they mainly create tasks for the owner, it is not working.
- To train judgement, ask staff "what do you think I would say?" and tell them to do that.

### When work isn't getting done

Two lectures give the same triangle. Before judging the person, check:

| Cause | Question | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Expectation (what) | Did I state it clearly, in writing, and is it in their reporting? | Write it down with a deadline; review it often |
| Training (how) | Do they know how, given their workload? | Do it together, record it, define how to report back |
| Motivation (why) | Do they know why it matters to the company and to them? | Explain the why; check the comp plan rewards it |

He starts from "this is probably my fault" and checks expectation first. If communication and training are fixed and nothing changes, he names it as motivation and has the direct conversation. Moving the person to another role is usually wrong.

### The founder hides the real constraint

His sharpest observation: founders keep working on what they enjoy and are good at, and hand off what they dislike. Their strengths become the bottleneck. A media person optimises media; a closer studies closing. If you dislike people work, recruiting and managing are probably your constraint. Growth requires an identity change: from best salesperson or best coach to the person who teaches and then builds the system. "I have trust issues" is not an acceptable reason in his view.

## Examples {#examples}

*Examples are anonymised cases from the source videos; the last one is invented for illustration.*

### Field-service owner who is in everything

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A high-end field-service company, about $1.5M revenue and $700K net. Each technician brings $180–300K a year. The owner insists on being involved in everything and has stopped recruiting.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** A one-week time study, the bottom half of his activities delegated, and the freed time spent recruiting technicians nationally with a budget of about $50K per hire.
</div>
</div>

Half the owner's time back could double the business, because a technician is worth far more than the cost to recruit one.

### Dental practice with one expert doing every step

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A founder-doctor handles every high-ticket case, capped at about 25–30 patients a month, with a booking delay of weeks.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** Associates take the simpler half of the visits. The founder becomes the architect and teacher. Combined with price and payment changes, the follow-up showed average ticket up from about $28K to $44K and booking within a week.
</div>
</div>

Halving the expert's workload doubles capacity. The founder's identity moves from best practitioner to best teacher.

### Owner who closes best

An event company owner closed about 40% while two reps underperformed. Alex turned the reps into appointment setters for the owner, still on commission, with daily morning and end-of-day check-ins, until the process was documented well enough to hand back.

### Analytics consultancy founder (invented)

A founder of a ten-person BI consultancy does presales, solution design and code reviews. A time study shows 12 hours a week on reviews. He writes a review checklist (green), records himself doing three reviews (document, demonstrate), then has a senior consultant review while he watches (duplicate). After a month the senior handles all reviews at about 80% of his quality, with a rule: escalate only changes to shared data models. The 12 hours go into presales.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Abdication.** Handing off and never looking again. Results drop, and the owner concludes nobody can do it.
- **Delegating only what you dislike.** You keep the tasks you enjoy, and those become the bottleneck.
- **Handing over tasks without decisions.** Without if-then rules and spending limits, every exception still comes back to you.
- **Waiting for 100%.** Demanding your own quality on day one means you never hand anything off.
- **Wasting the freed time.** Time bought back has to go into higher-value work or margins suffer.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He starts with a time study,** never with a guess. The log shows where the hours go.
- **He hands off from the bottom of the value list** and keeps going in the fixed order: support, admin, selling, the sales team, leaders.
- **He wants decisions on paper,** with if-then rules and a spending limit, so people can act without calling the owner.
- **He sets a graduation bar** at roughly 80% of the owner's quality and accepts less at first if improvement is visible.
- **He diagnoses before he blames:** expectation, then training, then motivation.
- **He tests independence for real** by having the owner leave for a month, then longer, and fixing what breaks.
- **Questions he would ask:** What did your last week look like in 15-minute blocks? Which tasks could someone do for a fraction of your hourly value? Can you name each department's current problems? Would the sales model still work if reps closed half as well as you? Did they know it was expected, do they know how, and do they know why? What are you holding on to because you enjoy it?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Run this over the next two weeks.

1. **Time study:** Log one week in 15-minute blocks. Which ten tasks took most of your time?
2. **Colour code:** Mark each task green, yellow or red, with a person, process or project next to it.
3. **Rules:** For your most frequent green task, write five if-then rules and a spending limit.
4. **Bar:** Define what 80% as good looks like and how you will measure it.
5. **Absence:** Pick a date for a week away. What must exist before then so nothing breaks?
