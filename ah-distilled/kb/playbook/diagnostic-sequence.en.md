## The gist {#gist}

Alex diagnoses almost every business the same way. He asks for the numbers before he listens to the story, because the problem the owner brings is rarely the problem the numbers show. "We need more leads" turns out to be an offer problem, "ads stopped working" turns out to be a lifetime value problem, and "my team is the issue" turns out to be a broken model.

Then he sorts the business into one of two buckets: it cannot get enough customers (demand) or it cannot serve more customers (supply). Inside that bucket he finds the single stage that sits furthest below a healthy benchmark, names it as a "how do I…" question, and limits his advice to the two or three moves that attack it.

**Numbers** → **Supply or demand?** → **Weakest stage vs benchmark** → **One "how do I…" question** → **2–3 moves**, then the owner's real goal decides the plan
</div>

## How it works {#how}

### 1. The fixed order of questions

In his live hotseats the questions come in nearly the same order every time: what do you sell, to whom, at what price, how do customers find you, how many sales a month, what is the close rate, what is a customer worth over their lifetime, what does it cost to get one, how many leave, what is the gross margin, how much capacity is left. Only then does he ask what the owner actually wants.

He expects owners to know these by heart. In one walkthrough he reduces it to six numbers: new sales per month, current revenue, price, churn, lifetime value and gross margin, and notes that most owners cannot name the last three. In another he says plainly that he breaks down unit economics before asking about payroll or anything else, because that is where the problem shows up.

### 2. Distrust the stated problem

A recurring pattern across many videos: the owner names a symptom, the numbers point somewhere else. A practice with three "separate" problems (low margin, pricing set by insurers, one channel) had one model problem. A founder listing six problems was usually missing around three senior people. An owner who wants to start a new business after a plateau usually does not know how to grow the current one. Alex treats the stated problem as a hypothesis and checks it against the numbers.

He also separates a complaint from a constraint: if you know more spend would double the business and you are not doing it, your chores are not the constraint.

### 3. Supply or demand triage

His first real question in most hotseats: is this business supply-constrained or demand-constrained? The test is concrete. If leads, ad spend or outreach doubled tomorrow, would sales double, or would you create a mess? A mess means supply. He says you can only have one of the two at a time, and the constraint swings back and forth like an accordion. Owners stay stuck for years because they keep working on the side they already solved.

| If the answer is… | The work is… | What he refuses |
|---|---|---|
| Demand (capacity is free) | Offer, price, conversion, creative, then more spend | Fixing delivery or hiring ahead of growth |
| Supply (doubling would break delivery) | Raise price, change the delivery ratio, productise, hire | More marketing; a second business "to market more" |

On the supply side he goes further: tolerate mediocre marketing while capacity is full, because improving it would make the business worse.

### 4. Compare each stage to a benchmark

Inside the funnel he walks the stages (click, opt-in, booking, show, close, repeat purchase) and compares each to the numbers he knows. He fixes the stage furthest below its benchmark, because that is where a 2–4x is possible. Another version of the same idea: add the same small percentage to every stage and see which one moves output the most. That is usually the weakest stage.

He normally optimises acquisition front to back, since early stages touch the most people. The exception is one stage far below the norm, such as a 5% show rate where 60% is normal. A 60% show rate cannot triple; a 1% click-through can.

### 5. Turn the constraint into a "how do I…" question

Once he has the constraint, he rewrites it as a solvable skill question: "how do I recruit, train and manage a sales team", "how do I get customers from a channel I am allowed to use". In one story he refused to discuss pricing, messaging or lifetime gross profit with an owner until the named constraint was solved.

### 6. Limit the advice to two or three moves

Hotseats end with a short, ordered list. He writes the easy, high-impact moves first and the big bets last, and repeatedly leaves alone the parts that already work (a converting offer, a sales process with healthy close rates). In one case he trimmed his plan to two steps the owner would actually carry out. He also orders the fixes so that cheap conversion gains come before more spend, since they may already push the business into a supply constraint.

### 7. Then the owner's real goal

Before he commits to a plan he asks what the owner wants: double it, sell it, or simply make money. The answer changes the advice. If the owner only wants cash, he avoids complexity and repeats the working playbook. If they want to sell, concentration risk and owner dependence move up the list. In some talks he asks the goal first, together with current state, desired state and the obstacle between them.

### Online weight-loss program, about $4M, webinar funnel

The owner was nervous about launching a big new lead magnet. Alex compared each stage to his numbers: show rate at 30% was already above benchmark, opt-in sat at 5% where 20% or more is possible. All testing went to the opt-in page and the lead magnet's name, because the stage furthest below benchmark held the largest multiple.

### Business at about $150K a month

Many things could have been improved, including the pricing model and messaging. Alex named one constraint (he had never built a sales team), rewrote it as "how do I recruit, manage and train salespeople", and declined to talk about anything else on the follow-up call.

### Qlik consultancy, invented

A two-person BI consultancy says it needs more leads. Numbers: 6 proposals a month, 5 signed (83% close), both consultants booked for the next four months. Triage answer: doubling leads would break delivery, so it is supply. The question becomes "how do I deliver the same result with less senior time?" Two moves: raise the day rate on new work, and split delivery so a junior builds the data model while seniors handle design and sign-off.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Starting from the story.** Advice given before the numbers usually treats a symptom.
- **Working the solved side.** Buying more leads while delivery is full, or hiring while capacity sits idle.
- **Fixing everything at once.** A list of ten fixes gets none of them done; he keeps it to two or three.
- **Touching what works.** Rewriting an offer or script that converts well, instead of attacking the weak stage.
- **Skipping the goal.** The right plan for a cash-flow owner differs from the plan for someone building to sell.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **Numbers before opinions.** He asks for price, volume, close rate, churn, lifetime value, acquisition cost and gross margin before he says anything about strategy.
- **Supply or demand first.** If doubling customers would break delivery, he works on capacity and price. If not, it is a pure demand problem, and he works on offer and traffic.
- **The weakest stage gets everything.** He benchmarks each funnel stage and puts all testing on the one furthest below its norm.
- **One constraint, phrased as a skill.** He turns it into a "how do I…" question and refuses side topics until it is solved.
- **Short ordered list.** Two or three moves, cheapest and highest impact first, working parts left alone.
- **A new venture is a red flag.** If the owner would drop the new idea once the current business tripled, it is an escape from the constraint.
- **Questions he would ask:** What do you sell, to whom, at what price? Where do customers come from? What are your show and close rates? What is a customer worth and what does one cost? If customers doubled, what breaks? Why aren't you spending ten times more on ads? What do you want: double it, sell it, or take cash out?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Answer with numbers, not impressions.

1. **The numbers:** Write down new sales per month, price, close rate, monthly churn, gross margin and acquisition cost. Mark the ones you had to guess.
2. **Triage:** If leads doubled next month, what would break first? Is that a supply or a demand answer?
3. **Benchmarks:** List your funnel stages with today's rate next to each. Which is furthest below a healthy level?
4. **The question:** Rewrite your constraint as one "how do I…" sentence.
5. **The moves:** Pick at most three moves that attack it, in order. What are you deliberately leaving untouched?
6. **The goal:** Do you want to grow it, sell it, or take cash out? Does the plan still fit?
