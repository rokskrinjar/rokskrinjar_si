## The gist {#gist}

Alex separates income from wealth. Income comes from work. Wealth comes from owning things that create value without you. If a business shrinks materially when the owner steps away, he calls it a high-paying job you can't quit, not an asset. Two companies with the same profit can be worth wildly different amounts: one needs the owner 80 hours a week and cannot be sold, the other runs without them and sells at a multiple.

He frames value as a fraction. The top is how many customers you have and how much gross profit each brings over their lifetime. The bottom is risk: everything that makes a buyer worry the profit won't survive the sale.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Enterprise value** = (**customers** × **lifetime gross profit**) ÷ **risk**
</div>

Most of his enterprise-value advice is about shrinking the denominator: concentration risk, churn and founder dependency.

## How it works {#how}

### Income versus asset

His standard illustration: two businesses at $10M revenue and $2M profit. The owner-run one adds roughly $500k a year to the owner's net worth after tax and living costs. The owner-free one, at a 6x multiple, is worth $12M, and every extra $500k of profit adds about $3M of value against about $250k of take-home cash. In another talk he puts the wealth gap between a business sellable at 10x and one that can't be sold at roughly 20x.

Two guardrails: enterprise value only matters once personal cash needs are covered, and you should build it sellable whether or not you sell, because a sellable business is an asset you can borrow against.

### Concentration risk

He treats concentration as the line between a job and an asset. It shows up in four places:

| Risk | What it looks like | His default fix |
|---|---|---|
| Key person | One person carries marketing, sales, delivery or the operational "glue" | Process plus people; vesting equity if the key person isn't you |
| Channel | All sales from one ad platform or one referral source | Below ~$10M don't worry; above it, build a second channel |
| Customer | One or two clients are a large share of revenue | Treat it as an acquisition red flag; open "more wells" |
| Vendor / partner | One processor, supplier or platform deal feeds the business | Redundancy in every dependency; keep backups approved |

Key-person risk counts double when the key person is the owner who leaves on a sale. The more areas one person covers, the worse it is. He names founder-as-acquisition-channel as the biggest single version. Single-channel risk is acceptable early; depending on a partner or platform is not.

### Sticky, recurring revenue

Buyers pay for revenue that repeats. In his rules of thumb, private equity looks for roughly 80% annual customer retention, and licensing or software only earn attractive multiples (6x or more) above that level. A business that sells one product once with no back end is, in his words, closer to a job. Consulting and education are often non-recurring by nature: either find a consumable (such as staff turnover that keeps needing training) or accept that you can make good money with less enterprise value. For B2B he pushes expansion revenue, aiming for net revenue retention above 100%.

### Brand and proof as moats

Without a defensible product, brand is the main differentiator, and he notes that acquirers buy brands, not products. He lists three measurable effects: higher click-through (lower CAC), more repeat buying (higher LTV) and premium pricing (higher gross profit). Without brand, a product business is "media arbitrage" that gets copied. He also counts capital requirements as a moat.

### Founder dependency: the route from income to value

His owner-independence test is whether the company keeps growing for months without you stepping back in. The moves he recommends:

1. Time-study your week and hand off tasks to people, processes or projects.
2. Get yourself out of acquisition first, with systems that produce marketing without your face.
3. Replace the founder in delivery with several narrow specialists, each branded as the expert in one area, rather than one generalist clone.
4. Transfer a founder brand gradually: founder alone in ads, then founder plus successor, then each separately, then the successor alone. In his own case it took about 12 months.
5. Keep a personal brand separate from a business you intend to sell.

If the founder is bigger than the company, you can still sell a minority stake, but he calls that a liquidity event, not an exit.

### Sellability, exits and M&A comps

His multiples, all rules of thumb: businesses under about $10M revenue typically sell at 1–4x earnings, with multiples rising above $10M revenue and $2M profit. Franchisors can get around 15x versus about 8x for local chains, because future openings are contracted. Before building for an exit, he checks M&A activity for that business type, studies how sold businesses differ from yours and closes the gap. He also asks bankers and PE firms what weakens a business; for his own the answers were churn, his name as the brand and a single channel.

### Sell the eggs, not the goose

The goose is the asset that creates value, often founder-led and unsellable. The eggs are the sellable assets it produces. His advice is usually to keep the goose and build the business to produce and sell eggs. For an educator or consultant to a specific business type, that can mean standardising clients' businesses so the best ones can be rolled up and sold together.

### Two businesses, one owner (hotseat)

A commercial builder at about $11M and 18% margin also ran a young elevator-service company at about $3M and 30% margin, with recurring, niche revenue. His own route: sell the construction business quickly, even at a discount, and go all in on the recurring one, because a quarter of focused recurring growth can match the old business's enterprise value.

### Selling the eggs (lecture)

A consultant to accounting firms wanted to sell his consulting business. Nobody buys that, but buyers do buy accounting firms in groups. His advice: assemble roll-ups of 10 to 20 client firms every year or two and take a slice for arranging the deal.

| | Client firm sold alone | Firms sold together |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple of earnings | ~4x | ~12x |
| Firm owner's share after a ~30% slice | 4x | ~8.4x, about double |

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Confusing profit with value.** High profit that needs the owner every day has a low or zero multiple.
- **Chasing exit value before cash is secure.** He puts personal cash needs first.
- **Ignoring concentration until a buyer finds it.** Key person, one channel, one big client and one supplier are exactly what acquirers discount.
- **Trying to sell the goose.** A founder-led education or consulting business rarely sells well; the sellable asset is often elsewhere.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He asks the goal first:** sell eventually, or just make money? If only money, he keeps it simple and runs the working playbook more times.
- **He audits concentration across all four risks** and fixes the one an acquirer would discount most.
- **He removes the founder from acquisition before delivery,** because founder-driven lead flow is the biggest key-person risk.
- **He prioritises retention over new revenue** when the goal is enterprise value, aiming for roughly 80% annual retention.
- **He prices hard problems in enterprise value terms:** losing $100k over six months to find a second channel is cheap if it adds $10–15M of value to a $2M-EBITDA business at 5x.
- **He invests surplus cash in talent and brand** in service businesses, and refuses to trade long-term brand for a short-term sale.
- **Questions he would ask:** Is anything in this business dependent on you? What would an acquirer see as its weaknesses? What share of revenue comes from your top client, channel and supplier? What is annual retention? Who buys businesses like this, and at what multiple? Which part is the goose, and what are the eggs?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Score each risk 1 (low) to 5 (high) for your business today.

1. **Founder test:** If you left for three months, which revenue, deals and decisions would stop?
2. **Concentration:** What share comes from your top client, top channel and top vendor or partner?
3. **Stickiness:** What is your annual customer retention, and what recurring or expansion revenue could you add?
4. **Moat:** What do people say about you when you aren't there, and does it let you charge more?
5. **Comps:** Who has bought businesses like yours recently, and how are they different from yours?
6. **Goose and eggs:** What creates the value, and what does it produce that someone else would buy?
