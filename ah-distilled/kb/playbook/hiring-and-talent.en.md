## The gist {#gist}

In almost every lecture on scaling, Alex ends up at the same place: once demand is solved, the business grows only as fast as it can find, train and keep good people. When an owner lists six problems, he often reads them as one: the company is missing around three senior hires. Above roughly $30M a year he describes his own bottleneck as nothing but talent.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Scaling speed** = **speed of recruiting talent**. Ask "who" before "how": often the fastest answer is someone who has already done it.
</div>

He backs this with blunt economics. In his framing an A-player costs a bit more and produces several times as much, so paying properly for the right person is one of the largest arbitrages in business. His recurring rules of thumb: hire when the freed time or capacity returns several times the salary (he mentions 4 to 5x), and treat each new salary band as a new territory that reveals talent you did not know existed.

## How it works {#how}

### Recruiting is a second funnel

Across at least five videos he describes recruiting as a mirror of the customer pipeline: application generation, application nurture, the interview (which is a sales call), onboarding and training (fulfilment), then retention and ascension. You use the same channels you use for customers: ads, content, your own lists, direct outreach including poaching, referrals and word of mouth. You track the same kind of numbers: reach-outs per interview, finals per hire, cost and time to fill, net headcount after churn.

His rules of thumb: about 100 LinkedIn reach-outs per hire in one example, and strong candidates are gone within about 8 days, so a slow first reply loses them. Referral bonuses are sized against the gross profit a hire produces.

### Two interview tests

These recur in several talks:

1. **Metrics.** How many relevant metrics does the candidate use to describe their job, and how well do they know them? Someone who cannot define the outcome cannot deliver it. A strong HR candidate once quizzed him on cost per hire, time to fill and two-way fit at 90 days.
2. **Money.** "How does what you do make the business money?" He rejects vague answers. A sales leader should talk about raising conversion across the team's leads; a finance lead about cutting costs and turning numbers into decisions.

For leadership roles he adds a third: if you are not learning from them in the interview, do not hire them. For specific roles he prefers a real problem over hypotheticals: show the actual stats and ask how they would attack it.

### Interview volume is the method

He interviews far more people than feels sensible. Volume finds the outlier and trains your judgement: by the fiftieth interview for one role, you can tell. He cites a partner who interviewed about 600 developers to find a technical co-founder, and his own five sales managers before one worked. For front-line to manager roles he runs group interviews, where strong candidates speak clearly and help the nervous ones. For a role you do not understand, the interviews are your education.

### Who to hire

- **Smallest skill gap.** Every skill is trainable; the question is the cost of training it. Prefer the candidate whose missing skill is quickest to teach.
- **Experience you can buy.** For senior roles, hire someone who has already taken a similar business from your size to the size you want, ideally with about ten years in the function. Their mistakes were paid for by someone else.
- **Leaders over managers.** He grades people on three levels: individual contributor (knows what), manager (knows how, teaches it), leader (builds the system that trains people). He says leaders become necessary from about $3M a year.
- **First hires.** The first five or so are extensions of you that take over repeated, low-value work: support and admin, then a setter or salesperson, marketing help, a fractional bookkeeper, a tech person. If you sell well, hire sales later.
- **Bar raiser.** Hire only people who raise the average of the team they join, because people tend to hire slightly worse than themselves.

### Promotion risk

His most repeated warning: the best closer is rarely the best sales manager. Hunters rarely want to coach. Assess each level separately, show a top performer the manager's day before they ask for it, and keep early titles modest so there is room to hire above. If a promotion fails, redefine their role with someone above them, or part ways. Own your mistake and frame the senior hire as an investment in their career.

### Standards, tolerance and culture

The weakest team has the manager who tolerates the most. A long list of rules is, in his reading, a sign of low standards and wrong hires. The person with the highest standards should be in charge. His sales-team culture system: visible daily leaderboard, clear KPIs, a competition every 4 to 6 weeks, and the bottom 10% cut each quarter. A high-performance culture keeps attrition low even while firing often, because the strong stay.

### Firing and pay

Two weeks below KPI means a performance plan; two more weeks without improvement means exit, and new hires hear this on day one. He rarely moves an underperformer sideways (he says nine times out of ten it is the wrong call). On pay: change it only to fix a problem attracting or keeping talent, because raises cannot be taken back. Before approving a new hire, ask for the team's hour-by-hour utilisation. Do not hire ahead of growth, with commission sales reps as the exception.

## Examples {#examples}

### Used-car dealership with too many priorities

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** A profitable dealership (about $500–600K profit) wants branding, social ads, a commercial-van line and a service shop, all at once, run by the owner.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** Alex reads it as a missing-people problem: about three senior hires. First a finance lead, told to find their own salary in savings within 90 days. Then operators for service and the used lot. Only then does the owner chase the van line with freed time.
</div>
</div>

The finance hire funds the next hire, and each senior person removes a whole problem from the owner's list.

### Wholesale business after a bad promotion

<div class="ba" markdown="1">
<div class="before" markdown="1">
**Before.** Deals fall from about 70 to 40 a month after the sales manager and second-best closer leave. The top closer is promoted and coaches poorly. The owner wants to hire a sales director.
</div>
<div class="after" markdown="1">
**After.** The owner learns sales personally, simplifies one script, enforces it for everyone and trains through call review and drills.
</div>
</div>

The wrong personality was promoted, and a sales-led company whose owner cannot run sales stays hostage to whoever does.

### Solo agency afraid of the first hire

A solo ads agency with about $90K real profit asked how to make its first hire. Alex's diagnosis: the model is broken, and hiring would only scale the problem. Before hiring, either 10x the price per client or 10x delivery efficiency, because there is no margin to pay anyone.

### BI consultancy hiring a senior consultant (invented)

| Step | Weak | Strong |
|---|---|---|
| Sourcing | One job post, wait | Outreach to consultants at firms you admire, referral bonus, content aimed at candidates |
| Screening | CV and certifications | Group call, then "which metrics do you track on a project?" |
| Final | Chat about tools | Real client data model and question: "how would you cut report delivery time in half?" |
| Decision | First decent candidate | After 20+ interviews, only if they raise the team average |

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Hiring the first decent person.** Without volume you have no comparison and no judgement.
- **Promoting the best performer by default.** You can lose a great contributor and gain a weak manager.
- **Saving on salary for a key role.** A few thousand saved on a person who moves the company years forward is a false economy in his view.
- **Recruiting with no system.** No channels, no speed, no metrics, then concluding "good people don't exist".
- **Tolerating low standards to keep the peace.** The tolerance becomes the culture.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He checks whether talent is really the constraint.** If the model has no margin, he fixes price or delivery before any hire.
- **He makes recruiting the owner's job when it is the bottleneck,** compressing it hard: many interviews in one week, tracked like a sales pipeline.
- **He buys experience for senior roles** and accepts a short-term profit dip to pay for higher-level leaders, framing it as skipping years of mistakes.
- **He interviews with the two tests** (metrics, how the role makes money) and passes on leaders he cannot learn from.
- **He sets standards visibly and enforces them on everyone,** including the top performer, with time-boxed performance plans.
- **He refuses** sideways moves for failing people, inflated titles in small teams, and pay raises that do not solve a recruiting or retention problem.
- **Questions he would ask:** If you had ten of your best person, would you make more money? What are you doing this week to recruit? How many candidates per hire, how fast is first contact? What metrics does this person use for their job? How does their role make money? Does this hire raise the team's average?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Answer in writing for your next or most important hire.

1. **Constraint:** Is talent the thing that stops you from doing more, or is it price, demand or the model?
2. **Return:** What will this person free up or produce, and is it at least several times their cost?
3. **Funnel:** Which channels will you use, how many candidates do you need per hire, and how fast will you reply?
4. **Tests:** Which metrics should a strong candidate name, and what real problem will you give them in the final round?
5. **Standards:** What will you no longer tolerate, and what is the written performance-plan rule new hires will hear on day one?
