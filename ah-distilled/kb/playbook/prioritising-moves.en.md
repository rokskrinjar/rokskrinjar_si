## The gist {#gist}

Most hotseats end the same way. Alex writes a short numbered list, with quick, likely, high-impact moves at the top and heavy strategic bets at the bottom. Before anything new appears on that list, he asks why the owner cannot simply do more of what already works. New ideas have to prove they beat spending more on the existing winner.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**More** of what works → make it **better** → only then something **new**.
Rank candidates by **Impact × Confidence × Ease**, and hand over **two or three**.
</div>

The ordering is how he controls risk. A move that adds volume to a proven channel has known economics. A move that changes the offer, the avatar or the channel resets the learning curve. He picks up the obvious money first and lets the cash and confidence from those wins fund the harder bets later.

## How it works {#how}

### More, then better, then new

He states the default order in several talks: first ask why you cannot do more of what works; if you cannot do more, ask whether you can make it better; only then try something new. He uses it as a quarterly plan too ("more, better", repeated until something blocks it). In one Q&A he explains why small owners stall: they find something that works, stop doing it, and chase several new things.

Typical "more" moves from the cases: going from a few networking events a week to several a day, from one stage talk a year to one a week, doubling the ad spend on a funnel that already returns about 4x, or sending 20 partner emails a day instead of a handful. A channel that works but has never been scaled is, in his view, usually the biggest win in the business.

### Rank by impact, confidence and ease

He names ICE (impact, confidence, ease) as his prioritisation tool and in one hotseat splits ease into speed and effort, so the ranking mirrors the value equation. The practical rules he attaches to it:

- **Light first, heavy later**, unless a later step depends on an earlier one.
- **Cheapest to heaviest.** In one case the sequence was better creative, then the right pixel event, then a high-ticket one-time offer, and only then a sales team selling a large back-end package.
- **Low operational risk when execution is weak.** If the team is unlikely to execute a complex change well, he prefers the move that still works when done badly.
- **Low operational drag.** Price is his favourite example: you change a number or a sentence, and nothing in delivery has to change.
- **Can these people execute it?** He checks the team before finalising the list.

### His order of operations

When several areas need work, he follows a fairly stable sequence across videos:

| Step | What he fixes | Why it comes here |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Offer and price | Highest leverage, lowest drag; frees cash |
| 2 | Sales motion and conversion | Fixes leaks before you pay for more traffic |
| 3 | Demand on existing channels | Spend and volume on what is proven |
| 4 | New channels, avatars, products | Highest risk, funded by steps 1–3 |

He puts it as: do not push more demand through thin margins, and do not buy traffic into a leaky bucket. For a stalled local practice he ordered it as pricing and packaging, then attribution, then ads, then content. He adds that cheap conversion fixes can double leads on their own and push a business into a supply constraint. The supply-versus-demand triage itself comes before all of this (see the diagnostic sequence).

### Never switch off what works

He repeats this rule often. Do not turn off a channel that makes money to test a new one; add the new one on top. Do not let the main channel drop while building a second. Do not put a new person on a new channel: hand the existing channel to someone, confirm it holds, then put a proven person on the new one. When testing a new lead source, route it to one proven closer so the only variables are the source, the offer and the process. If budget has to move, he moves it gradually (for example 30%, then 50%).

### Do not change what customers love

If demand exceeds capacity, he raises price or adds capacity rather than changing the product, because pricing is easy to change and delivery is hard. He warns that products get worse through addition, that a working offer in a large company touches billing, sales, ads and fulfilment at once, and that a sales process with healthy close rates should be left alone. He also refuses to fix problems that are not showing up in the metrics: a narrative about a threat, with no rise in churn, is no reason to rebuild.

### The biggest obvious pile of money first

In a keynote he says most businesses grow most by picking up the biggest obvious pile of money in front of them. Elsewhere he asks owners which single thing would double the business this year, and tells them to drop whatever is not that. In one case he refused to discuss anything except the main constraint until the owner stopped asking about secondary optimisations.

### Two or three moves only

He deliberately limits advice to the two or three highest-impact moves so the owner actually executes them, and in one hotseat simplifies a plan to two steps.

## Examples {#examples}

### Home-service company with nothing broken

A duct-cleaning business with about $1.25M revenue, 38% profit, a 99% show rate and an 82% close rate asked for more leads. Alex's ordered list:

| # | Move | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Raise price about 10%, hold while close rate stays above ~65% | About +25% net profit, no operational change |
| 2 | Keep paying down debt | Lower risk |
| 3 | Dedicated ad landing page, all buttons to it | Cheap conversion fix |
| 4 | More creative, organic winners as ads, then more spend | Ads already returned about 13:1 |
| 5–7 | Retargeting, reactivation emails, partner offer | More from the existing base and partners |
| 8 | Outbound to new segments | Newest and heaviest, so last |

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Starting with "new".** A new channel or product feels like progress, while the working channel stays under-exploited.
- **Fixing the loudest problem instead of the biggest one.** He looks for the move that changes throughput most and ignores secondary optimisations until it is done.
- **Turning off a winner to test.** Replacing a working channel removes the cash and the benchmark you need to judge the test.
- **Handing over a long list.** Ten good ideas executed badly lose to two executed fully.
- **Improving what customers already love.** Changing the core product or a healthy sales process risks the part that works.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He asks "why can't you just do more of this?"** before discussing anything new, and treats the answer as the next constraint.
- **He writes a numbered list in ICE order**: quick, likely, high-impact items first, structural bets last, dependencies respected.
- **He fixes offer and price first**, then conversion, then volume on proven channels, and only then new channels.
- **He keeps every working channel running** while testing, and isolates new variables (one closer, one source, one change).
- **He leaves alone what customers love** and what the metrics say is healthy.
- **He cuts the plan to two or three moves** the owner will actually carry out.
- **Questions he would ask:** Where do your best customers come from, and have you scaled that? If you could do only one thing this year and it had to double the business, what would it be? What happened when you increased spend? What breaks if we do more? Is this new idea better than simply spending more on what works?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

List every move you are considering, then work through these.

1. **More:** Which channel or offer already works, and what would 2x or 10x of it look like?
2. **Better:** Which step of that working path is furthest below where it should be?
3. **Score:** Give each move 1–10 for impact, confidence and ease. Multiply and sort.
4. **Order:** Re-sort so offer and price come before conversion, conversion before volume, and volume before anything new.
5. **Protect:** Write what you will not touch (working channels, the core product, a healthy sales process).
6. **Cut:** Keep the top two or three moves. Put the rest on a dated idea list.
