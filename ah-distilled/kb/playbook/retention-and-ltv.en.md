## The gist {#gist}

In several lectures Alex calls retention the most valuable property a business can have. Retained customers stack year on year and growth compounds. Without retention you are permanently in the business of replacing customers, and a business that depends only on new sales dies when its channel breaks. Since the cost of reaching a given customer is roughly the same for every competitor, the lasting edge is the lifetime value you build on top.

His order is consistent: plug the leaks, then pour in more. Scaling acquisition into a leaking bucket just scales the problems. And when he digs into churn, the cause is usually upstream of the product: weak onboarding, customers who don't use what they bought, or a sales promise that didn't match reality.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Churn** = cancels this month ÷ active clients · **LTV** = price ÷ churn · **LTGP** (lifetime gross profit) = LTV × gross margin
**Ceiling:** new sales per month × LTV is roughly where revenue stops growing.
</div>

## How it works {#how}

### Why it is worth more than acquisition

- **The ceiling moves.** With 100 new sales a month and an LTV of about $7.7K, revenue tops out around $770K a month in one of his worked examples. Halve churn and the ceiling doubles without one extra sale.
- **You can pay more per customer.** At a fixed LTV:CAC target, doubling LTV doubles what you can afford to acquire a customer. In one portfolio company, LTV rising from about $2,000 to $3,800 took the business from about $2M a month to about $2M a week.
- **It is often easier.** He frequently finds dropping churn far easier than multiplying acquisition. He would rather keep the same 300 customers than sell 900 a year to stay at 300.

### Fix the leak first, but read it correctly

If clients drop after a couple of quarters, fix that before spending more on the front end. Two cautions he adds: young programmes (under 90 days or six months) show inflated churn, so don't overhaul everything because month one looks bad; and if retention is already strong, put the effort into acquisition rather than reinventing the product.

### Where churn happens

Across a large membership platform he describes a stable shape: over 20% churn in month one, a second drop around month three (about 10%), and about 2% a month after month six. So he reframes churn work as getting people past day 30, past day 90 and past month six:

| Window | What has to happen |
|---|---|
| Month one | Activation, onboarding, expectations set, the right promises made |
| Day 90 | A real outcome or a visible change in behaviour |
| Month six | Customers connected to each other or embedded in the routine |

### Activation and onboarding

The activation point is what your longest-staying customers did early. If you don't know it, study them and point the whole early experience at that behaviour. His examples: a first $2,000 sale within seven days for a gym licensee, 5+ lbs lost in week one, a trainer's first five clients. If there is no onboarding at all, he adds it first: one-on-one where possible, restate the customer's goal and why they want it, deliver a quick win within 24 hours, introduce one named contact. In one SaaS case, onboarding added two to three months to LTV.

### Churn as consumption and expectations

- **Usage predicts cancellation.** Low renewal is usually preceded by low consumption. Track usage and act on the first drop.
- **Overwhelm drives churn.** Paying for five things and using one feels like 20% value. Removing unused inclusions raises perceived value.
- **Promises set the gap.** High promises raise close rates and lower retention. Set modest expectations and over-deliver; fix the gap by rescripting sales rather than adding delivery.
- **Stated exit reasons mislead.** "We already got the value" usually hides poor onboarding and communication. He prescribes frequent updates that tell clients what you did for them, without asking for anything.

### Billing cadence

At each charge, customers judge value over the period since the last charge. Longer billing periods smooth over bad months. He cites a study with about 10.7% monthly churn on monthly billing, about 5% on quarterly and about 2% on annual. So he makes annual the default (about 16% off, with status perks), charges the one-time value up front and keeps the recurring fee small ("big head, long tail"), and prices the recurring fee at what customers would still pay in their worst month.

### Talk to the people who left and the people who stayed

Message every canceller and tag the reason (price, overwhelm, not using, missing feature, not as expected). Every ~20 cancels, fix the top one or two; don't react to a single answer. Ask the longest-tenured customers why they stay: that answer is your retention engine. Tell customers at signup that cancelling includes an exit conversation, then offer to credit what they've paid toward staying, toward a tier that fixes their real problem, or a lighter version without the feature they don't use.

### Referral ask and upsell at the moment of need

Ask for referrals at the activation point. The customer's gratitude fades quickly while you remember the favour as bigger than it was, so ask early. After activation he wants a testimonial, a referral, then ascension. Selling customers the next thing is itself a retention tactic: if you don't meet their urge to buy again, someone else will. The two upsells that work almost anywhere are more of what they bought or more help with it, offered right after purchase. Renewals get pitched at several points (week four, milestones, halfway, last chance).

### Benchmarks he states

Treat these as his rules of thumb, each from its own context: B2B annual client retention above 80% (accounting firms, private equity buyers); 80% annual retention ≈ five years of revenue per customer, 50% ≈ two; best-in-class prosumer 12-month retention 60%+; if fewer than 7 in 10 members stay each month, retention is your highest-return lever; service churn rarely goes below 1–2% a month; SMB services at $1,500–3,000 a month stick about four to six months.

### Portfolio SaaS company, churn rising after a launch

He reports 61% fewer cancellations, ascensions up from 20% to 48% with one-on-one onboarding, and about $2M saved in six months. Lowering the promises did not hurt close rates.

### Online communities and gyms

On his community platform, one group that deleted content cut monthly churn from about 30% to 5%. His own community cut three weekly calls to one after overwhelm topped the cancel reasons. In his gyms, seven stacked tactics (onboarding, unlockables, exit interviews, attendance tracking and others) took churn from 11% to 6% a month.

### B2B consultancy with retainer churn

A ~$1.1M bar consultancy lost retainer clients after a couple of quarters. His diagnosis: value wasn't being shown. Fix churn first by showing clients their numbers every month, before putting more into the front end.

### Invented: Qlik managed analytics service

30 manufacturing clients at €1,500 a month started the year; 18 were still paying twelve months later. That is 60% annual retention, about 2.5 years and €45,000 revenue per client. Interviews show the stayers ran a management meeting from the dashboard within the first month. Onboarding is rebuilt around that meeting, with the referral ask right after it. At 80% retention, lifetime revenue per client would be about €90,000.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Scaling a leaking bucket.** More ad spend into high churn just buys more people to lose.
- **Measuring LTV as revenue.** He wants lifetime gross profit. Revenue LTV can make an unprofitable channel look healthy.
- **Cutting price to stop churn.** His own cut didn't change churn, and in one case a recurring price drop of about a sixth changed nothing.
- **Adding features to keep people.** Unused inclusions widen the gap between paid and consumed and raise cancellations.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He calculates churn and LTV first.** Most owners don't know either. He works out the plateau before talking about growth.
- **He fixes retention before acquisition,** unless retention is already strong, in which case he sends the effort to acquisition.
- **He starts with onboarding.** If there is none, it is the first fix; then unlockables, exit interviews and usage tracking, one tactic at a time.
- **He defines activation from the best customers** and points the first 30 days at it.
- **He makes annual the default** and separates one-time value from the recurring fee.
- **He places the referral ask and the upsell at the moment of need,** right after activation and right after purchase.
- **Questions he would ask:** How many customers cancel each month, out of how many active? What is your price and gross margin? How many of last year's customers are still paying, and how much of last year's revenue? What does onboarding look like? What is your activation point? Why did the last 20 leave, and why do your longest customers stay? Where exactly does the referral ask happen?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Use your client list from the past 12 months.

1. **Numbers:** Monthly churn, LTV and LTGP. What is your revenue ceiling at today's new-sales rate?
2. **Cohort:** Of clients active a year ago, what share still pays? Compare with his 80% B2B rule of thumb.
3. **Activation:** What did your five longest-staying clients do in their first 30 days?
4. **Leavers:** List the reasons of your last 20 cancellations. Which one or two will you fix first?
5. **Billing:** Could annual or quarterly billing become the default for new clients?
6. **Ask:** At which moment will you ask for a referral and offer the next purchase?
