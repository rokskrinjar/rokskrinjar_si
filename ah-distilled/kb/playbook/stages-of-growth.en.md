## The gist {#gist}

Alex treats growth as a series of levels, like a video game. Each one has a different boss, and the skills that beat the last boss don't beat the next one. That is why he places almost every hotseat business on his head-count-based scaling roadmap before giving advice: the right move at 5 people is often the wrong move at 50.

Two rules run through all of it. First, at each stage the thing you are holding on to is the thing you have to give up next: delivery, then selling, then marketing, then leading. Second, better before bigger. He refuses to help a business multiply a unit that doesn't work yet, because you only scale the problem.

<div class="formula" markdown="1">
**Nail it** (profitable unit, runs without you) → **squeeze it** (more of what works) → **only then multiply it** (new location, product, channel)
</div>

## How it works {#how}

### The stages and what changes

He uses revenue bands, with slightly different cut-offs across videos. These are his rough rules of thumb, not fixed laws.

| Stage | Founder's job | Typical constraint | What to give up |
|---|---|---|---|
| $0–100k | Sell something to someone | Getting any reliable flow of customers | Distractions; he notes founder personal issues often derail the $0–1M stage |
| $100k–1M | One product, one avatar, one channel | Founder's own selling and delivery hours | Fulfilment, so you can pass $1M |
| $1–10M | From doing to teaching | Founder-led selling hits its ceiling around $1M; leaders are needed from about $3M | Selling, then running the sales team |
| $10–30M | From managing to leading | Margin compression, channel dependency, lack of leaders | Marketing and direct leadership |
| $30M+ | Deciding and advising | Founder can no longer push growth personally | Leading the culture yourself |

In one lecture he describes the founder's role moving from doing to managing to leading to deciding to advising. The meta-skill in sales changes too: close, then teach others to close, then keep many closers, then recruit someone who can do all of that. He admits he got stuck in the mid-$30Ms for three years because past about three layers of hierarchy he could no longer drive growth himself.

On the head-count roadmap he mentions specific placements: around 10 to 19 employees is the "productize to optimize" phase, where service businesses typically struggle with churn (nothing else to sell) and expensive leads. He also notes that one company can sit at different stages for different functions.

### First hires and the doer-to-teacher shift

The first five or so hires are extensions of the founder, not departments. He hires in reverse order of value: admin and support first, then a setter or salesperson, marketing help, a fractional bookkeeper and a tech person. The test is the gap between what your hour is worth and what replacing it costs.

The identity change is the hard part. A founder who is the best salesperson or best practitioner has to become the best teacher of that skill, judged by the team's results. His "Talent Grid" names the three levels: individual contributor (what), manager (how, by teaching) and leader (who, building the machine that builds the machine). Strong doers often fail as managers, and few managers become leaders.

### Nail it before you scale it

This is one of his most repeated rules. Before a second location, product line or business, the first must be profitable and run without the founder. In one talk he sets the bar at six months of growth without you; in another, a three-month absence the business survives and grows through. Opening early doubles debt and overhead while halving talent, and he names over-expansion as a top cause of failure.

He also defines over-expansion precisely: selling to more people than you have hired and trained good people to serve. The fix is to slow sales, recruit and train, cut your own draw and accept a profit dip until delivery, churn and referrals recover.

### Squeeze before you add

Before multiplying, check unused capacity. In several hotseats he shows that doubling revenue per existing location reaches the goal with almost no extra fixed cost. One retail chain grew existing locations about 4x before opening new ones. Up to about $1M he wants one offer, one avatar, one channel; below about $10M he doesn't worry about channel dependency, above it he wants a second channel.

### Better before bigger

In mass-market models, rising ad costs, colder audiences and heavier infrastructure squeeze margin as you grow. His illustrative numbers: 40% margin falling to 30% from $1M to $10M, and toward 10% by $30M if nothing changes. The counterweight is word of mouth, so somewhere around $1M to $5M he wants the founder to pause scaling and fix customer experience.

Fixed costs barely move when an existing site doubles, so new locations may be unnecessary. He also flagged "founder magic": the first site carried the founder's personal pull that the others lacked.

### Retail service chain with a new sales process (lecture)

A low-wage, multi-location team got a simple process built into the point-of-sale system so no step could be skipped. Average revenue per customer went from about $200 to about $800, and only then did the chain grow from 14 to 32 locations in about 18 months. Packaging and selling were fixed before capacity was added.

### Founder-dentist at capacity (hotseat)

One founder-doctor handled every high-ticket case, with a booking delay of two to four weeks and close rates of 80–90%. Associates took the simpler half of visits, the founder became the architect and teacher, prices rose and payment moved up front. In the follow-up, average ticket rose from about $28K to about $44K and no new location was needed.

## Common mistakes {#mistakes}

- **Multiplying a broken unit.** A second location or product line on top of mediocre unit economics scales the problem and halves your talent.
- **Holding on to the old job.** Founders keep doing what they are best at, so their strength becomes the bottleneck.
- **Promoting doers into leadership by default.** Strong individual contributors often fail as managers; assess each level separately.
- **Starting something new to escape a plateau.** He sees most plateaus as a founder skill gap, not a vehicle problem. A new business caps at the same level.
- **Growing for ego.** Adding reps or locations because a revenue number feels important, without the margin and team to support it.

## How Alex would apply it {#alex}

- **He places the business first.** Headcount, revenue and who does what decide which stage's rules apply.
- **He names what the founder must give up next,** and frames it as the next level rather than a loss of control. Accountability stays with the founder.
- **He blocks expansion until the unit is nailed:** profitable, payback known, and growing for months without the owner.
- **He squeezes existing capacity first:** revenue per location, price, close rate, then new units.
- **He accepts a short-term profit hit to hire leaders** when the goal is beyond the founder's own hours, and treats each higher salary band as new territory.
- **He pauses scaling around $1–5M to fix experience** when CAC rises faster than ad costs, a sign of negative word of mouth.
- **Questions he would ask:** How many people work here? Does the core unit make money at full capacity? Does it run without you for three months? What are you holding on to? If you doubled customers, what would break? What do you actually want: many locations, one big one, or an exit?

## Worksheet {#worksheet}

Answer in writing for your business as it is today.

1. **Stage:** What is your revenue band and headcount? Which column of the table are you in?
2. **Holding on:** Which job (delivery, selling, marketing, leading) do you still do that the next stage needs you to give up?
3. **Unit test:** Is your core unit profitable, with known payback, and has it grown for three months without you?
4. **Squeeze:** How much more could the current unit produce before you add a new location, product or channel?
5. **Level check:** Which of your key people are doers, which teach, and which build systems? Who could move up?
