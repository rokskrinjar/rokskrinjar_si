Alex's Playbook · Hotseat cases

Thirty-six real situations from agencies, consultancies, law and accounting firms, staffing, B2B services and real-estate and insurance businesses, with what Alex diagnosed and what he told the owner to do.Šestintrideset resničnih situacij iz agencij, svetovalnih podjetij, odvetniških in računovodskih pisarn, kadrovskih in B2B storitev ter nepremičninskih in zavarovalniških podjetij, s tem, kakšno diagnozo je postavil Alex in kaj je lastniku svetoval.

The gist

Service owners usually come to Alex with a growth goal and a vague sense that they need more leads. In most of these cases the business is underpriced, priced by the hour or by cost-plus, and sold to anyone who asks. His first move is to repackage the work as an outcome with a clear price, often a fixed fee with a performance component, an anchor tier far above it and a cheaper downsell below. He reads close rates near 100% as proof the price is too low, and he expects some fall in close rate when the price rises.

The second pattern is acquisition that depends on something the owner doesn't control: vendor referrals, a handful of partners, the founder's network, one ad platform. He adds an owned channel, usually outbound to a defined list (top agents by volume, high-spending advertisers, partner types), and keeps doing more of whatever already works before trying something new. Partnerships and affiliates get real attention in his advice; he wants them activated, measured per partner and given an offer that changes their behaviour.

The third pattern is churn. Agencies and recurring services lose clients in months three to six, and he traces it to the wrong clients and a weak start more than to the work itself. He tightens the avatar to those who activate, adds qualification friction before the sale, makes the first 30 days deliver a visible win and keeps communicating what was done. For exits he looks at what a buyer would pay for: recurring revenue, multiple channels and no dependence on the founder, sometimes reached by rolling up the clients instead of selling the service firm.

Cases

B2B hospitality consultancy · ~$1.1M/yr · $30K programme sold cold, LTV:CAC 1.7

  • Situation. About 11% net after 4.5 years. A $30K 90-day programme is sold to strangers from paid ads at about $11.8K CAC. Retainer clients churn. Goal: triple.
  • Diagnosis. A high-ticket offer pushed at cold traffic, weak ads, a high-friction funnel, and churn because value isn't shown.
  • Advice. Call-out ads with pain hooks and a lead magnet; a two-page funnel with a VSL above the scheduler; a $5.8K audit as the front end, closed in one call by the founder; roll that credit into a quarterly retainer, with the big fee waived for annual commitment; show clients their numbers monthly.
  • Why. A lower first step turns strangers into customers, who are far more likely to buy again; a fast win funds the bigger purchase.
  • Source. Building a $3,000,000 Business for a Stranger in 57 Mins (04:47)

Event planning and AV company · ~$44K average deal, ~1 deal a week · thin margins

  • Situation. The owner closes about 40%; two reps underperform; line-item pricing is invented case by case.
  • Diagnosis. Mispriced, ad hoc offer (a margin problem) plus a demand problem because the reps produce little.
  • Advice. Build a cost-based quote generator at 5–10x cost with a price lock and a prepay discount. Turn the reps into setters for the owner, with daily check-ins and role play. VSLs at three touchpoints. Ask for multi-event commitments. Go after B2B conferences via booths and speaking. Max out LinkedIn outreach plus a dialer.
  • Why. Don't pour demand into thin margins; doubling appointments for an owner who closes 40% changes the business fast. Follow-up: pipeline up about 200%, one-year clients converted to three years, one booth produced a roughly $500K deal.
  • Source. How to Get Rich for Service-Based Businesses (Thank Me Later) (15:08)

Music-industry law firm with side lines · ~$300K/yr, losing ~$100K · six months of cash

  • Situation. Legal work at $250 an hour (about $1,500 per client), plus a $500 course and contract software burning about $7K a month. Large social audiences, but no clear call to action.
  • Diagnosis. Demand is sprayed across three offers, hourly billing underprices the work, and the software loses most of the money.
  • Advice. Kill or sell the software. Make education free to feed services. Route every call to action to DMs and qualify new followers. Sell an outcome package at about $5,800 plus a small share of deals, with a $25K anchor and a $2–3K DIY downsell, sold on a scripted call.
  • Why. Services already earn; about 800 new followers a month at 5% close and $6K could mean roughly $240K a month.
  • Source. I Confronted a Stranger With a Failing Business… (00:00); I Blew Up a Business Through 3 Small Changes (Just Copy Me) (00:00)

Luxury personal styling · ~$309K/yr, 42% margin · strong economics, low volume

  • Situation. About 40 recurring clients, about one churn a year; $8.5K onboarding, then $2K a month. About 1,800 paid clicks to a form with no headline gave 8 sales. Six partners, found with about 40 cold emails, bring a third of revenue.
  • Diagnosis. Conversion is the bottleneck, and the partner channel is barely tapped. No price anchor, continuity isn't the default.
  • Advice. A style-assessment lead magnet, a short VSL and BANT questions; qualified leads get a call, the rest a freebie. Send 20 partner emails a day and hold partner events. Add a 10x top-tier anchor and default continuity after onboarding.
  • Why. The product already converts on calls, so the cheap gains are upstream; partners earned about $2,500 per email sent, better than ads.
  • Source. I Blew Up A Secret Business To Prove It's Not Luck (00:47); Building a $1,000,000 Business for a Stranger in 56 Mins (07:59)

Agencies serving local businesses · $1,500/month retainer · ~0.5:1 return in the first 30 days

  • Situation. The offer looks the same as every competitor's retainer.
  • Diagnosis. A commoditised offer.
  • Advice. Restructure it: one payment up front, the agency works the leads, then the client pays per person who shows up.
  • Why. Response rate rose 2.5x, appointments went from 40 to 100, close rate from 16% to 37% at four times the price, and cash collected rose about 22x on the same ad spend.

New marketing agency for home-service SMBs · $0 to ~$500K in four months · goal eight figures

  • Situation. About $2K a month per client, about ten sales a month.
  • Diagnosis. The price sits in the small-business churn zone (clients stay four to six months on average). Growth will stall near $100K a month as churn catches new sales, CAC rises and margins shrink.
  • Advice. Choose one: go much cheaper and automated ($300–500 a month with visible deliverables such as map rankings and review management), or move up-market to sophisticated clients.
  • Why. Cheap, visible services can stick 30–40 months with low CAC; high-touch work only sticks with clients who already have a working model.

Subscription service · ~$400K/month · 100 new sales a month, didn't know churn

  • Situation. 380 clients at $1,000 a month, growing fast; churn and LTV untracked.
  • Diagnosis. About 13% monthly churn caps the business near $770K a month; growth will flatten.
  • Advice. Put the effort into cutting churn toward about 3% before adding acquisition.
  • Why. At 3% churn LTV rises to about $33K and the ceiling to about $3.3M a month, which is far easier than 10x lead flow. At 13% a month, under 20% of clients remain after a year, and buyers won't value that.
  • Source. "My business has stopped growing..what should I do?" (01:36)

Website-as-a-service for SMBs · ~$20M/yr, ~$3.6M EBITDA · afraid AI will kill the product

  • Situation. About $450 a month per client, 100% of growth from outbound cold calling, clients stay about 29 months.
  • Diagnosis. The fear isn't in the metrics. The real risks are a single channel and a thin margin from being people-heavy.
  • Advice. Don't rebuild the product. Add paid inbound. Spend about six months rebuilding workflows with AI and cut headcount by about half to roughly double margin, then accept a negative first quarter on CAC, knowing the 29-month back end.
  • Why. Margin funds acquisition without touching price, and customers who would build their own site with AI were never buyers.
  • Source. Watch This If You Have a Service Business (15:07)

CFO and tax advisory · ~$2.9M/yr · paused new sales, wants to sell courses

  • Situation. Growing 30–35% a year on organic demand alone, but sales are paused because delivery is hard. The owner has made courses and books and wants to sell them.
  • Diagnosis. Supply-constrained. A course business would be a new and equally hard business.
  • Advice. Resume selling. Add offshore staff for two to three times leverage per person. Build a data layer, then AI. Keep courses and books as future marketing assets.
  • Why. Zero new sales guarantees zero growth; fix the constraint you have before creating demand you can't serve.
  • Source. Watch This If You Have a Service Business (21:24)

Virtual CPA firm · ~$3M/yr, ~$1.1M net · ~70% annual retention

  • Situation. Quarterly fees from about $1,950. Departing clients say they have learned the strategies.
  • Diagnosis. Bad onboarding and too little communication; value isn't really being lost.
  • Advice. Audit the handoff from sales to onboarding, keep notes on each client, communicate often about the work done and train accountants on client touchpoints. Then hire a top tax specialist.
  • Why. He targets retention above 80%; visible care signals competent work.

Enterprise software-implementation consultancy · ~$1.4M/yr · thinking of starting over

  • Situation. About 12 clients, almost all from vendor referrals as a subcontractor: capped hourly rate, about 17% margin against about 83% when selling direct. Considering a new healthcare business with a spouse.

Lead and sales service for new real-estate wholesalers · CAC ~$5K, LTV ~$35K · very low close rate

  • Situation. Many prospects can't afford the first payment.
  • Diagnosis. Unqualified lead flow drains the sales team and the close rate.
  • Advice. Define the avatar from the highest-spending, longest-staying customers (demographics, quantifiables such as income, geography, behaviour in the sales process). Put that friction into ads, landing pages, form drop-downs and the VSL. Send the best leads to sales and the rest to an automated self-liquidating offer.
  • Why. Qualification raises cost per call but lowers CAC and raises LTV. He couldn't recall a case where it lost him money, and selling to people spending their last dollar hurts ethics, morale and reputation.

B2B licensing for gym owners (Alex's own) · 100+ new gyms a month · poor-fit clients

Patterns

  • Underpriced, hourly or cost-plus (about 9 cases). Hourly legal work, invented line items, capped subcontractor rates, close rates of 84–100%. He moves to an outcome price, often a fixed fee plus a performance share, with a high anchor and a cheaper downsell.
  • Acquisition depends on something the owner doesn't control (about 9 cases). Vendor referrals, a few partners, the founder's network, one ad platform. He adds an owned channel, usually outbound to a defined list, and keeps scaling what already works.
  • Partners and affiliates under-used (about 6 cases). Referral partners bringing a third of revenue from a few emails, community partners, partner-level retention. He scales partner outreach, gives partners an offer that changes behaviour, and measures value per partner.
  • Churn from the wrong clients and a weak start (about 7 cases). Agencies losing clients in months three to six, CPA clients leaving at 70% retention, a subscription at 13% monthly churn. He tightens the avatar, adds qualification, engineers an early win and communicates the work done.
  • Offer mismatch with cold traffic (about 4 cases). High-ticket offers pushed at strangers, commodity retainers. He lowers the first step (audit, front-end offer, pay per show) and ascends later.
  • Price in the churn zone (2–3 cases). Mid-priced SMB services churn fast; he says go cheap and automated with visible deliverables, or go up-market.
  • Founder or partner as the sales problem (about 4 cases). A founder who doesn't know sales, a partner who doesn't sell, an unmotivated hunter stuck farming. He puts the owner back into selling or forces the partner conversation.
  • Sellability (about 5 cases). Consulting and talent businesses are hard to sell; he points to recurring revenue, multiple channels, no founder dependency, or rolling up the clients.
  • Supply before demand (2–3 cases). Paused sales or a solo operator without margin; he fixes delivery leverage or price before adding leads.

How Alex would apply it

First questions. 1. How do you price: hourly, cost-plus, or by outcome? What is your close rate? 2. Where does each new client come from, and how much of it do you control? 3. How many clients reach month six, and what do the ones who stay have in common? 4. What happens in the first 30 days, and when does the client see a visible win? 5. If you sold tomorrow, what would a buyer actually be paying for?

Default moves by situation. - Close rate very high, hourly billing: repackage as an outcome with a fixed fee, add a performance component, an anchor tier above and a downsell below; accept a lower close rate. - All work from referrals or one partner: build outbound to a defined list (top performers in the target market, big spenders), and run a partner programme with an offer partners care about. - Churn in months three to six: define the avatar from your best long-stay clients, qualify on the form and the call, set expectations up front, make onboarding personal and show the results regularly. - Selling high-ticket to cold traffic: add a cheaper front-end step (audit, assessment, pay per show) and roll its value into the main offer. - Too many offers or units: keep the one with demand and margin, turn the others into marketing assets or close them. - Delivery capacity full: raise price first, add offshore staff and AI to the workflow, and keep selling. - Planning an exit: add a second channel, remove the founder from the front, raise retention, or roll up the clients you serve.

Bistvo

Lastniki storitvenih podjetij običajno pridejo k Alexu s ciljem rasti in nejasnim občutkom, da potrebujejo več leadov. V večini teh primerov ima podjetje prenizke cene, zaračunava po urah ali po načelu strošek plus pribitek in prodaja vsakomur, ki vpraša. Njegova prva poteza je, da delo zapakira kot izid z jasno ceno, pogosto kot fiksni znesek z delom, vezanim na uspešnost, s sidrom v obliki veliko dražje ravni nad njim in cenejšo različico (downsell) pod njim. Stopnje zaključka blizu 100 % bere kot dokaz, da je cena prenizka, in pričakuje, da bo zaključek ob dvigu cene nekoliko padel.

Drugi vzorec je pridobivanje, ki je odvisno od nečesa, česar lastnik ne nadzoruje: priporočil dobavitelja, peščice partnerjev, ustanoviteljeve mreže, ene oglaševalske platforme. Doda lasten kanal, običajno outbound na določen seznam (najboljši agenti po obsegu, oglaševalci z velikimi proračuni, tipi partnerjev), in še naprej dela več tistega, kar že deluje, preden poskusi kaj novega. Partnerstva in affiliate partnerji dobijo v njegovih nasvetih resno pozornost; želi jih aktivirati, meriti po posameznem partnerju in jim dati ponudbo, ki spremeni njihovo vedenje.

Tretji vzorec je odliv strank (churn). Agencije in ponavljajoče se storitve izgubljajo stranke med tretjim in šestim mesecem, on pa vzrok išče bolj v napačnih strankah in šibkem začetku kot v samem delu. Zoži avatar na tiste, ki se aktivirajo, pred prodajo doda trenje za kvalifikacijo, poskrbi, da prvih 30 dni prinese viden uspeh, in redno sporoča, kaj je bilo narejeno. Pri izstopu gleda, za kaj bi kupec plačal: ponavljajoče se prihodke, več kanalov in neodvisnost od ustanovitelja, včasih dosežene z združevanjem strank namesto s prodajo storitvenega podjetja.

Vzorci

  • Prenizke cene, po urah ali strošek plus pribitek (okoli 9 primerov). Urno pravno delo, izmišljene postavke, omejene postavke podizvajalcev, stopnje zaključka 84–100 %. Preide na ceno za izid, pogosto fiksni znesek plus delež uspešnosti, z visokim sidrom in cenejšo različico.
  • Pridobivanje je odvisno od nečesa, česar lastnik ne nadzoruje (okoli 9 primerov). Priporočila dobavitelja, nekaj partnerjev, ustanoviteljeva mreža, ena oglaševalska platforma. Doda lasten kanal, običajno outbound na določen seznam, in še naprej povečuje to, kar že deluje.
  • Premalo izkoriščeni partnerji in affiliate partnerji (okoli 6 primerov). Partnerji, ki iz nekaj e-sporočil prinesejo tretjino prihodka, partnerji iz skupnosti, zadrževanje na ravni partnerja. Poveča outreach partnerjem, jim da ponudbo, ki spremeni vedenje, in meri vrednost na partnerja.
  • Odliv zaradi napačnih strank in šibkega začetka (okoli 7 primerov). Agencije, ki izgubljajo stranke med tretjim in šestim mesecem, računovodske stranke pri 70 % zadrževanju, naročnina s 13 % mesečnega odliva. Zoži avatar, doda kvalifikacijo, načrtuje zgodnji uspeh in sporoča opravljeno delo.
  • Ponudba se ne ujema s hladnim prometom (okoli 4 primeri). Drage ponudbe, potisnjene neznancem, generične mesečne pogodbe. Zniža prvi korak (revizija, uvodna ponudba, plačilo za prihod na termin) in kasneje stranko pripelje višje.
  • Cena v coni odliva (2–3 primeri). Storitve za mala podjetja v srednjem cenovnem razredu hitro izgubljajo stranke; svetuje bodisi poceni in avtomatizirano z vidnimi rezultati bodisi premik navzgor.
  • Ustanovitelj ali partner kot prodajni problem (okoli 4 primeri). Ustanovitelj, ki ne pozna prodaje, partner, ki ne prodaja, nemotiviran lovec pri obdelovanju polja. Lastnika vrne v prodajo ali izsili pogovor s partnerjem.
  • Prodajljivost (okoli 5 primerov). Svetovalna podjetja in podjetja s talenti je težko prodati; pokaže na ponavljajoče se prihodke, več kanalov, neodvisnost od ustanovitelja ali združevanje strank.
  • Najprej ponudba, nato povpraševanje (2–3 primeri). Ustavljena prodaja ali samostojni izvajalec brez marže; najprej uredi vzvod pri izvedbi ali ceno, šele nato doda leade.

Kako bi to uporabil Alex

Prva vprašanja. 1. Kako oblikuješ ceno: po urah, strošek plus pribitek ali po izidu? Kakšna je tvoja stopnja zaključka? 2. Od kod pride vsaka nova stranka in koliko tega nadzoruješ? 3. Koliko strank pride do šestega meseca in kaj imajo skupnega tiste, ki ostanejo? 4. Kaj se zgodi v prvih 30 dneh in kdaj stranka vidi viden uspeh? 5. Če bi jutri prodal, za kaj bi kupec dejansko plačal?

Privzete poteze po situaciji. - Zelo visok zaključek, obračun po urah: zapakiraj kot izid s fiksnim zneskom, dodaj del, vezan na uspešnost, sidro nad njim in cenejšo različico pod njim; sprejmi nižji zaključek. - Vse delo iz priporočil ali od enega partnerja: zgradi outbound na določen seznam (najboljši na ciljnem trgu, veliki porabniki) in vodi partnerski program s ponudbo, ki partnerjem nekaj pomeni. - Odliv med tretjim in šestim mesecem: avatar določi po najboljših dolgoletnih strankah, kvalificiraj v obrazcu in na klicu, pričakovanja postavi vnaprej, uvajanje naj bo osebno in redno pokaži rezultate. - Draga ponudba hladnemu prometu: dodaj cenejši uvodni korak (revizija, ocena, plačilo za prihod na termin) in njegovo vrednost prenesi v glavno ponudbo. - Preveč ponudb ali enot: obdrži tisto s povpraševanjem in maržo, ostale spremeni v marketinška sredstva ali jih zapri. - Zmogljivost izvedbe je polna: najprej dvigni ceno, v delovni proces dodaj sodelavce iz tujine in UI ter nadaljuj s prodajo. - Načrtuješ izstop: dodaj drugi kanal, umakni ustanovitelja s pročelja, dvigni zadrževanje ali združi stranke, ki jim strežeš.
