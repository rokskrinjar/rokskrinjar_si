# DIY woodworking knowledge base: softwoods from a Slovenian sawmill

Knowledge base for a beginner DIY advisor (guide, chatbot, order configurator) built on Solis Timber's product range: dry sawn, planed, radial (quarter-sawn), fresh sawn, glulam BSH GL24h, upcoming KVH, plus by-products.

Researched 2026-10-28. Source tags such as **[S5]** point to the source list at the end.

**How to read the figures**
- A number with a source tag is quoted from that source.
- **Rule of thumb** marks practitioner guidance or my own estimate. It is not a measured or standardised value.
- **Arithmetic** blocks show how a quantity was calculated, so it can be checked and reused in a configurator.
- Where sources disagree, both values are given.

**Gaps to know about**
- I could not reach primary Holzforschung Austria (HFA) publications. The species data come from the equivalent German institute sheets: LWF Bayern / Holzforschung München, based on DIN 68364 and Grosser & Zimmer [S5–S8]. Moisture and coating data come from the USDA Wood Handbook [S11] and Swedish Wood [S9, S10].
- The EN standards themselves (EN 350, EN 335, EN 338, EN 14080, EN 1912) are paywalled. Their values are taken from manufacturer, institute or trade summaries, and every value carries its source.

## 1. The four species for hobbyists

### 1.1 Key numbers

| Property | Spruce (Norway, *Picea abies*), SL: smreka | Silver fir (*Abies alba*), SL: jelka | Scots pine (*Pinus sylvestris*), SL: rdeči bor | European larch (*Larix decidua*), SL: macesen |
|---|---|---|---|---|

The Brinell values above are DIN 68364 values cited by LWF. Other teaching tables give slightly different across-grain values for spruce and fir, so treat about 12–16 N/mm² for both as the realistic band.

**What this means for a beginner**
- Spruce and fir are soft. You can dent them with a fingernail or a dropped tool, and they are easy to cut, plane and nail.
- Larch is about twice as hard as spruce (Janka 3,290 vs 1,680 N), and pine sits between them.
- For comparison, hard maple, the classic cutting-board wood, is about 6,450 N (1,450 lbf) on the Wood Database. That is roughly four times spruce.

### 1.2 Workability, glue and finishes

**Spruce**
- Saws, planes, routes, drills and sands easily by hand or machine.
- Easy to nail and screw. Glues without problems with PVA (thermoplastic) and thermoset glues [S5].
- Problem features: knots, resin pockets (Harzgallen), spiral grain and compression wood. Compression wood (reaction wood) is the main cause of strong distortion [S5].
- Takes paint, glaze, oil, wax and lacquer well. Cut-open resin pockets can disturb film formation and need pre-treatment [S5].
- Staining views differ:
  - LWF calls spruce excellent to stain, with the stain bringing out the earlywood/latewood contrast [S5].
  - The Wood Database reports blotchy results with pigment and dye stains and suggests a sanding sealer, gel stain or toner [S1].
  - Advice for the bot: **always test the stain on an offcut first.**
- Low extractive content (2.3 %). Iron does not corrode in contact with it but gives a faint grey stain in damp wood. Copper and brass do not stain it [S5].

**Fir**
- Works like spruce. It is a little more brittle and "woolly" and splinters more easily, but works without real difficulty [S6].
- Glues well, including wetwood [S6].
- Being resin-free, it is "an excellent substrate for coatings" [S6].
- After drying it is stable and "moves less than spruce" [S6].
- Preferred where spruce resin would be a nuisance [S6]:
  - sauna interior lining
  - containers exposed to acids and alkalis
  - baking boards, because it is taste- and odour-neutral
- Usually traded together with spruce as "Fichte/Tanne" (Fi/Ta) [S5, S6].

**Pine**
- Easy to work. Resin gums up tools [S8].
- Glues well, except very resinous pieces [S8].
- Takes any finish. Very resinous boards should be sorted out, and you should de-resin before staining or when the piece will be heated [S8].
- Moist sapwood is very prone to **blue stain**. It is cosmetic only and does not reduce strength [S8].
- Extractives about 9 %. Iron gives a faint grey stain when damp [S8].

**Larch**
- Works well but resin can overheat saw blades and make them wander [S7].
- **Pre-drill for heavy nails and screws** to avoid splitting [S7].
- Glues fine. Takes lacquer, glaze, matt finishes and wax well, but **stains poorly** [S7].
- Resin pockets and dark knots can disturb film finishes [S7].
- Slightly acidic (pH 4.7). In damp conditions iron reacts with its tannins and gives grey to blue-black stains, so **use stainless fasteners outdoors** [S7].
- Sanding can leave dips because soft earlywood and hard latewood wear at different rates [S4].

### 1.3 Durability: indoor vs outdoor

EN 350 rates the heartwood's natural resistance to decay fungi, mainly in ground contact. The classes are 1 very durable, 2 durable, 3 moderately durable, 4 slightly durable and 5 not durable [S9].

**Spruce**
- Class 4, and "rather quickly perishable" in ground contact [S5].
- Above ground it does better than the class suggests. It takes up liquid water only slowly, so with good detailing it reaches decay-level moisture (above 20 %) only slowly [S5, S9].
- That is why spruce is widely used for façade cladding in Austria and Bavaria [S5].

**Fir**
- Class 4 [S6].
- Considered better than spruce where wood is permanently or alternately wet [S6].

**Pine heartwood**
- Class 3–4 in ground contact, but "good" resistance when weathered above ground [S8].
- **Sapwood is highly susceptible** to fungi and insects [S8].

**Larch heartwood**
- Normatively class 3–4, but more weather-resistant than all other native softwoods [S7].
- Under DIN 68800-1 its heartwood may be used in use classes 2 and 3.1 without preventive chemical treatment [S7].
- Larch sapwood is class 5 [S7].

**Lifetime of heartwood stakes (20×50×500 mm) in ground contact, Swedish data [S9]:** spruce under 5 years, pine and larch 5–10 years.

**Use classes (EN 335 / DIN 68800, German GK)**

| Use class | Situation | Beginner example | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| UC1 / GK1 | Indoors, dry | Shelf, bed, bookshelf | Any species, no protection needed [S19] |
| UC2 / GK2 | Covered, occasional condensation or wetting | Carport beams under the roof, firewood shed frame | Spruce/fir fine if it can dry out |
| UC3.1 / GK3.1 | Outdoors above ground, water runs off and dries quickly | Garden bench, cladding, pergola with good detailing | Spruce/fir with good detailing and a coating; larch or Douglas heartwood if in doubt [S20] |
| UC3.2 / GK3.2 | Outdoors above ground, water stays (horizontal surfaces, joints) | Decking, the top of a horizontal beam | Load-bearing decking is usually GK3.2, or GK4 if dirt collects [S20]. Spruce here is short-lived. |
| UC4 / GK4 | Ground or freshwater contact | Raised bed walls, fence posts in soil | Spruce under 5 years [S9]. Avoid soil contact by design, or accept the part as a consumable. |

The German "dry" threshold is a mean wood moisture **below 20 %**. Keeping wood below 20 % is the main protection against decay fungi, and using kiln-dried wood is the simplest protection against insects [S12, S20].

### 1.4 Typical defects and what they mean

| Defect | Seen in | Meaning for the DIYer |
|---|---|---|
| Knots (sound, loose, black "pin knots") | All. Larch has many small black "nail knots" [S7]. | Loose knots fall out. Knots near an edge weaken beams. Knots bleed resin and extractives through paint (see 5.1). |
| Resin pockets / pitch (Harzgallen) | Spruce, pine, larch, not fir [S5, S6, S11] | Resin oozes when warm, including through paint. Sun-exposed and sauna parts are worst [S11]. |
| Compression wood (Druckholz, red/hard streaks) | Spruce, pine | Strong warping and lengthwise shrinkage. Graders sort it out for dimension-stable parts [S5]. |
| Spiral grain (Drehwuchs) | Spruce | Twists as it dries [S5]. |
| Pith / boxed heart in the piece | Centre-cut pieces | More splitting and checking. Choose boxed-heart-free (herzfrei) or pith-cut (herzgetrennt); see 2.1. |
| Blue stain (Bläue) | Pine sapwood mainly; also spruce, fir, larch | Cosmetic only. Unlimited blue stain is allowed in structural grades [S8]. A sign the wood was wet. |
| Wetwood (Nasskern) | Fir | Very wet core (up to 220 % MC fresh), sour smell. Dries out; the odour disappears [S6]. |
| Shrinkage checks (cracks) | Thick sections, especially with pith | Normal in solid and glulam timber. For glulam, cracks up to 1/6 of the width (no tension across the grain) are not structurally critical [S17]. |

### 1.5 Quarter-sawn (radial) vs flat-sawn movement

Wood shrinks and swells about **twice as much tangentially (along the growth rings) as radially** (across them):
- spruce: 0.39 vs 0.19 % per 1 % MC change [S5]
- spruce tangential/radial ratio: 2.1 [S1, S5]

A flat-sawn board (rings lying flat) therefore changes width a lot and **cups**. A quarter-sawn or radial board (rings near-vertical) changes width about half as much and stays flat [S11].

**Formula (USDA Wood Handbook) [S11]:** ΔD = D × C × (MC_end − MC_start)
- D is the dimension.
- C is the movement coefficient per 1 % MC. Use the tangential coefficient for flat-sawn width and the radial coefficient for quarter-sawn width.
- The formula is valid between about 6 and 14 % MC.

**Arithmetic: 145 mm wide spruce board drying from 15 % to 8 % MC (7 points)**

| Source of coefficient | Flat-sawn (tangential) | Quarter-sawn (radial) |
|---|---|---|
| LWF/DIN, Norway spruce, 0.39 / 0.19 % [S5] | 145 × 0.0039 × 7 = **4.0 mm** | 145 × 0.0019 × 7 = **1.9 mm** |
| Wood Handbook, North American red/Sitka spruce, 0.274 / 0.130 % [S11] | 145 × 0.00274 × 7 = **2.8 mm** | 145 × 0.0013 × 7 = **1.3 mm** |

Using both sources, expect a 145 mm spruce board to shrink by roughly **3–4 mm flat-sawn or 1.3–2 mm quarter-sawn** when it goes from "outdoor dry" to "heated-room dry".

**Arithmetic: 800 mm tabletop indoors, seasonal swing 8 % → 12 % (4 points)**
- All flat-sawn: 800 × 0.0039 × 4 ≈ **12.5 mm** of seasonal movement.
- Mixed grain, using the average coefficient (0.39 + 0.19) / 2 = 0.29 %: 800 × 0.0029 × 4 ≈ **9.3 mm**.
- Therefore never glue or screw a solid top rigidly to a frame across its width. Use slotted holes, figure-8 fasteners or buttons.

### 1.6 Moisture content: why kiln-dried matters and what to order

**Basic physics**
- Fresh softwood heartwood typically holds 30–50 % MC. Fir wetwood can reach 220 % [S6].
- Wood only shrinks below the **fibre saturation point (about 30 % for softwoods)** [S12].
- It then moves towards the **equilibrium moisture content (EMC)** set by the air's relative humidity (RH). RH is the main driver; temperature matters little [S10].
- Install wood at the moisture content it will have in service, to avoid shrinkage cracks, gaps and distortion [S12].

**Measured and recommended values**

| Situation | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Heated homes, mid Sweden | Average 7.5 % over the year; 7–12 % in summer, **2–6 % in winter** (indoor RH 10–25 %) | [S10] |
| Interior woodwork, furniture, flooring (US most areas) | Average **8 %**, individual pieces 6–10 % | [S11] |
| Softwood for furniture, cabinets, millwork | Dried to 7–9 % | [S11] |
| Interior components, German practice | **8–12 %** | [S13] |
| Floorboards in heated rooms (Swedish delivery target) | 8 % | [S10] |
| Exposed cladding and mouldings in heated rooms | 12 % | [S10] |
| Exterior wood (US most areas) | Average **12 %**, pieces 9–14 % | [S11] |
| Exterior components, German practice | **12–15 %** | [S13] |
| Solid wood and glulam for exterior cladding (Swedish target) | 16 % | [S10] |
| Outdoor EMC in a damp November (Malmö) | about 20 % | [S10] |
| KVH structural timber | 15 ± 3 % | [S14] |
| Maximum for painting or surface treatment | 16 % | [S10] |
| Risk of paint blistering | above 20 % | [S11] |
| Firewood (Slovenian small-combustion rules) | under 20 % | [S29] |

**Practical ordering rule for Solis KD 8–20 %** (rule of thumb derived from the table above)

| Use | Order MC |
|---|---|
| Indoor furniture, shelves, picture frames, tabletops | **8–10 %** |
| Indoor cladding and structural parts in heated rooms | **10–12 %** |
| Covered outdoor structures (carport, pergola, shed frame) and outdoor furniture | **12–16 %** |
| Rough outdoor work (compost bin, firewood store) | **16–20 %**; fresh sawn is acceptable if you accept shrinkage |

**Acclimatisation** (rule of thumb)
- Bring the wood into the room where it will be used. Stack it flat on spacer sticks ("stickers") so air reaches all faces.
- Wait **1–2 weeks** for furniture stock, longer for thick pieces or wet wood. Check with a moisture meter rather than counting days.
- Resistance meters with hammer electrodes are accurate to about **±2 % MC** and better for batch averages than for single pieces [S10].
- Measure about 300 mm from the end [S10].
- Right after kiln drying to 16 %, the surface can be below 10 % while the core is still above 20 %. A rest period lets this gradient equalise [S10].

## 2. Sawn vs planed vs glulam vs KVH

### 2.1 Terms the customer will meet

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Rough sawn** (sägerau, žagan) | The surface straight from the saw. It is not flat, square or smooth to furniture tolerance. Fine for frames, sheds and garden structures. For furniture you must surface-plane one face, joint one edge, then thickness-plane, or buy planed stock. |
| **Planed / dressed** (gehobelt, skobljan) | Four sides planed, flat and square, smooth enough to finish after light sanding. |
| **Centre cut** | Pieces from the middle of the log that contain the pith. More likely to check. |
| **Side cut** | Pieces from outside the pith. Better quality and fewer checks. |
| **Pith-cut** (herzgetrennt) | The pith was cut through, so the piece does not contain a whole boxed heart [S14]. |
| **Boxed-heart-free** (herzfrei, brez stržena) | No pith at all. A separate heart plank of at least 40 mm is cut out [S14]. Least checking. |
| **Radial / quarter-sawn** | Rings near-vertical to the face. Stable (see 1.5). |
| **KD** | Kiln dried. Technically dried timber in a controlled kiln, at ≥ 55 °C for at least 48 h, to ≤ 20 % MC [S14]. |
| **Sawn vs planed size** | Planing removes material. **Rule of thumb:** expect about 3–5 mm less per dimension. For example, 50×100 sawn becomes about 45×95 planed, and 24 mm sawn boards become about 19–21 mm planed. Ask Solis for its actual planed sizes. |

### 2.3 Strength grading: S10 and C24 explained

- **DIN 4074-1** grades softwood visually into S7, S10 and S13 by knots, ring width, slope of grain, cracks, wane and so on. **EN 338** defines strength classes C16, C24 and C30. **EN 1912** maps one to the other [S5, S16].
- For spruce/fir: **S7 ≈ C16, S10 ≈ C24, S13 ≈ C30** [S16].
- For spruce, S13 allows ring widths up to 4 mm and S10 up to 6 mm [S5].
- In Austria, timber in load-bearing structures must be graded to ÖNORM DIN 4074-1 by law [S16].
- "TS" means graded dry, at ≤ 20 % MC, which is required for building use in Germany [S14].

| Strength class (EN 338) [S16] | C16 (S7) | C24 (S10) | C30 (S13) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Characteristic bending strength | 16 N/mm² | 24 N/mm² | 30 N/mm² |
| Mean modulus of elasticity | 8,000 N/mm² | 11,000 N/mm² | 12,000 N/mm² |
| Characteristic density | 310 kg/m³ | 350 kg/m³ | 380 kg/m³ |

**Beginner meaning**
- For furniture, grading barely matters; appearance (knots, straightness) matters more.
- For anything that carries people, snow or a roof (loft ladder, pergola with a roof, carport, shed rafters), use **at least S10/C24** and have the sizes checked.

### 2.5 When should a beginner choose which?

1. **Indoor furniture and shelves:** planed spruce/fir at 8–10 % MC. Use radial stock for tabletops, doors and anything that must stay flat. Choose rounded edges for kids' items.
2. **Garden furniture:** planed spruce/fir with a good coating, or larch (order larch specially; Solis buys larch logs). Choose rounded edges.
3. **Raised beds, compost bins, firewood stores:** dry sawn (rough is fine) or fresh sawn for the cheapest option. Larch lasts longer.
4. **Pergola or carport posts and beams:** glulam GL24h or KVH. These are straighter and crack less than solid squared timber.
   - Spans and sections must come from a structural calculation for the local snow and wind load.
   - Keep the glulam under a roof. It is made for service classes 1–2.
   - In service class 3, durable species such as larch or Douglas heartwood are recommended [S17].
5. **"I don't own a planer":** buy planed. A rough-sawn board needs a jointer and a thicknesser (or a lot of hand-planing) to become furniture stock.

**Arithmetic: how heavy is a long glulam beam?** 100×200 mm × 13.5 m = 0.27 m³. At about 470 kg/m³ that is about **127 kg**. Plan for two or three people or a lifting aid.

## 3. DIY projects (33 ideas)

### 3.0 Conventions and how to calculate quantities

**Difficulty**
- 1 = beginner: straight cuts and screws
- 2 = intermediate: joinery, glue-ups, angles, small structures
- 3 = advanced: structural or large builds, possibly needing a permit or an engineer

**Method used throughout**
1. Make a cut list: how many parts, of which section and length.
2. Group parts by section.
3. **Nest the parts into stock lengths** (2.0 / 2.5 / 3.0 / 4.0 m). This matters more than total metres, because offcuts are wasted.
4. Add **10–15 % waste** (rule of thumb: defects, saw kerf about 3 mm per cut, mistakes).
5. Optionally convert to m³ for pricing: m³ = n × t × w × L (all in m).
6. Mass ≈ m³ × density. Spruce is about 460 kg/m³ at 12–15 % MC and larch about 600 [S5, S7].

**Useful shortcuts**
- **Metres of board per m² of surface** = 1 / (board width + gap), in m.
  - Example: 24×95 boards with no gap need 1 / 0.095 = 10.5 m/m².
  - Slats 45 mm wide with 20 mm gaps need 1 / 0.065 = 15.4 m/m².
- **Number of slats** = surface width / (slat width + gap), rounded up.
- Imperial plans (US YouTube and Pinterest):
  - 2x4 = 38×89 mm. The nearest EU size is 45×95 or 45×70.
  - 1x6 = 19×140 mm. The nearest is 20/21×145 planed.
  - 8 ft = 2.44 m.
  - Re-check any dimension that depends on board thickness.

**Generic tool kits**
- **Basic:** tape measure, pencil, square, hand saw or circular saw, cordless drill/driver, clamps, sandpaper or orbital sander, safety glasses, hearing protection, dust mask.
- **Intermediate:** add a mitre saw, jigsaw, pocket-hole jig or dowel jig, router, more clamps.
- **Advanced:** add a table saw, planer/thicknesser, levels and string lines, post anchors, ladder or scaffold.

**Assumed sizes for the large builds.** For pergola, carport, shed, greenhouse, stairs and decking, the sections given are **typical kit-style sizes for budgeting only**. Real sizes must come from a structural calculation for the site's snow zone (Slovenia ranges from lowland to alpine snow loads) and from the applicable building rules (see Q&A 17).

### 3.1 Garden and outdoor

**1. Raised garden bed, 200 × 100 × ~60 cm**
- **Difficulty:** 1. **Outdoor.**
- **Product:** dry sawn or planed boards 28×145. Larch for a longer life; spruce is a consumable in soil contact (under 5 years for stakes in soil [S9]; untreated pine/fir beds about 3–5 years, a hobby-source estimate [S31]).
- **Corner and middle posts:** 45×70 (or 45×45 at the corners).

Arithmetic:
- Rows: 4 × 145 mm = 580 mm, about 60 cm.
- Per row: 2 long sides of 2.00 m, and 2 short sides of 1.00 − 2 × 0.028 = 0.944 m (they fit between the long sides).
- Long sides: 8 pieces × 2.0 m = **8 boards**.
- Short sides: 8 pieces × 0.944 m, at 2 per 2.0 m board = **4 boards**.
- Boards: **12 × 28×145×2000**, which is 12 × 0.028 × 0.145 × 2.0 = **0.097 m³**.
- Posts: 4 corners and 2 mid-posts on the long sides (stops the 2 m sides bowing outwards) = 6 × 0.60 m = 3.6 m of 45×70, so **2 × 45×70×2000**.
- Screws: (16 board pieces × 2 ends + 8 mid-post crossings) × 2 screws = about **80 exterior screws** (5×60 mm, stainless for larch).
- Soil to fill it: 1.94 × 0.94 × 0.58 ≈ **1.06 m³**.

- **Tools:** basic kit.
- **Finish:** none inside (soil contact). Optionally line the inside with a dimpled membrane, open at the top so the boards can dry; this is a rule of thumb. Raw linseed oil on the inside is suggested by hobby sources [S31]. The RHS notes lining helps keep treated-wood chemicals out of the soil [S31].
- **Beginner mistakes:**
  - boards thinner than about 25 mm, which bow
  - no mid brace on 2 m sides
  - posts dug into the soil
  - using wood with a "bio-protective dip" for vegetables (ask what it is)
  - buying too little soil

- **Difficulty:** 2 (precision). **Outdoor.**
- **Product:** knot-free radial spruce/fir laths, planed to the exact profile in your supplier's drawing. Bee space tolerances are tight, so stable radial stock helps.
- The AŽ (Alberti-Žnideršič) standard frame is **26 × 41 cm** outside, as reported from Žnideršič's text "Naš panj" (verify against the current Čebelarska zveza Slovenije drawing) [S38].

Arithmetic:
- One frame's perimeter = 2 × (0.41 + 0.26) = **1.34 m of lath**.
- 10 frames = 13.4 m. A 20-frame AŽ hive = 26.8 m, plus 10 % ≈ **30 m of lath per hive**.

- **Finish:** none on frames or the inside of hives. The outside of a hive can be painted (Slovenian painted hive fronts, panjske končnice, are a tradition).
- **Mistakes:** dimensions off by 1–2 mm; resin pockets; fresh (wet) wood that shrinks out of square.

**30. Nest box / birdhouse**
- **Difficulty:** 1. **Outdoor.**
- **Product:** planed spruce/fir board **at least 15 mm thick** (BTO); one 20×145 board, about 1.2 m.
- Entrance holes [S34]:
  - 25 mm for blue, coal and marsh tits
  - 28 mm for great tit and tree sparrow
  - 32 mm for house sparrow
  - 45 mm for starling (larger box)
- **No perch.** Hole at least 12 cm above the floor to protect chicks from cats [S34].
- **Finish:** outside only, water-based; none inside.
- **Mistakes:** a perch; a hole too low; no drainage or ventilation; no way to open it for cleaning.

**31. Tool / garden shed, 2 × 2 m**
- **Difficulty:** 3. **Outdoor.**
- **Product:** KD planed or KVH 45×95 studs; 45×145 joists and rafters; 28×145 floor; 24×145 cladding with 24×48 battens (board-and-batten). Typical layout (rule of thumb):
  - Floor joists 45×145: 5 × 2.0 m (spacing ≤ 60 cm), plus a rim of 2 × 2.0 m = 14 m.
  - Floor boards 28×145: 2.0 / 0.145 ≈ 14 × 2.0 m = 28 m.
  - Wall studs 45×95 at 60 cm, with door framing: about 18 × 1.9 m, plus bottom and top plates 2 × 8 m; about 50 m.
  - Cladding: wall area 8 × 1.9 = 15.2 m², minus a 1.6 m² door = 13.6 m². Boards: × 6.9 m/m² (1 / 0.145) = 94 m of 24×145. Battens over the joints: about 82 m of 24×48.
  - Roof: 2.4 × 2.4 = 5.8 m² of 24×145 boards = 40 m; rafters 5 × 2.5 m = 12.5 m of 45×145.

Arithmetic (volume):

| Section | Metres | Volume |
|---|---|---|
| 45×145 | 26.5 m | 0.173 m³ |
| 28×145 | 28 m | 0.114 m³ |
| 45×95 | 50 m | 0.214 m³ |
| 24×145 | 134 m | 0.466 m³ |
| 24×48 | 82 m | 0.094 m³ |
| **Total** | | **about 1.06 m³** (about 490 kg in spruce) |

- **Tools:** advanced kit.
- **Finish:** paint or pigmented glaze on the cladding; ventilated roof.
- **Mistakes:**
  - floor framing on the ground
  - cladding reaching the splash zone (keep 30 cm clear [S19])
  - no permit check (Q&A 17)
  - forgetting roof overhangs

**32. Small greenhouse, 2 × 3 m, polycarbonate**
- **Difficulty:** 3. **Outdoor (very humid inside).**
- **Product:** 45×70 frame, preferably larch, or painted spruce; sill 70×70 or 45×145 larch on a plinth.

Arithmetic (rule-of-thumb layout):
- Sill 10 m.
- Posts: corners 4, plus about 1 m spacing on the long walls (2 + 2) and short walls (1 + 1) = 10 posts × 1.8 m = 18 m.
- Top plates 10 m and mid rails 10 m.
- Gable rafters: 4 pairs × 2 × 1.25 m = 10 m, plus a ridge of 3.0 m.
- Total about **61 m of 45×70**, plus a 10 m sill.

- **Finish:** paint, or none for larch heartwood.
- **Mistakes:** frame spacing that does not match the polycarbonate sheet width; no roof vent; a spruce sill in contact with soil.
- In Slovenia, a greenhouse without foundations and without utility connections is reported as a "simple object" under the 2022 classification (verify current rules) [S30].

**33. Steep stair / loft ladder, floor-to-floor 2.6 m**
- **Difficulty:** 3. **Indoor.**
- **Product:** stringers of **C24/S10 planed 45×195 or 45×245, knot-free edges**; treads 45×145. Radial stock is good for the treads.

Arithmetic:
- Steepness 60°: stringer length = 2.6 / sin 60° = 2.6 / 0.866 = **3.0 m**, plus about 0.1 m, so 2 × 3.1 m.
- Rises: 2.6 / 0.22 ≈ 12 rises, so **11 treads** (the top floor is the 12th step).
- Treads: 11 × 0.63 m (60 cm wide plus 15 mm housing each side) = 6.9 m, so **2 × 45×145×4000**.

- **Tools:** advanced kit, router with a template for housings.
- **Finish:** hard-wax oil with a slip-resistant additive.
- **Mistakes:**
  - uneven rises (dangerous)
  - no handrail
  - knots at the stringer edges
  - not checking national building rules on stair geometry

### 3.4 Small crafts and "not ideal" uses

**34 (bonus). Cutting / serving board: softwood is not ideal.**
- Spruce is about 1,680 N Janka versus about 6,450 N for hard maple, so knives scar it quickly. Spruce, pine and larch contain resin.
- If a customer insists, suggest a **serving or bread board from knot-free fir**. Fir is resin-free and odour- and taste-neutral; it is traditionally used as a baking board for fish dishes [S6].
- Finish with food-safe mineral oil. Do not use it for heavy chopping or raw meat.

**35 (bonus). Pallet-style lounge from fresh sawn timber (2 m sofa)**
- **Difficulty:** 1. **Outdoor or covered terrace.**
- **Product:** fresh sawn 24×100 decking and 45×95 runners. HT-treated (heat only) is fine. Avoid bio-dipped wood for seating.

Arithmetic:
- One "pallet" unit of 1.0 × 0.8 m: 3 runners × 1.0 m = 3 m of 45×95, plus 7 deck boards × 0.8 m = 5.6 m of 24×100.
- Seat: 2 units side by side × 2 layers = 4 units, plus 1 backrest = 5 units. That is **15 m of 45×95 and 28 m of 24×100**.

- **Key point:** fresh wood is wet. Let it air-dry stacked on stickers under cover for several weeks to months before building (rule of thumb; measure until under 20 %). Otherwise expect shrinkage, loosening screws, checks and possibly mould under cushions.
- **Finish:** sand well; exterior oil once dry.

## 4. By-products for hobbyists and the home

### 4.1 Pellets (A1, spruce/fir, 15 kg bags)

**What A1 means** (EN ISO 17225-2 / ENplus, via certification summaries) [S23]:
- Made from chemically untreated wood: virgin wood or untreated residues.
- Moisture ≤ 10 %.
- Ash ≤ 0.7 %.
- Additives ≤ 2 % (for example starch).
- Net calorific value ≥ 16.5 MJ/kg.
- Diameter 6 ± 1 or 8 ± 1 mm.
- Bulk density 600–750 kg/m³.
- Mechanical durability ≥ 98 % (ENplus; one summary of the ISO standard says ≥ 97.5 %).

**Arithmetic: energy in one bag**
- 16.5 MJ/kg ÷ 3.6 = 4.58 kWh/kg.
- × 15 kg = **at least about 69 kWh per bag**.
- Rule of thumb: 1 litre of heating oil ≈ 10 kWh, so one bag ≈ 7 litres of heating oil.

**Uses**

| Use | Advice |
|---|---|
| **Heating** (pellet stove or boiler) | The intended use. Store dry; damp pellets swell and crumble. |
| **Cat litter / small-animal litter** | Wood pellets are widely used as clumping-free litter: they swell and turn to sawdust when wet. Pet-product sellers warn that some heating pellets may contain binders, accelerants or fragrance, and that pellets made for pets are kiln-dried and dust-extracted [S37]. Advise customers to confirm with Solis whether their A1 pellets contain any additive, and to choose unscented pellets. |
| **Horse and small-animal bedding** | Same logic as litter. Pellets need water to "fluff" into bedding (practice tip). |
| **Smoker / pellet grill** | **Not recommended.** Cooking-pellet makers advise against heating pellets: they are often spruce or pine and may contain binders, and resinous softwood smoke tastes bitter [S26]. Use 100 % hardwood pellets sold for cooking. |
| **Fire lighting / pellet baskets in log stoves** | Works in purpose-made pellet baskets. Do not overfill a log stove with pellets (practice tip). |

### 4.2 Sawdust (fresh or dry)

**Animal bedding**
- Dry sawdust is absorbent and cheap.
- Fine dust can irritate airways, so use coarser shavings or pellets for chickens and horses (practice guidance; rule of thumb).
- Only untreated, unpainted wood.

**Compost**
- Sawdust is a strong carbon ("brown") material: C:N about **100–500 : 1** [S24], with other Cornell figures up to 300–700 : 1.
- The compost target is about **30 : 1** [S24].
- Mix thin layers with nitrogen-rich greens (grass, kitchen scraps, manure).
- Sawdust is too fine on its own to keep a pile aerated; chips or bark give structure [S24].

**Mulch on paths and around shrubs:** fine, but do not dig fresh sawdust into vegetable beds. Woody material dug into soil ties up nitrogen for months to years; as a surface mulch this is seldom a problem [S25].

**Mushroom growing**
- Oyster mushrooms grow on sawdust substrates.
- A Romanian study found substrates partly containing softwood sawdust gave yields comparable to beech sawdust [S36].
- Hardwood sawdust remains the usual recommendation. Spruce works best mixed with straw or hardwood and well pasteurised (cautious summary of mixed evidence).

**Other uses:** absorbent for oil spills in the workshop; filler for home-made wood filler when mixed with glue. The filler should be made from the same species, so the colour matches.

### 4.3 Wood chips G30 (without bark)

- **G30** is the size class of the withdrawn Austrian standard ÖNORM M 7133 (1998). It is still widely quoted and has a maximum particle length of about 85 mm [S28].
- It is roughly comparable to the EN ISO 17225-4 "P31" classes, but there is no exact one-to-one equivalence [S28].
- UK smoke-control lists accept chips under 35 % moisture meeting ÖNORM M 7133 G30/G50 [S28].

| Use | Advice |
|---|---|
| Chip boiler fuel | Use the moisture class the boiler maker specifies. |
| Garden paths, play-area surfacing, mulch under shrubs | Debarked spruce chips are bright and clean, and break down within a few years. |
| Compost structure | Coarse chips aerate piles of grass and kitchen waste [S24]. |

### 4.4 Ground bark (mulch, fuel, fertiliser)

**Mulch for shrubs, trees, berries and paths**
- Bark C:N is about 100–130 : 1 [S24].
- Nitrogen tie-up is mainly a problem when bark is **dug in**; as a surface layer it is seldom an issue [S25].
- Sources disagree on whether bark mulch lowers soil pH. Some report bark pH of 2–5; a Wisconsin study found no soil pH difference [S25]. Advice: test soil pH for pH-sensitive plants, and add some nitrogen when mulching young plantings.
- Rule of thumb: a layer of about 5–8 cm suppresses weeds. Keep it a few cm away from tree trunks and house walls.

**Other uses:** fuel in suitable biomass boilers; soil-improver component in composts.

### 4.5 Offcuts 7–40 cm (firewood, kindling, crafts)

**Firewood**
- Burn only below **20 % moisture** (Slovenian small-combustion regulation) [S29].
- Spruce needs about **one year** of covered, airy stacking, preferably in the sun [S29].
- Splitting and cutting to stove length speeds drying [S29].
- Kiln-dried offcuts can be burned straight away.
- Softwood burns fast and hot. It is excellent for kindling and lighting, and less ideal as the only overnight fuel (practice).
- Spruce tends to spark: use a closed stove or a fire screen (practice).

**Never burn:**
- treated, painted or glued wood in home stoves; treated wood belongs in licensed incinerators only [S19]
- pieces with a bio-protective (anti-blue-stain) dip (treat as chemically treated)

HT-marked wood (ISPM-15) is only heat-treated: core at least **56 °C for 30 minutes** [S27]. It contains no chemical.

**Crafts and kids**
- building blocks (sand and round all edges)
- coasters (end grain from 95×95 slices)
- trivets
- bird feeders
- toy cars and boats
- candle holders (with a metal cup insert)
- plant labels
- wooden spoons are better from hardwood (rule of thumb)

### 4.6 Fresh sawn timber with bio-protective dip

- The dip on fresh sawn timber is normally an anti-sapstain (blue-stain) treatment.
- The bot should advise asking Solis for the product data sheet.
- Until then, treat dipped wood like chemically treated wood:
  - not for raised beds with vegetables
  - not for kids' furniture
  - not for indoor use
  - not burned in home stoves
- The Energieinstitut Vorarlberg advises no wood preservatives at all in living spaces [S19].

## 5. Finishing, outdoor protection and safety

### 5.1 Indoor finishes (spruce, fir, pine)

| Finish | Pros / cons | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Hard-wax oil / oil** | Natural look, easy to repair, slight yellowing | Two thin coats; wipe off the excess. Mind oily rags (5.5). |
| **Water-based lacquer** | Clear, low yellowing, durable on tabletops | Raises the grain: sand lightly after the first coat. |
| **Paint** | Hides knots and colour differences | **Seal knots first with shellac or a special knot sealer.** Oil-alkyd and latex primers do not block knot extractives [S11]. For a white finish, use knot-free wood [S11]. |
| **Stain** | Colour | Test on an offcut. Spruce and pine can blotch; use a pre-stain conditioner or gel stain [S1]. Larch stains poorly [S7]. Remove resin before staining pine and larch [S7, S8]. |
| **Nothing** | Fine for shelves and frames | Spruce and fir darken and yellow with light [S6]. |

**Resin bleed**
- Pitch oozes when the wood gets warm, for example in sun, near radiators or in saunas, and it can come through paint [S11].
- Kiln schedules can "set" pitch, but construction grades seldom have set pitch [S11].
- Hardened pitch can be scraped off; soft pitch can be removed with turpentine or mineral spirits [S11].
- **Shellac seals extractives but not pitch** [S11].
- Choose fir or knot-free radial stock for warm places.

### 5.2 Outdoor: constructive wood protection first

Austrian, German and Swedish sources agree: **detailing beats chemicals** [S17, S19].

**Rules from the Energieinstitut Vorarlberg [S19]**
- Choose a suitable species.
- Use heartwood outdoors (no sapwood).
- Rough-sawn beats planed outdoors.
- Keep a splash zone of **at least 30 cm** at the base.
- Use large roof overhangs.
- **Do not butt wood against wood:** capillary joints suck in water.
- Build so wood can dry out (vapour-open).
- **Cover end-grain faces and form drip edges.**
- Inspect regularly.
- Make heavily weathered parts easy to replace.

**More rules**
- Keep wood below about 20 % MC; this is the key protection against decay [S12, S20].
- Use kiln-dried wood [S20].
- Rules of thumb:
  - Slope horizontal top faces (for example post tops cut at an angle or capped).
  - Use spacers (8–10 mm) where wood meets wood or wall.
  - Use post anchors so posts stand 2–3 cm or more clear of concrete or paving.
  - Never set posts directly in soil or concrete.
  - Leave ventilation gaps behind cladding.

**Fasteners**
- Use stainless steel outdoors, especially with larch: iron and tannins give blue-black stains [S7, S11].
- If you use galvanized fasteners, use hot-dip galvanized only [S11].
- Spruce and pine show only faint grey stains with iron [S5, S8].

### 5.3 Outdoor coatings: what lasts how long

US Wood Handbook estimates (Table 16-4) for pine, fir and spruce siding, average US climate [S11]. Treat these as **relative** guidance; local exposure and climate change the numbers.

| Surface | Tinted / clear penetrating finish | Semitransparent stain | Paint | Solid-colour stain |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Smooth (planed, flat grain) | 1–2 years | 2–3 years | 6–10 years | 6–8 years |
| Saw-textured (rough) | 2–3 years | 4–7 years | 8–12 years | 8–10 years |

**Notes from the same source [S11]**
- Pigment is what protects against UV. **More pigment means a longer life**, so clear finishes last shortest.
- The paint lives assume one primer and two top coats. A two-coat system lasts about half as long.
- **Finish soon after installation.** In one test, paint on boards left to weather for 16 weeks before painting peeled within 3 years; on boards weathered only 1 week it peeled after 13 years; boards not pre-weathered had no peeling after 20 years. Low-density species are most sensitive to this. Prime before or right after installation.
- **Paint wood at about its in-service moisture content.** Painting above 20 % MC raises the risk of blistering and peeling.
- **Seal end grain.** Unsealed end grain raises the moisture of painted wood.
- Edge-grain (radial) surfaces hold paint better than flat-grain surfaces.

**Species-specific**
- **Spruce outdoors:** opaque paints, including on rough-sawn surfaces, give good durability [S5]. Uncoated spruce turns grey or black from UV and mould [S5].
- **Larch outdoors:** heartwood can be left untreated and will turn grey (often deliberately). Under pigmented glazes or paints, tannins can discolour; use a stain-blocking primer (practice guidance).
- **Greying** is UV degradation of lignin plus surface mould. It is cosmetic, but erosion continues slowly [S5, S11].

**What counts as wood protection**
- Glazes, normal paints, oils and waxes are **not wood preservatives**. They are water-repellent and weather-protective coatings [S19].
- Wood preservatives (biocides) are mostly unnecessary with good detailing. If unavoidable, use certified products and specialists [S19].

### 5.4 Glulam and KVH outdoors

- Glulam and KVH are made for service classes 1 and 2: indoors, or covered and sheltered [S14, S17].
- In service class 3 (exposed), the glulam information sheet says to use durable species instead: larch or Douglas heartwood up to use class 3.1 [S17].
- **Beginner rule:** glulam outside is fine **under a roof**, with protected beam ends and posts standing above the ground.
- Apply a temporary weather coat during construction [S17].

### 5.5 Safety basics

**Wood dust**
- UK workplace exposure limits: **5 mg/m³ for softwood dust** and **3 mg/m³ for hardwood dust**; mixtures are treated as hardwood [S21].
- The EU binding limit for hardwood dust has been **2 mg/m³** since January 2023 [S39].
- Health concerns include dermatitis and respiratory problems [S21]. Cleaning up also raises a lot of dust [S21].
- **Practice:** use dust extraction or a shop vacuum on sanders and saws; wear an FFP2/FFP3 mask when sanding; vacuum instead of sweeping.

**Oily rags (linseed oil, hard-wax oil) can self-ignite**
- Soak them in water in a closed metal container, or dry them flat or hung outdoors, then dispose of them [S22].
- Never leave them crumpled in a bin or on a pile [S22].

**Machines (practice)**
- Eye and hearing protection.
- Riving knife and blade guard on table saws; push sticks; never stand behind the blade line (kickback).
- Clamp the workpiece, not your hand.
- Keep blades sharp (dull blades plus resin cause burning and kickback).
- Unplug or remove the battery before changing blades.
- Feed routers against the bit rotation.

**Lifting:** long beams are heavy. A 100×200 glulam beam 13.5 m long weighs about 127 kg (see 2.5).

**Structures:** anything people stand on or under (stairs, lofts, roofs, bunk beds) needs a proper design. Snow load on a carport roof is not a DIY guess.

## 6. Typical beginner questions (short expert answers)

**1. Why did my board warp, cup or twist?**
- It changed moisture unevenly or after you cut it.
- Flat-sawn boards cup because tangential shrinkage is about 2× radial (spruce 0.39 vs 0.19 % per % MC) [S5].
- Other causes:
  - wood not dried to its service moisture
  - one face finished and the other not
  - stored leaning or in the sun
  - compression wood or spiral grain [S5]
- Fixes:
  - buy KD at the right MC (1.6)
  - acclimatise the wood stacked on stickers
  - use radial boards for wide flat parts
  - finish all faces equally
  - alternate growth-ring orientation in glue-ups (rule of thumb)

**2. Can I use spruce for a tabletop?**
- Yes, it is common and cheap, but it is **soft** (Janka 1,680 N vs about 6,450 N for maple) and will dent.
- Use a hard finish (water-based lacquer or hard-wax oil). Use radial or edge-glued boards at 8–10 % MC.
- Fix the top so it can move: an 800 mm wide top can move about 9–12 mm seasonally (1.5).
- For a harder softwood, choose larch (3,290 N) or pine (2,420 N).

**3. How do I calculate how much wood I need?**
1. Draw the piece.
2. List every part (count × section × length).
3. Group the parts by section.
4. Nest the lengths into the stock lengths you can buy (2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0 m).
5. Add 10–15 % waste.

For cladding or decking, m per m² = 1 / (board width + gap) in m. See the worked examples in section 3.

**4. Do I need a planer?**
- Only if you buy rough-sawn timber and want furniture-quality flat, square, smooth parts.
- For outdoor structures, rough-sawn is fine and holds stains better [S11, S19].
- Otherwise order **planed** timber, cut to length if possible.

**5. How long should wood acclimatise?**
- Rule of thumb: 1–2 weeks in the room where it will be used, stacked with air gaps; longer for thick or wetter wood.
- Better: measure with a moisture meter until it reads about the expected service MC:
  - about 8–10 % in heated rooms
  - about 12–16 % for covered outdoor use [S10, S11, S13]

**6. Can glulam go outside?**
- Yes, under a roof: it is designed for service classes 1–2 [S14, S17].
- Fully exposed spruce glulam is not recommended. For exposed use, choose durable species (larch or Douglas heartwood) [S17] or protect the beams constructively (cover boards, end caps) and coat them.

**7. Spruce or larch for my raised bed?**
- Larch heartwood lasts longer.
- Spruce in soil contact is a consumable: heartwood stakes in soil last under 5 years [S9], and hobby sources quote 3–5 years for untreated pine or fir beds [S31].
- A dimpled liner on the inside helps.
- Thicker boards (28 mm or more) last longer and bow less.

**8. Which moisture content should I order?**

| Use | MC |
|---|---|
| Indoor furniture | 8–10 % |
| Interior cladding and structure | 10–12 % |
| Covered outdoor and garden furniture | 12–16 % |
| Rough outdoor | 16–20 % |

See 1.6 for the sources behind these values.

**9. What's the difference between S10 and C24?**
- S10 is the visual grade under DIN 4074-1. C24 is the European strength class it maps to (for spruce/fir) via EN 1912 [S16].
- C24 means a characteristic bending strength of 24 N/mm² [S16].
- You need it for load-bearing parts. For furniture it is irrelevant.

**10. Why is my "45×95" not 50×100?**
- Planed sizes are net after planing.
- Sawn nominal sizes refer to a moisture of about 20 % [S43], and the wood shrinks further as it dries (1.5).

**11. Can I build furniture from fresh sawn wood?**
- Not indoor furniture: it will shrink by several percent across the grain and crack.
- Air-dry it first (stickered, under cover; months), or use it only for rough outdoor projects such as a compost bin or pallet lounge.

**12. Treated or untreated wood for vegetable beds?**
- Untreated larch or untreated spruce (as a consumable) is the low-chemical option.
- If wood is pressure-treated or dipped, line the soil side [S31] and ask for the product data sheet.

**13. What finish for outdoor furniture?**
- Pigmented exterior oil or glaze, renewed every 1–3 years (rule of thumb, consistent with [S11]).
- Or paint for the longest life (6–12 years on softwood in US data) [S11].
- Seal all end grain. Clear finishes last shortest [S11].
- Larch can be left untreated and allowed to grey [S7].

**14. My white paint turned yellow or brown over the knots. Why?**
- Knot extractives migrate through oil and latex primers [S11].
- Seal the knots with shellac or a special knot sealer, then prime and apply two top coats [S11].
- Or use knot-free wood for white finishes.

**15. Resin is oozing from my spruce or pine. What now?**
- Heat drives it out [S11].
- Let it harden and scrape it off; remove soft resin with turpentine or mineral spirits; then sand, spot-prime and recoat [S11].
- It can recur in summer.
- For hot places (sauna, south-facing, near radiators) choose fir, which has no resin canals [S6], or knot-free radial stock.

**16. Which screws outdoors?**
- Stainless steel (A2, or A4 near pools and the coast).
- Mandatory for larch, to avoid blue-black iron-tannin stains [S7].
- Pre-drill larch near ends and for large screws [S7].
- If galvanized, use hot-dip galvanized only [S11].

**17. Do I need a permit for a carport, shed or greenhouse (Slovenia)?**
- The 2022 Decree on the classification of buildings (Uredba o razvrščanju objektov) introduced "simple objects" (enostavni objekti) that are built without a building permit but must still comply with spatial plans [S30].
- Reported conditions:
  - Simple objects are single-storey, up to about 4 m high.
  - Greenhouses without foundations or utility connections are simple objects.
  - Canopies (nadstrešnica): about 20 m² is cited as the simple limit, with larger sizes non-demanding and needing a permit. Sources differ.
- **Always check with the municipality or the upravna enota before building** [S30].

**18. Is radial (quarter-sawn) wood worth the extra cost?**
- For wide or visible flat parts (tabletops, doors, frames, cladding, sauna benches, beehive frames): yes. It moves about half as much across its width as flat-sawn wood and hardly cups [S5, S11].
- For frames and garden structures: no.

**19. Can I burn offcuts, or use pellets in my smoker?**
- Offcuts: yes, if untreated and under 20 % MC [S29]. Never burn painted, glued, treated or dipped wood [S19].
- Heating pellets in a smoker: no. Use food-grade hardwood pellets; softwood resin gives a bitter taste [S26].

**20. Can I glue spruce and larch?**
- Yes.
- Spruce glues easily with PVA and thermoset glues [S5]. Larch glues without general problems [S7].
- Very resinous pine may glue poorly [S8].
- Glue fresh-planed surfaces, at matching moisture, within a day of planing (rule of thumb).

**21. How do I stop end grain splitting?**
- Pre-drill near ends, especially in larch [S7].
- Keep screws at least about 5× their diameter from the end (rule of thumb).
- Seal end grain outdoors (paint or end-grain sealer) and protect it from standing water [S11, S19].
- Choose boxed-heart-free (herzfrei) pieces for posts to reduce checking [S14].

**22. My pine has blue or grey streaks. Is it rotten?**
- No. It is blue stain: a fungus that does not reduce strength and is allowed without limit in structural grades [S8].
- It shows the wood was wet once. Dry it and finish with a pigmented coating if the look bothers you.

**23. Are knots a strength problem?**
- For furniture, rarely. Loose or black knots may fall out; fill them or glue them in.
- For beams and stair stringers, large knots at the edges significantly lower strength. That is why grading (S10/C24) exists.
- Knot-free radial stock is the premium option for stairs and sauna benches.
